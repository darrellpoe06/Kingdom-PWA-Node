## 1. Dr. Frederick K.C. Price — "Race, Religion & Racism" (1997)

*id: `pv-price` · 1590 words*

**The big idea.** Dr. Frederick K.C. Price (1932-2021), founder of Crenshaw Christian Center and its FaithDome in Los Angeles, says that in the late 1980s Yahweh gave him an assignment to teach on race, religion and racism in the Church. In October 1997 he began it on his Ever Increasing Faith broadcast, and it became 76 televised parts and three books. His case, in summary: racism is sin against a God who made all nations of one blood; men twisted the Word to justify it; and slavery could not have stood in America without the consent of the Church. His call: expose it, repent of it, and read the Bible right.

Dr. Frederick K.C. Price was born on January 3, 1932, and went home on February 12, 2021. For most of a generation his was one of the best-known Bible-teaching voices in America. He had served as an assistant pastor in a Baptist church and then as an AME pastor in the 1950s; he received the baptism with the Holy Spirit in 1970 and came into the Word of Faith teaching; and in November 1973 he and about three hundred members founded Crenshaw Christian Center in Los Angeles. The congregation outgrew one sanctuary after another, bought the old Pepperdine University campus, and on January 21, 1990, dedicated the FaithDome, a domed sanctuary of 10,146 seats, paid for in full. His Ever Increasing Faith broadcast went out weekly on television and radio to an audience of every race. By any worldly measure he had arrived. And in October 1997 he spent that standing on the one subject most of his peers would not touch. In his own words from the Preface to the first book: "In October 1997, I began to deliver the message as a series on my Ever Increasing Faith television program." The series is kept in Ever Increasing Faith’s own archive as seventy-six broadcasts of nearly an hour each, and it became three volumes published by his Faith One Publishing in 1999: A Bold Encounter With Division in the Church; Perverting the Gospel to Subjugate a People; and Jesus, Christianity & Islam. He describes the work as "the culmination of five year’s work, forty-five years as a minister, and a lifetime as a black man." To understand why he spoke, start where he starts, with the chapter he titled My Assignment From God. He opens with his childhood: "When I came into the world in Santa Monica, California, in the early 1930s, it was primarily a white city." Black families were crowded into a small area. At the beach, he writes, "we had to go to a certain section about a block wide that had been set aside for black people." He was a curious boy and asked questions, including this one: "I couldn’t understand why there were black churches and white churches and Hispanic churches in different parts of the city, each church serving only one ethnic group." The answer he got is the answer a whole nation gave: "No one seemed to have an answer. They said, “Well, that’s the way it’s always been.”" He was not raised in a Christian home. At twenty-one he met Betty Ruth Scott, and shortly after they married he gave his life to Jesus Christ at a tent revival. Here the story turns. In the counseling area, with only two or three Black people in the room, he asked his counselor the question he had carried since boyhood: "I don’t understand how this Jesus whom I’ve just received is the way, the truth and the life, and how He’s the answer to all of humanity’s problems, and yet everything about Him is racially segregated." The counselor, he writes, took the Bible and showed him from the Scriptures, interpreting them in his own way, that it was God’s will for the races to be separated. Price is honest about why he believed it: "Number one, he was white; number two, he was a minister of the Gospel; number three, he had a radio broadcast; and number four, he was a teacher in a Bible institute." And then: "So I accepted what he said. I did not like it, but I accepted it because it seemed to make sense, and history certainly seemed to back him up." Looking back, he names what that night was: "I did not know it then, but this was my introduction to the way the Bible has been twisted and distorted by one group to the detriment of another." He also asks how many generations of Black people have believed they were cursed and inferior because of the so-called curse of Ham, which he calls a corrupted teaching. That is the first thing to understand about this voice. The lie came to him at the altar, wearing a minister’s authority, on the night of his salvation. What freed him was not a movement or a theory but the Word itself, studied over years. Jesus said, "If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed." (John 8:36) Price came to see that Paul told the philosophers of Athens that Yahweh "hath made of one blood all nations of men for to dwell on all the face of the earth" (Acts 17:26), and that Peter, sent to the house of a Roman soldier, confessed, "Of a truth I perceive that God is no respecter of persons" (Acts 10:34). He then saw the second half of that confession, "But in every nation he that feareth him, and worketh righteousness, is accepted with him." (Acts 10:35) The second thing to understand is the assignment. Price writes that in the late 1980s Yahweh gave him the assignment to teach on race, religion and racism, and he does not dress up his response: "I knew in my spirit this was something I had to do, but to be truthful about it, I had been dragging my feet. After all, who wants to deal with this sensitive issue in a public forum?" That is a pastor confessing delay, and it is worth noticing that he put it in print. What moved him was a painful incident in the 1990s with a minister he counted a friend, which the next lesson tells. In his Preface he calls that incident the catalyst, and he says three years of research followed: magazine and newspaper articles, popular and scholarly books, letters, tapes and websites. The third thing is the message itself, which the lessons after this one follow step by step. In summary, he argues that racism and color prejudice are sin, because the God who made every nation of one blood shows no partiality, and James says without qualification, "But if ye have respect to persons, ye commit sin, and are convinced of the law as transgressors." (James 2:9) He distinguishes racial prejudice, which any person can carry, from racism as a system of power. He reads the Bible’s own record of color, from Cush in Eden’s rivers to the Ethiopian wife of Moses, and he takes apart the so-called curse of Ham from the text of Genesis. He holds a famous study Bible’s notes up against the verses they cite. He says the Church in America did not merely fail to stop slavery but blessed it; his line is, "That is the reason slavery could exist — because the Church gave its approval for slave owners to practice slavery and call themselves Christians." That is the heart of his charge that slavery could not have stood without the consent of the Church, and he is careful to add that there were genuine Christians who spoke against it. He says plainly that the answer is not to abandon the Bible: "This is not the white man’s religion; that is the lie that has been disseminated through the centuries. This is God’s Word for everyone who is willing to receive it." And he names the purpose of the whole work: "Put in its most basic terms, the purpose of this book is to expose racism in the Church and in our country for the sin that it is." Some readers heard anger in him, and he answered that directly: "I may appear angry, but my anger is a holy anger that is scripturally based. It is not anger against an individual person or group of persons, so whatever the color of your skin, as you read this book, I do not want you to feel intimidated." Now weigh him by the Word. On the center of his case he stands on rock. Yahweh made all nations of one blood; He is no respecter of persons; and James calls partiality in the assembly a sin by name. Paul tore down any wall of rank between believers: "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither bond nor free, there is neither male nor female: for ye are all one in Christ Jesus." (Galatians 3:28) A Church that baptized the ownership of human beings was not keeping the Word; it was bending it, and Price is right to say so plainly. He is also right that the cure is the Bible read rightly, not the Bible thrown away. The Bereans were called noble because they "searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so" (Acts 17:11), and that is exactly what Price did with the lie he was handed at the altar. Where he goes further than the text, in particular inferences about history or genetics, the lessons ahead name those points as his own reasoning and weigh them one by one. What does it mean for us now? It means the truth can be told by a man who once believed the lie, and it means grace runs through this story from the start. Price was not raised in the faith, believed a false teaching on the night he was saved, delayed the assignment he was given, and still Yahweh used him. It means we, too, can be wrong for years and corrected by the Word. And it means each of us must open the Book ourselves. Hear Price speak for himself in the lessons that follow, and weigh every line by the Word he loved.

## 2. Dr. Frederick K.C. Price — The Tape: Forgiven at Once, Corrected in Public

*id: `pv-price-the-tape` · 1654 words*

**The big idea.** In the 1990s a group of Black ministers handed Price a tape of a 1992 Sunday-morning sermon by a prominent white charismatic minister he knew well, telling a congregation that their families should not date or marry across the color line. Price forgave the man the moment he apologized in private, but he argued that apology and correction are different things: a word broadcast to thousands must be recanted where it was spoken. He took his call from Ephesians 5:11 to expose works of darkness, removed every name from the record, and kept the door open for reunion.

Every prophetic word in Scripture has an occasion, and Dr. Price’s series had one too. He had carried the assignment for years; he says Yahweh gave it to him in the late 1980s and that he dragged his feet. Then, in his words, something happened "so traumatic that it catalyzed me to take up my assignment from God and take on the subject of racism and religion." He describes the incident as "an example both of apparent racism and of apparent dishonesty," and he adds, "What made it particularly painful was that it occurred with someone I thought of as a friend; what made it particularly significant was that the man was a minister." The setting matters. Price was a leading Black voice inside the Word of Faith and charismatic world of the 1980s and 1990s, a fellowship whose teachers preached on television and at large conventions to audiences of every race. He had a long relationship with one of its most honored ministries. Then, he writes, "The incident began for me when a group of black ministers gave me a tape of a Sunday-morning sermon delivered in 1992 by a prominent white charismatic minister whom I knew well. My fellow black ministers were disturbed by the sermon, which was heard by thousands of people worldwide." In chapter 5 of his first volume he prints a transcription of it, and before he does, he removes every name: "I have deleted all names mentioned on the tape, because I am not interested in naming names. I am interested only in the content of what is being said, because I think that it speaks to the subject of racism and that it is symptomatic of the problem and shows where the issues lie." He also says plainly that it was not his purpose "to malign the white minister in any way." This lesson follows him and names no one. What did the tape say? In the transcription the minister tells parents that he cannot choose their children’s dates, but that if they do not want their children dating somebody, they must control it long before dating age. Then: "We can be friends with everybody; we are not prejudiced, but we are not going to date this group of people; it’s not in our culture to do it. We’re not going to do it." Price reports that the sermon went on from dating to the question of who should marry whom along the color line. Now follow his argument step by step. First, it was not a slip of the tongue. "I was as disturbed by the tape as my colleagues were. The recorded version of this excerpt was eight minutes long. A man does not say what this man said off the cuff over an eight-minute period." He grounds this in Jesus’ own teaching, that "out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh" (Matthew 12:34), and asks, "What was in the heart of the minister who delivered this sermon? And why was it there?" He adds that this was no beginner: "He did not just get saved three weeks before he preached that sermon. He has been around the Word of God since before he was born." Second, it was not a private matter. The delegation of Black ministers, he writes, "were hurt, outraged and bewildered by it. They were doubly mystified that it came from a ministry that is held in such high esteem in the charismatic community." They came to Price because of his long standing with that ministry. He admits he could have kept quiet. In the bluntest line of the book he describes his position at that time with the old plantation word for the slave who served in the master’s house, close to power and expected to keep the peace. He refused the role. Third, Scripture required him to speak. Paul wrote, "And have no fellowship with the unfruitful works of darkness, but rather reprove them." (Ephesians 5:11) Price comments: "That is actually every Christian’s call. Works of darkness should be exposed, and racism is darkness developed out of the very pit of hell. It needs to be exposed." He also remembers Paul’s question to the Galatians, "Am I therefore become your enemy, because I tell you the truth?" (Galatians 4:16) Fourth, his anger was aimed at a principle, not a person. He compares it to Jesus cleansing the temple, and to Jesus in the synagogue, who, Mark writes, "looked round about on them with anger, being grieved for the hardness of their hearts" (Mark 3:5). Price says, "I am not angry with any individual person, but I am angry about a situation that until now, apparently, nobody has fully addressed." And, "That is the kind of anger I have; it is holy, righteous indignation. Do not take it personally. I am angry about a principle that should have been dealt with but was not." Paul allows that anger and fences it: "Be ye angry, and sin not: let not the sun go down upon your wrath:" (Ephesians 4:26) Fifth, and this is the hinge of the story, apology is not correction. The minister apologized to Price privately, and later read a statement from his pulpit: "In one of the lessons I made a few statements that came out all wrong; I wish to apologize for those statements and ask for forgiveness for them." Price’s answer is the sentence this lesson is built on: "It was never about apology. I forgave him the moment he said, “We’re so sorry that we hurt you.” I told him, “Apology accepted, but we have to deal with this situation. We have to fix it. There is no way in the world that I can hold anything against you, but what about the tape of your sermon? You’ve got to correct the statements you made.”" Forgiveness, for Price, was not negotiable; he writes elsewhere that as a child of God he does "not have any choice but to forgive someone or I hinder my own blessings." But forgiveness settled the account between two men. It did not unteach thousands of listeners. When a letter later went out on the ministry’s letterhead telling Price’s own members that he would not accept the apology, he called it "another lie that needed to be corrected publicly because it was sent out publicly." In his letter to the head of that ministry he explained why fellowship had to pause: "We felt that to continue the fellowship with you, with no reconciliation of the statements, would be to send a very disturbing message to the Body of Christ, and especially to those of color who view me, as I said in an earlier letter, as someone they look up to, as a leader." What would correction have sounded like? Price gives the model in plain speech: "You know what? I lied. I repent of it." What did he call the hearer to do? To refuse silence when darkness is preached; to forgive at once and without conditions; and to require that a public error be recanted where it was spoken, for the sake of those who heard it. And he kept the door open. Near the end of the book he writes that he stands ready to respond whenever the minister is ready to recant, and, "I yearn to see our ministries once again united, as I yearn to see racism eliminated from the Body of Christ." Now weigh it by the Word. Price is right that forgiveness is commanded and immediate. Paul says, "Forbearing one another, and forgiving one another, if any man have a quarrel against any: even as Christ forgave you, so also do ye." (Colossians 3:13) He is right that forgiveness does not erase the need to turn. Jesus joined the two in one sentence: "Take heed to yourselves: If thy brother trespass against thee, rebuke him; and if he repent, forgive him." (Luke 17:3) And the proverb names exactly what Price asked for: "He that covereth his sins shall not prosper: but whoso confesseth and forsaketh them shall have mercy." (Proverbs 28:13) Confessing is the apology; forsaking is the correction. Mercy is promised to the one who does both. Price also followed the order Jesus gave. He went to the man first, privately, in a car, as Jesus teaches: "Moreover if thy brother shall trespass against thee, go and tell him his fault between thee and him alone: if he shall hear thee, thou hast gained thy brother." (Matthew 18:15) Only when the public record stayed uncorrected did he speak publicly, and even then he removed the names. Where we must be careful is in the judgment of another man’s heart. Price reasoned from the length and content of the sermon to its source, and Matthew 12:34 gives him ground to do so; but the final judge of hearts is Yahweh, and Price himself said he was angry at a principle, not a person. That is the posture to keep. What does it mean for us now? Our words travel farther than his tape did. A sermon clip, a post or a joke at a men’s breakfast can reach thousands before lunch. When we wrong a people in public, a private apology is the beginning, not the end. And when we are the ones wronged, we forgive before the sentence is finished, and still ask, with love and without contempt, for the truth to be set right where everyone can hear it. That is grace and truth together, the way Jesus carries them.

TALK ABOUT IT TOGETHER. Parents, ask your children how Dr. Price forgave the minister at once and still corrected him in public, and listen to the end before you teach. Children, ask your parents or a grandparent what they remember. Friends, talk it over and share one wrong you will forgive and one truth you will still speak in love.

**Verses quoted:** Matthew 12:34; Ephesians 5:11; Galatians 4:16; Mark 3:5; Ephesians 4:26; Colossians 3:13; Luke 17:3; Proverbs 28:13; Matthew 18:15

**Honest note.** Price names neither the minister nor his ministry in the book, and this lesson follows him. The tape is known only through Price’s transcription, which he says he stripped of names; where his transcription of the sermon ends and his own comment begins is not marked in every passage, so only lines plainly inside the transcription are given here as the minister’s. Price’s reading of the minister’s heart is his judgment, offered from Matthew 12:34, and is presented as his.

## 3. Dr. Frederick K.C. Price — Racism and Prejudice: Naming the Sin

*id: `pv-price-naming-the-sin` · 1484 words*

**The big idea.** Price insists that the Church call racism by its Bible name: sin. He separates racial prejudice, an attitude any person of any color can carry, from racism as a system of power, borrowing the definitions of a white educator, Paul Kivel, and a Black economist, Dr. Claud Anderson. But on the moral verdict he refuses to split hairs: racism is sin, and racial, ethnic and color prejudice is sin, because they insult the God who made every people and they break His plain command against partiality.

When Dr. Price began his series in October 1997, many American churches still treated racism as a political subject, a matter for editorials and elections, not for repentance at the altar. Black congregations had preached against injustice for generations, yet Price wrote that inside the Church as a whole it was a situation that, as far as he could see, "nobody has fully addressed." His assignment, as he understood it, was to bring it under the Word and give it its Bible name. The series opens with the sentence that its own description still carries: "There is a difference between racism and racial prejudice." That distinction runs through the first volume, and the lesson begins there. Follow his argument step by step. First, he defines terms. In chapter 8, The Underlying Reason for Racism, Price introduces Paul Kivel, author of Uprooting Racism, an educator "who happens to be white," and Dr. Claud Anderson, a Black economist and author of Black Labor, White Wealth. He quotes Kivel’s definition: racism is "the institutionalization of social injustice based on skin color, other physical characteristics, and cultural and religious difference." Kivel continues that white racism is "the uneven and unfair distribution of power, privilege, land and material goods favoring white people." Price chose the white writer on purpose, and said so: "Again, Paul Kivel is a white man, not an angry black revolutionary." So in Price’s usage prejudice is an attitude of the heart that any person of any color can carry, while racism is that attitude joined to power and built into a nation’s institutions. Second, he draws the power line with care. Drawing on Anderson, he argues that a prejudiced person who has no power can insult and protest but cannot enslave, exclude or ruin a people, while a group that holds the law, the banks and the land can. And he immediately guards it against misuse, in his own words: "Dr. Anderson is not saying black people cannot be prejudiced. He is not saying that Blacks cannot be bigots." He is not handing any group a license. Third, he gives the theological reason it is evil. On pages 27 and 28 he writes, "To me, racism is evil because it is an affront to God. In fact, racism is telling God that He is a blithering idiot because He did not know that when He created black people, He was creating an inferior product. If you are a racist, in essence that is what you are saying." He presses the logic: if Yahweh made all men of one blood, how could only one part of what He made be inferior? Fourth, he pronounces the verdict, and it covers both words. "Racism is evil; racial, ethnic and color prejudice are evil; they do not come from God and they are not of faith. Therefore, they are sin." And, in another place, more plainly still: "Racism is a sin; racial, ethnic and color prejudice is a sin. So if you act in a prejudiced manner, or if you support racial discrimination, you are in sin." He alludes to Paul’s principle, "whatsoever is not of faith is sin" (Romans 14:23), and to the command, "Abstain from all appearance of evil." (1 Thessalonians 5:22) Fifth, he says it is learned. "Racism is not in the genes, it starts in the home. I am not saying every white person is a racist." He reaches for a hard example to show that a sin does not need every person to carry it in order to do great harm: "When Adolf Hitler came to power, it did not take every person who was a German citizen to slaughter millions of innocent people. So it doesn’t have to be everybody." Sixth, he names it partiality, the Bible’s own word. "If God does not show partiality, how can we? Why should any of us show partiality, unless we are prejudiced?" In his second volume, on James 2:9, he writes, "The wonder is that so many Church leaders have not recognized that showing partiality to white people over black people is a sin. It has been in the Bible in black and white all this time." What did he call the hearer to do? Name it as sin, without euphemism. Repent of it where it lives in us. Stop showing partiality in the house of God. And, as he puts it elsewhere, "Put yourself in a black person’s shoes, and you will get a different perspective on the enormity of the wrong that has been perpetuated against black people in this country." Before weighing it, notice how carefully Price keeps the two halves of his argument together. The first half explains history: why prejudice held by people with the law, the land and the banks did generations of damage to Black Americans that prejudice without power could not do. That is documented harm, and he states it plainly. The second half judges hearts: any person, of any color, who looks down on another people is in sin. He does not let the first half excuse anyone from the second, and he does not let the second half erase the first. That balance is why his definitions help rather than divide. He also gives his readers a way to look inward. He asks white readers to examine whether they have absorbed a negative way of viewing other races from their parents and grandparents without knowing it, and he asks every reader to step into another’s shoes. That is not an accusation against a whole group; he says plainly, "I am not saying every white person is a racist." It is an invitation to self-examination, the kind Paul commends when he tells believers to examine themselves. Now weigh it by the Word. Price is right on the verdict, and right that the Word, not sociology, settles it. Yahweh’s own character is the standard: Moses says He "regardeth not persons, nor taketh reward" (Deuteronomy 10:17), and Paul says, "For there is no respect of persons with God." (Romans 2:11) James opens his letter’s second chapter with the command, "My brethren, have not the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory, with respect of persons." (James 2:1) and then applies it to the seating of a church service, where the man with the gold ring gets the good seat and the poor man is told to stand: "Are ye not then partial in yourselves, and are become judges of evil thoughts?" (James 2:4) Then he concludes, "But if ye have respect to persons, ye commit sin, and are convinced of the law as transgressors." (James 2:9) John removes the last hiding place: "If a man say, I love God, and hateth his brother, he is a liar: for he that loveth not his brother whom he hath seen, how can he love God whom he hath not seen?" (1 John 4:20) Notice what these verses do not ask. They do not ask how much power the sinner holds. They judge the heart. That means the distinction Price borrowed from Kivel and Anderson is useful for describing history and systems: it explains why prejudice backed by law, money and land did generations of damage that prejudice without power could not do. But it does not shrink anyone’s accountability before Yahweh, and Price did not use it that way. He said prejudice is sin, in anyone. Where the definitions are debated, the Word is not. The Law already forbade partial judgment either way: "thou shalt not respect the person of the poor, nor honor the person of the mighty" (Leviticus 19:15). Respecting persons is sin whether it flatters the powerful or excuses the weak. Here also is the grace in Price’s teaching. Because racism is learned, it can be unlearned. Because it is sin, it can be confessed and forgiven, and the Blood of Jesus reaches it like every other sin. Moses told Israel to teach Yahweh’s words "when thou sittest in thine house" (Deuteronomy 6:7), and the same home where contempt was taught can become the place where it is uprooted. What does it mean for us now? It means our churches can stop speaking about race only as a grievance or only as a politics, and speak of it as the Word does, as partiality that grieves Yahweh. It means each of us checks our own table talk, our seating, our jokes and our fears, whatever our color. And it means we can say the word sin out loud, not to crush anyone but to open the door Yahweh always opens to the one who confesses.

TALK ABOUT IT TOGETHER. Parents, ask your children why Dr. Price called racism by its Bible name, sin, and listen to the end before you teach. Children, ask your parents or a grandparent what they remember. Friends, talk it over and name one partiality you will turn from this week.

**Honest note.** The definitions of racism as institutional power are Paul Kivel’s (Uprooting Racism) and Dr. Claud Anderson’s (Black Labor, White Wealth), quoted and adopted by Price; they are attributed to them here. That distinction is a definition of terms, not a teaching of Scripture, and writers who share Price’s faith use the words differently; the moral verdict this lesson teaches stands on the Word whichever definition is used.

## 4. Dr. Frederick K.C. Price — One Blood, One Race: "The Subject Is Dust"

*id: `pv-price-one-blood` · 1507 words*

**The big idea.** Price argues from Acts 17:26 that there is only one race, the human race: Yahweh made all nations of one blood, and the Bible sorts people by kindred, tongue, people and nation, never by color. Every human body is dust from one ground, so no dust is better than another, and the many colors of mankind came from one source for Yahweh’s purpose. In Christ the flesh-categories end altogether, and a nation that calls itself Christian must stop knowing people by the flesh.

Price’s first volume turns, in its second part, from his own story to the beginning of the human story. The setting matters. He was teaching in the late 1990s to a nation that, within living memory, had written race into law by blood. Tennessee in 1910 and Virginia in 1930, with other states between, made the one-drop rule law, so that a single ancestor decided a person’s race. The American Red Cross kept donated blood separated by race until 1950, over the protest of the Black surgeon Dr. Charles Drew, who called it without scientific foundation. And laws against marriage across the color line stood until June 12, 1967, when the Supreme Court struck down Virginia’s in Loving v. Virginia. Churches had sung under those laws and, too often, defended them. Price meets all of it with a verse. His thesis is one sentence: "There is really only one race, the human race. That is what the Bible tells us in Acts 17:26: That God made all nations out of one blood." Follow his argument step by step. First, one blood. When Paul stood among the philosophers of Athens he began with the God "that made the world and all things therein" (Acts 17:24), the One who gives "to all life, and breath, and all things" (Acts 17:25), and then said, "And hath made of one blood all nations of men for to dwell on all the face of the earth, and hath determined the times before appointed, and the bounds of their habitation;" (Acts 17:26) Price draws it out plainly: "God only made one blood, not six bloods, not five bloods." And, "Every nation comes out of one blood." He adds Paul’s word to the Corinthians, "All flesh is not the same flesh: but there is one kind of flesh of men, another flesh of beasts, another of fishes, and another of birds." (1 Corinthians 15:39) There is one kind of flesh of men. Second, how the Bible sorts people. "The Bible never distinguishes by skin color or appearance, but only by kindred, tongue, people and nation. Never by color." This is an observation anyone can test. Genesis 10 is the Bible’s table of the nations after the flood. It lists the sons of Japheth, Ham and Shem, and closes each family the same way: "These are the sons of Shem, after their families, after their tongues, in their lands, after their nations." (Genesis 10:31) Families, tongues, lands, nations; no colors. The Bible ends with the same categories. When the elders sing the new song to the Lamb, they say, "And they sung a new song, saying, Thou art worthy to take the book, and to open the seals thereof: for thou wast slain, and hast redeemed us to God by thy blood out of every kindred, and tongue, and people, and nation;" (Revelation 5:9) Third, one color, many shades. Price writes, "There is only one color; what we see in people is a myriad of colors created by a continual mixing of color groups." He asks why Yahweh did not make everyone one color, which might have spared the world much trouble, and answers, "But we have many colors, and they all came from the same source, so He must have had a purpose." Fourth, dust. Genesis says, "And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul." (Genesis 2:7) After the fall Yahweh said to Adam, "In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread, till thou return unto the ground; for out of it wast thou taken: for dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return." (Genesis 3:19) Price lands one of the most memorable passages in the book on this verse: "Notice that the subject is not skin color; the subject is dust. God did not say you are white, you are black, you are red, you are yellow, you are brown; God said you are dust." He tells his reader to look in the mirror and say, in his words, "My body is nothing but dust. And there is no dust better than any other dust. It is all dust. And eventually it is all swept away." He puts the same thought another way in the same chapter: "White dust, black dust, red dust, yellow dust, brown dust — just dust." Fifth, one family. In chapter 6, Adam, Eve and Genetics, Price argues that all the shades of mankind were in the first couple: "when God created Adam, He placed in Adam the genetic coding for all the colors of all the people who were ultimately going to inhabit the planet." He writes that "Adam is all mankind," and Scripture agrees that Eve is "the mother of all living" (Genesis 3:20). Sixth, in Christ. Price takes the reader to 2 Corinthians 5:16-17 and says the verses "show us exactly what is wrong with this nation that calls itself Christian." Paul writes, "Wherefore henceforth know we no man after the flesh: yea, though we have known Christ after the flesh, yet now henceforth know we him no more." (2 Corinthians 5:16) and then, "Therefore if any man be in Christ, he is a new creature: old things are passed away; behold, all things are become new." (2 Corinthians 5:17) He adds Colossians 3:11, where in the new man there is neither Greek nor Jew, Barbarian nor Scythian, but Christ is all and in all. Price also uses the account of Adam naming the animals to make a point that is easy to miss. When Yahweh brought every beast and bird to Adam, no suitable companion was found among them, because man is a different kind of flesh. Adam was not one kind among many human kinds; in Price’s words, "Adam is all mankind." The whole human race is one kind, and every person in it is in the image of God: "So God created man in his own image, in the image of God created he him; male and female created he them." (Genesis 1:27) That is why the dust-and-breath account and the one-blood sermon in Athens say the same thing from two ends of the Bible. What did he call the hearer to do? To stop knowing people after the flesh. To see every person as made from the same dust, of the same blood, for the same purpose. And, as he puts it in the call that runs through the whole volume, to "Put yourself in a black person’s shoes." Now weigh it by the Word. On the center of his case Price is plainly right, and he is right on the text. One blood is Paul’s word, not a sociologist’s. One dust is Moses’ word. One first mother is Adam’s own name for Eve. The prophet Malachi asked Israel the same question Price asked America: "Have we not all one father? hath not one God created us? why do we deal treacherously every man against his brother" (Malachi 2:10). And the redeemed in Revelation are counted by kindred, tongue, people and nation, the same categories Genesis 10 uses, with no color anywhere. In two places Price reaches beyond the text, and honesty requires saying so. First, he follows the creationist writers Ken Ham, Andrew Snelling and Carl Wieland in arguing that Adam and Eve "had to be mid-brown" to carry the genes for every shade. Scripture does not tell us the color of Adam and Eve; it tells us that every nation came from them. That reasoning is his, built on their book, and it should be held as such. Second, he writes that Noah’s three sons "are the patriarchs of the three major color groups that exist today." Genesis does trace all nations to Shem, Ham and Japheth, "and of them was the whole earth overspread" (Genesis 9:19); but it never assigns them colors, and Price’s own rule, "Never by color," is the better reading of his own Bible. Where his reasoning outruns the text, the text he loved corrects it, and his main point only grows stronger. What does it mean for us now? It means the Word dismantled the drop-of-blood order long before the courts did, and it still dismantles every new way of ranking people by the flesh, including the ways the wounded sometimes rank in return. It means that at every funeral we preach the same sermon Price preached in that chapter: dust to dust, no dust better than another. And it means our churches should look like the song of Revelation 5: every kindred, tongue, people and nation, singing to the one Lamb who bought them all with His Blood.

TALK ABOUT IT TOGETHER. Parents, ask your children what it means that Yahweh made every nation of one blood, and listen to the end before you teach. Children, ask your parents or a grandparent what they remember. Friends, talk it over and tell each other one way you will treat every person as kin.

Genesis 10 records the nations by families, tongues, lands and nations and names no colors, and Price’s own principle, "Never by color," is the better guide; those two points are taught here as his reasoning, not as Scripture.

## 5. Dr. Frederick K.C. Price — Color in the Bible: Cush, Egypt and the Wife of Moses

*id: `pv-price-color-in-the-bible` · 1529 words*

**The big idea.** Price argues that the Bible itself puts Africa and African peoples at the center of its story: Cush is named in the description of Eden, Mizraim the son of Ham is the Hebrew name of Egypt, and Moses married a Cushite woman. When Miriam and Aaron spoke against that marriage, Yahweh rebuked the critics, not Moses. He is careful to say he does not claim everyone in the Bible was Black, only that the record tells the truth that others hid, and that a lie about someone’s color must not be used to keep a people down.

For most of the twentieth century American churches taught the Bible through pictures: Sunday school papers, stained glass, church fans from the funeral home, and, after 1956, Cecil B. DeMille’s film The Ten Commandments. In nearly all of them every Bible figure was European. Dr. Price grew up under those images, and in the second part of his first volume, in a chapter titled Africa, the Birthplace of Humanity, he set them beside the text. His third volume expands the theme in a chapter titled Color in the Bible. His question is not whether everyone in Scripture was Black; he says plainly, "It is not my contention that everyone in the Bible was black." His question is whether the Church told the truth about who is in the Book, and whether the untruth was used against a people. Follow his argument step by step. First, Eden. Genesis describes a river going out of Eden and parting into four heads. Of the first it says, "The name of the first is Pison: that is it which compasseth the whole land of Havilah, where there is gold;" (Genesis 2:11) Of the second, "And the name of the second river is Gihon: the same is it that compasseth the whole land of Ethiopia." (Genesis 2:13) Price writes, "It is interesting that in describing the garden of Eden, the names Havilah and Cush (Ethiopia) are mentioned. Havilah was a son of Cush, who was a son of Ham." He notes that the King James renders the Hebrew Cush as Ethiopia, and from the two names he reasons that Eden lay in Africa. Second, who these people were. Genesis 10 gives the sons of Ham: "And the sons of Ham; Cush, and Mizraim, and Phut, and Canaan." (Genesis 10:6) and "And the sons of Cush; Seba, and Havilah, and Sabtah, and Raamah, and Sabtechah: and the sons of Raamah; Sheba, and Dedan." (Genesis 10:7) Price concludes, "it’s clear that some people were black. Among them were Ham, Cush and Havilah." The record outside Scripture agrees on the land of Cush. The Cush of the Bible is the ancient kingdom of Kush on the Upper Nile, centered at Napata and Meroë in what is now Sudan; the Greeks and Romans called that region Aithiopia, which is how Ethiopia came into the English Bible; and the Jewish historian Josephus, writing in the first century, said the Ethiopians over whom Cush reigned were still called Cushites in his day. For nearly a century, from 744 to 656 BC, the kings of Kush ruled Egypt as its Twenty-fifth Dynasty, and one of them appears in Scripture by name: when Sennacherib of Assyria threatened Jerusalem, "he heard say of Tirhakah king of Ethiopia, Behold, he is come out to fight against thee" (2 Kings 19:9). The Bible itself records what everyone in Jeremiah’s time knew about them: "Can the Ethiopian change his skin, or the leopard his spots?" (Jeremiah 13:23) Third, Egypt. Price writes that Mizraim, a son of Ham, "in the Hebrew language translates to the English word Egypt." That is simply true: Mizraim is the Hebrew name for Egypt and its people. The Psalms use the same identification in parallel lines: "Israel also came into Egypt; and Jacob sojourned in the land of Ham." (Psalms 105:23) and, of the plagues, Yahweh "smote all the firstborn in Egypt; the chief of their strength in the tabernacles of Ham" (Psalms 78:51). From this Price reasons that the people of Egypt must have been Black, and he takes aim at the film that showed Pharaoh as white with one of his most important sentences: "I don’t care what he was, but I do care if someone tells me he was something that he was not, and then uses that false information against me to keep black people enslaved." That is the real concern of the chapter. A false picture of the past was used to justify a cruel present. Fourth, the wife of Moses. Here Price stands on the plainest text of all. "And Miriam and Aaron spake against Moses because of the Ethiopian woman whom he had married: for he had married an Ethiopian woman." (Numbers 12:1) He writes in the first volume, "Moses, the Bible tells us, married an Ethiopian," and adds, "Apparently, however, interracial marriage was all right with God, because He did not chastise Moses for marrying a black woman." In the second volume he presses it: "Moses wasn’t just having a sexual relationship with her, he married her; they became man and wife. He was Hebrew, she was a black Ethiopian." Miriam and Aaron used the marriage as an opening to question Moses’ standing, asking, "Hath the LORD indeed spoken only by Moses? hath he not spoken also by us?" (Numbers 12:2) Yahweh called all three to the tabernacle, defended His servant, and then, "And the anger of the LORD was kindled against them; and he departed." (Numbers 12:9) Price draws the lesson: "God did not get angry with Moses," and, after reading the whole chapter, "There is nothing in it that says that God identifies miscegenation as causing disunity among His people. God identifies criticism as the problem." He also prints a letter from a reader who was offended that he had said Moses married a Black woman, and answers that the writer had never read Numbers 12. What did he call the hearer to do? To read the text instead of the picture. To stop letting a false image of the past be used against living people. And to see that Yahweh Himself defended a Cushite woman against the critics in His own leadership. Now weigh it by the Word. Price is right on the central facts, and they are textual facts. Cush is named at Eden. Cush and Mizraim are sons of Ham. Egypt is the land of Ham. Moses married a Cushite woman. Yahweh rebuked her critics and not her husband. The prophet Amos even heard Yahweh rebuke Israel’s pride in these terms: "Are ye not as children of the Ethiopians unto me, O children of Israel? saith the LORD. Have not I brought up Israel out of the land of Egypt? and the Philistines from Caphtor, and the Syrians from Kir?" (Amos 9:7) And when the Ethiopian eunuch, treasurer of Candace, queen of the Ethiopians, read Isaiah in his chariot, the Spirit sent Philip to him; Africa is in the first wave of the Gospel. In three places Price reasons past what the text can carry, and the record lets us say exactly where. Genesis 2 names the Tigris and Euphrates alongside Cush, and the flood reshaped the earth, so the text does not let anyone fix Eden to one modern country. Genesis 10 names two men called Havilah, a son of Cush and also a son of Joktan in Shem’s line, so the land of Havilah in Eden cannot be assigned to Cush with certainty. And the biblical Cush is the Nile kingdom of Kush, in today’s Sudan, rather than the modern nation of Ethiopia. As for the skin color of the ancient Egyptians, Scripture names their ancestry, Ham, and does not describe their complexion; historians argue it, and this course takes it up in the lessons on Diop and Obenga. None of these corrections touches his main point. They sharpen it: the people of Cush, Egypt and Ham are African, the Bible says so in Hebrew and in the Psalms, and Yahweh defended a Cushite wife against His own prophets. One more witness from the text belongs here. When the prophet Jeremiah was let down into a dungeon of mire to die, it was "Ebedmelech the Ethiopian, one of the eunuchs which was in the king’s house" (Jeremiah 38:7) who went to the king and had him drawn out. Yahweh sent him a personal promise through Jeremiah, ending, "because thou hast put thy trust in me, saith the LORD" (Jeremiah 39:18). A Cushite official is remembered in Scripture as the man of courage who saved the prophet when Judah’s own princes would not. What does it mean for us now? First, it means truth about the past matters, because lies about the past are used in the present. Second, it means mercy is part of the record too. Miriam was struck, and Moses, whose wife she had attacked, did not gloat. "And Moses cried unto the LORD, saying, Heal her now, O God, I beseech thee." (Numbers 12:13) After seven days she was received back into the camp. The one who was wronged prayed for the one who did wrong. That is the posture Price asked for when he forgave quickly and still told the truth, and it is the posture we keep: tell the truth about who is in the Book, and pray for the healing of those who told it wrong.

Children, ask your parents or a grandparent what they remember. Friends, talk it over and find one African place named in the Word and read it together.

**Honest note.** The texts stand on their own: Cush is named in Genesis 2, Mizraim (Egypt) and Cush are sons of Ham, Egypt is called the land of Ham, Moses married a Cushite woman, and Yahweh rebuked her critics. ## 6. Dr. Frederick K.C. Price — The So-Called Curse of Ham: "Noah Did the Cursing"

*id: `pv-price-curse-of-ham` · 1467 words*

**The big idea.** Price takes apart the teaching, used for generations to justify slavery, that Black people bear a curse of Ham. Read the text, he says: God never cursed Ham; Noah spoke the words, and he named Canaan, not Ham; nothing in Genesis 9 mentions color; and no one can say what shade such a curse would be. Even if a curse had fallen, Christ has redeemed us from the curse of the law. The lie was in the notes, not in the Book.

No misreading of Genesis did more harm to our people than the so-called curse of Ham. The claim was that when Noah cursed after the flood, the curse fell on Ham and all his descendants, that it marked them with dark skin, and that it consigned them to servitude. In America it became a standard Bible defense of slavery, documented by historians such as Stephen R. Haynes in Noah’s Curse: The Biblical Justification of American Slavery. And it did not stay in the slaveholders’ pamphlets. It was printed in study Bibles that sat on thousands of pulpits. The 1917 Scofield Reference Bible, in its note on Genesis 9, stated, "A prophetic declaration is made that from Ham will descend an inferior and servile posterity", and it cited Genesis 9:24-25 for it. Dr. Price met this teaching in his own life before he met it in a book. In his first chapter, My Assignment From God, he asks, "How many generations of Blacks have believed that they are cursed and inferior to the rest of mankind because of the so-called curse of Ham, a corrupted teaching of the Bible that has been traditionally propounded by certain Christians?" In both volumes he answers it the same way: open Genesis 9 and read what it says. Follow the text with him. After the flood, "And the sons of Noah, that went forth of the ark, were Shem, and Ham, and Japheth: and Ham is the father of Canaan." (Genesis 9:18) Then Noah planted a vineyard, and "And he drank of the wine, and was drunken; and he was uncovered within his tent." (Genesis 9:21) The next verse reads, "And Ham, the father of Canaan, saw the nakedness of his father, and told his two brethren without." (Genesis 9:22) Shem and Japheth took a garment and covered their father, walking backward so as not to see. Then, "And Noah awoke from his wine, and knew what his younger son had done unto him." (Genesis 9:24) and "And he said, Cursed be Canaan; a servant of servants shall he be unto his brethren." (Genesis 9:25) Noah goes on to bless the God of Shem, to pray for Japheth’s enlargement, and to say twice more that Canaan shall be servant. That is the whole of it. Now follow Price’s argument step by step. First, who spoke? "God never cursed Ham at all. Noah did the cursing — but it was not Ham he cursed, it was Ham’s son Canaan. And Noah is not God." In the second volume he presses the point: "It doesn’t say, “God said to Noah, ‘Noah, curse Canaan.’ ”" The verse is a father’s word on waking, not a recorded word of Yahweh. Second, who was named? Canaan, the youngest son of Ham, and only Canaan. Ham’s other sons, Cush, Mizraim and Phut, the fathers of the African peoples in Genesis 10, are not mentioned in the curse at all. Third, what was said? Nothing about color. "Whatever it’s called, God did not curse Canaan, Noah did, and the curse was not blackness." Price adds a point about the wording, that Noah did not say Canaan and his posterity would be servants forever, and did not say to whom, beyond his brethren. Fourth, the theory defeats itself. Price writes, "If God cursed Ham and the curse was blackness so that all of his children came out black, then what shade of black would they be? Black people come in every shade — but if God cursed Ham, it would mean that every cursed black person would have to be the same shade." Fifth, the commentators. In the second volume he turns to the note in Scofield’s Bible and makes the simplest possible test: "When we read Genesis 9:24-25, the verse Scofield cites in reference to his statement, we should find something about Ham’s descendants being “inferior.”" There is no such word. The source tells on itself: Scofield’s own Bible prints the verse that names Canaan and annotates it with a note about Ham. Sixth, the Cross. "If, as the Bible says, I am redeemed from the curse of the law through Christ, then, if blackness is a curse, the blackness of my skin ought to vanish and I ought to be another color." Paul’s words are, "Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us: for it is written, Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree:" (Galatians 3:13) and the purpose clause that follows is for every nation: "That the blessing of Abraham might come on the Gentiles through Jesus Christ; that we might receive the promise of the Spirit through faith." (Galatians 3:14) What did he call the hearer to do? Read the verse before believing the note. Refuse a teaching that adds words to Scripture in order to subjugate a people. And let the Black believer who carried this lie in secret set it down. Notice also what Price’s method protects. He does not argue that the curse of Ham was merely unkind, or out of date, or culturally conditioned. He argues that it was never in the text, and he proves it from the text. That is a stronger answer, because it does not depend on anyone’s feelings, and it does not require anyone to set the Bible aside. It requires only that the Bible be read. Price himself knew the cost of the lie in Black hearts. In the same chapter where he first names it, he writes that Black people in America had been taught to believe the white man had all the answers. The curse-of-Ham teaching was one of those answers, and his life’s testimony was that the Word answers it better. Now weigh it by the Word. Price is right on the text, and the text is not hard. Noah speaks; Canaan is named; no color appears; and Yahweh never adopts the words as His own. The Law itself forbids the idea that a son inherits a father’s guilt: "every man shall be put to death for his own sin" (Deuteronomy 24:16), and the prophets repeat it, "The soul that sinneth, it shall die. The son shall not bear the iniquity of the father, neither shall the father bear the iniquity of the son: the righteousness of the righteous shall be upon him, and the wickedness of the wicked shall be upon him." (Ezekiel 18:20) The proverb says that a curse without a cause does not land: "As the bird by wandering, as the swallow by flying, so the curse causeless shall not come." (Proverbs 26:2) And the hired prophet Balaam, sent to curse Israel, could only ask, "How shall I curse, whom God hath not cursed?" (Numbers 23:8) Two of Price’s phrases need the Word’s precision. He says Noah "probably was still hung over"; the text says only that Noah awoke from his wine, and we can leave it there. He also says God "does not curse anyone through all eternity." Scripture does record Yahweh pronouncing curses, on the serpent and the ground in Eden, and under the Law. Price’s meaning, from the context, is narrower and true: no curse is handed down a family line forever, and Christ has borne the curse for all who believe. Scripture even shows where Noah’s words about Canaan came to pass. When Israel was strong, the text says, "they put the Canaanites to tribute" (Judges 1:28), and Solomon levied bondservice on the peoples left in the land. That was the Canaanites of Canaan, in Canaan. It never touched Cush, Egypt or Phut, and it never touched a slave ship. And grace runs even through Canaan’s line, for "By faith the harlot Rahab perished not with them that believed not" (Hebrews 11:31). What does it mean for us now? It means a lie that sat on pulpits for generations can be undone by reading one verse aloud. It means our elders who carried it can lay it down. It means the preachers who taught it, and the publishers who printed it, can be forgiven, as Price forgave, while the record is corrected in plain words. And it means we read the notes in our study Bibles the way Price did, as a man’s word about the Word, always to be tested by the Word itself. In the end the Book answers every curse with the Lamb, and it closes with this promise: "And there shall be no more curse" (Revelation 22:3).

TALK ABOUT IT TOGETHER. Parents, ask your children who really spoke the curse in Genesis 9, and why the old teaching was wrong, and listen to the end before you teach. Children, ask your parents or a grandparent what they remember. Friends, talk it over and read Genesis 9:25 together and say who is named.

**Verses quoted:** Genesis 9:18; Genesis 9:21; Genesis 9:22; Genesis 9:24; Genesis 9:25; Galatians 3:13; Galatians 3:14; Deuteronomy 24:16; Ezekiel 18:20; Proverbs 26:2; Numbers 23:8; Judges 1:28; Hebrews 11:31; Revelation 22:3

**Honest note.** What Genesis 9 says is not in question: Noah, awaking from his wine, said "Cursed be Canaan," and no color appears. Two of Price’s phrases are his own reading beyond the verses: that Noah "probably was still hung over" (the text says he "awoke from his wine"), and that "God does not curse anyone through all eternity," which he means of a curse passed down a family line forever (Ezekiel 18:20), not of every curse in Scripture. Scripture also records the Canaanites of the land later put to tribute under Israel, which is where Noah’s words about Canaan are seen fulfilled; it never applies them to Cush, Mizraim or Phut.

## 7. Dr. Frederick K.C. Price — Testing the Note by the Text: Dake’s "30 Reasons"

*id: `pv-price-testing-the-note` · 1563 words*

**The big idea.** The most widely used Pentecostal study Bible of its day printed, in its notes on Acts, thirty reasons for the segregation of the races, starting from Acts 17:26. Price did not answer with opinion; he answered with a method. If a commentator’s claim is true, Scripture should confirm it verse after verse and nothing in Scripture should contradict it. Applied to the note, the test failed at every step: the verse cited teaches the unity of all nations, "kind" in Genesis means species not color, Israel’s marriages were forbidden for idolatry not race, and one reference pointed to a chapter that does not exist.

Price’s second volume carries the title Perverting the Gospel to Subjugate a People, and its central chapters are a case study in how that happens: not usually by tearing pages out of the Bible, but by writing notes beside it. The note he chose to test came from one of the most trusted study Bibles in the Pentecostal and charismatic world. Finis Jennings Dake, who lived from 1902 to 1987, published the Dake Annotated Reference Bible in 1963, with more than 35,000 notes and half a million cross-references. Price quotes the Dictionary of Pentecostal and Charismatic Movements to show its reach: "Dake’s impact upon conservative Pentecostalism cannot be overstated," and between 1961 and 1988, 372,000 volumes "rolled off the presses," with an estimated 28,000 to 30,000 still being sold every year. In the notes on the book of Acts, Price writes, "Dake lists “30 reasons for segregation of races.”" Dake also published a pamphlet titled 30 Reasons for Segregation of Races, which used biblical arguments to support segregation. Price worked from the 1992 printing. He saw what such a note did in a home: "As I write, this edition of Dake’s notes has been in print for 37 years. Can you imagine how many people have read those 30 reasons during that time and felt justified in continuing the racist attitudes they learned from their parents across the dinner table or over the backyard barbecue or in the car? How many people have been confirmed in their racism because a Bible commentator said the races ought to be segregated? These roots of racism have to be pulled up, because racism can never be eliminated until people get it out of their hearts." Now follow his argument step by step. First, the method. Price does not begin by attacking Dake. He sets a standard that would apply to any teacher, including himself: "if what Dake says is true, the Scripture should substantiate it. We should find verse after verse, Scripture after Scripture, illustration after illustration indicating that God wants the races to remain segregated. We should also find no Scriptures that contradict what Dake says, because God could then be accused of being double-minded." That is the Bible’s own standard for a matter: "at the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses, shall the matter be established" (Deuteronomy 19:15). Second, the first witness. Dake’s first text was the one the note sat on, Acts 17:26, which Price quotes and then answers twice. "There are two reasons why this Scripture in no way supports the idea of segregation of black people from white people. The first is that while God did make the boundaries of nations in certain places at the time He originally created them, those boundaries have been changing ever since. Indeed, sometimes, as with Moses leading God’s chosen people to the Promised Land, they changed because of God’s orders." The second reason, he says, is the profound one: "Far from telling us that the races should be segregated, Acts 17:26 is a statement of unity of all the people in God’s creation!" Read the verse and its neighbour and see it: "And hath made of one blood all nations of men for to dwell on all the face of the earth, and hath determined the times before appointed, and the bounds of their habitation;" (Acts 17:26) "That they should seek the Lord, if haply they might feel after him, and find him, though he be not far from every one of us:" (Acts 17:27) The bounds are set so that every nation might seek the Lord, who is not far from any of them. Third, the meaning of a word. Dake built several reasons on the word kind in Genesis. Price answers, "Dake says that “kind means type and color.” However, as we can see from the above Scriptures, in the book of Genesis, the word kind, generally means “species”". The verses are about animals and plants: "And God said, Let the earth bring forth the living creature after his kind, cattle, and creeping thing, and beast of the earth after his kind: and it was so." (Genesis 1:24) Nothing in them speaks of human color. Fourth, Israel’s marriage laws. Several of Dake’s reasons cited the laws against Israel marrying the nations around them. Price reads the texts Dake cited and finds their stated reason. Of the passage in Judges, where Israel took the daughters of the Canaanite peoples as wives and served their gods, he writes, "Nothing in this Scripture tells us that God condemned the children of Israel for interracial marriage — it says God condemned them for intermarriage with pagans." The Law says so itself: "Neither shalt thou make marriages with them; thy daughter thou shalt not give unto his son, nor his daughter shalt thou take unto thy son." (Deuteronomy 7:3) and the very next verse gives the reason, "For they will turn away thy son from following me, that they may serve other gods: so will the anger of the LORD be kindled against you, and destroy thee suddenly." (Deuteronomy 7:4) The prohibition is about faith, and the proof is that a foreigner who turned to Yahweh was welcomed: Ruth the Moabitess said "thy people shall be my people, and thy God my God" (Ruth 1:16), married Boaz, and became the great-grandmother of David. Price adds the partiality test: since Yahweh is no respecter of persons, if He cursed Israel for interracial marriage He would have to curse every nation for it, and Scripture shows nothing of the kind. Fifth, the writer revealed. On Dake’s fourth reason, Price says, "I believe that Dake reveals something about himself in Reason 4". The note, as Price prints it, reads, "Miscegenation means the mixture of races, especially the black and white races or those of outstanding type of color. The Bible even goes farther than opposing this. It is against different branches of the same stock intermarrying, such as Jews marrying other descendants of Abraham". Price’s point is that the commentator reached for black and white before any text required it. Sixth, a reference that does not exist. One of the note’s citations reads Ezra 13:1-30. Price writes, "The fact is: there is no Ezra 13." The book of Ezra has ten chapters; the note was not checked even against the table of contents. Seventh, a law no one gave. Of a rule Dake drew from Abraham’s charge about Isaac’s wife, Price writes, "The Bible does not say it is a law; Abraham does not say it is a law; Isaac does not say it is a law; only Dake says it is a law. That is frightening." Abraham’s charge was that Isaac not take a wife of the daughters of the Canaanites among whom he dwelt; it was a father’s charge, and Scripture never makes it a law of race. What did Price call the hearer to do? To test every commentator, however honored, by the Scripture he cites. To refuse to let a note become a law. And to pull the roots up in our own hearts. Now weigh it by the Word. Price’s method is not his invention; it is the Berean way, for those in Berea "These were more noble than those in Thessalonica, in that they received the word with all readiness of mind, and searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so." (Acts 17:11) His conclusion on Acts 17:26 stands on the verse. His reading of kind stands on Genesis. His reading of Israel’s marriage laws is the Law’s own explanation in Deuteronomy 7:4, and Ruth confirms it. The note added to the Word, and Proverbs speaks to that directly: "Add thou not unto his words, lest he reprove thee, and thou be found a liar." (Proverbs 30:6) Where Price reads Dake’s heart from Reason 4, that is his judgment of a man, and it is fair to hold it as such; the text-work stands on its own without it. And here the story turns toward grace, which the next part of this course tells in full. In January 1997 the publisher printed a revised edition with the references the family called racially insensitive removed, and in February 1998, after hearing Price’s teaching, the Dake family wrote to him personally asking forgiveness. A note can be wrong for decades, and a family can still make it right. What does it mean for us now? It means we keep our study Bibles and our favorite teachers, and we test them, every one, the way Price tested Dake. It means a reference you have not looked up is not yet evidence. It means that when a note puts people in rank by color, the verse it cites will almost always say the opposite. And it means we hold the same standard for our own teachers, our own sermons and our own notes, because every one of us has said something about the Bible that the Bible did not say. Precept upon precept, line upon line: the Word explains the Word.

TALK ABOUT IT TOGETHER. Parents, ask your children how to test a study-Bible note by the text itself, and listen to the end before you teach. Children, ask your parents or a grandparent what they remember. Friends, talk it over and check one note or saying against the verse it cites.

## 8. Dr. Frederick K.C. Price — The source that tells on itself: the Dake family’s letter

*id: `pv-price-the-dake-letter` · 1592 words*

**The big idea.** While Price was teaching Race, Religion & Racism on television, the family behind Finis Dake’s study Bible wrote to him themselves, in a faxed letter dated February 18, 1998. They asked forgiveness and admitted in writing that Dake’s thirty reasons for separating the races can be read as advocating racism. Price answered on February 25, 1998: apology accepted, and "This is not personal; it’s principle." He argued that notes printed and circulated for thirty-five years have to be corrected in public, the way a car maker recalls a faulty part, and that pointing out the faulty parts was part of his assignment.

In October 1997 Dr. Frederick K.C. Price began teaching Race, Religion & Racism on his Ever Increasing Faith television program. He was the founder of Crenshaw Christian Center in Los Angeles, a Word of Faith teacher whose broadcast reached a large audience of every race, and he was preaching the series on Sundays at his own church while it aired. One subject he would not step around was a note in a study Bible. Finis Jennings Dake’s Annotated Reference Bible, first published in 1963, was the first widely used study Bible to come out of Pentecostalism, and for a generation it sat on the desks of pastors who prized its thousands of notes. In its notes on the Book of Acts, at Acts 17:26, Dake had printed a list titled "30 reasons for segregation of races." Dake also issued the list as a pamphlet. Price had already tested those reasons against Scripture one by one, and had shown that the verse Dake began with, which says Yahweh made all nations of one blood, is a statement of unity, not a charter for separation.

Then something happened that almost never happens. The people behind the book wrote to him. The letter came from the Dake family, faxed and dated February 18, 1998, and Price printed it in the ninth chapter of his second volume, under the heading When Will the Church Wake Up? Their words are worth hearing exactly, because they are the heart of this lesson. They wrote that they wished "to express our sincere regrets and apologies for any commentary in The Dake Annotated Reference Bible that has been interpreted as being supportive of slavery, racism, or discrimination." Many apologies stop there, at regret for how something was taken. This one did not. The family went on: "Yet we very clearly see how his thirty reasons for the separation of the nations can be interpreted as advocating racism, and we agree that racism has no place in the body of Christ, or anywhere else." And then: "Please accept this letter as a public statement from the entire Dake family that we ask for forgiveness." They enclosed a new edition of the Bible printed in January 1997 and said, "Therefore, we have labored to omit all racially insensitive references." They spoke warmly of Finis Dake himself, recalling that as a new convert he attended a Black church and was baptized there, and that he preached in Black churches across the country. Copies of the letter went to other well-known ministers, Creflo Dollar, Kenneth Copeland and Oral Roberts among them.

This is what a source that tells on itself looks like. A critic can always be dismissed as biased. But when the family that owns the book, with every reason to defend it, writes that its central list can be read as advocating racism, the question of whether the note carried that message is answered by the defense itself. The 1997 edition is a second witness of the same kind: no one rewrites words that were harmless.

The family made one request. Price had said in a sermon that radiation treatment, given improperly, can be as deadly as the cancer it is meant to cure. They took his picture and turned it: "the public airing of these problems could be just as dangerous as the evil of racism itself." They asked to meet with him and other leaders and resolve it privately.

Price answered on February 25, 1998. His reply opens with honest surprise: "In response to your faxed letter dated February 18, 1998, I must say, I was surprised, to say the least, to have received a letter from you." He forgave them at once. There were no conditions on the forgiveness; he wrote that he "would be the first to say to you, apologies accepted!" And he closed with the line that sums up his whole posture: "This is not personal; it’s principle." He invited them to come and meet: "If you think it would be beneficial to meet with me, you are cordially invited to do so."

But he would not stop teaching on it, and he explained why with a picture every driver understands. "The fact of the matter still stands, the notes are already printed and have been circulating for the past 35 years. It has to be fixed!" He saw two ways. "First, when automobile manufacturers — Ford, General Motors, etc. — find a flaw in their product, they do a recall at their expense. In other words, they fix or replace the faulty part. Are you willing to do that?" Thirty-five years of printed notes, he added, is a lot of parts. Second, "someone has to point out the faulty parts, so that people can avoid them. That is a part of my assignment with the series." He made clear this was not a grudge against one family. He wrote that he was not targeting Dake’s Bible as the only such publication, and that his notes on religion ran to hundreds of pages, from Dake’s Bible to the Mormons to Islam.

Then he did what an honest examiner does. He set the 1992 and 1997 wording of the list side by side so readers could see the changes for themselves. The title had become "30 reasons nations separated." In his bracketed notes he marks where "intermarriage" had replaced "miscegenation." And he observed that although the list now said separation instead of segregation, nineteen references using the word segregation remained in the same Bible’s concordance index. He did not tell the reader what to conclude. He asked them to judge whether the reasons had been sufficiently corrected. He also recorded, plainly and without bitterness, what came after his invitation: he had accepted their effort with thanks and offered to meet to discuss further changes, and "They have never met with me." His summary is balanced: "Their apology is accepted, their good intentions are noted. But what was done in the past needs to be uprooted."

What did he call his hearers to do? Two things, and they belong together. Forgive freely and honor a confession, because a confession is rare and right. And correct a public error in public, where it was printed and taught, because the people who read it deserve to know which part is faulty.

Weigh it by the Word. The Word honors confession, and it promises mercy to the one who confesses: "He that covereth his sins shall not prosper: but whoso confesseth and forsaketh them shall have mercy." (Proverbs 28:13) The Dake family did a thing that Scripture calls good, and Price answered it the way Scripture answers it, with mercy. That is grace, and it should be said plainly: their letter is to their credit.

But biblical repentance does not end in a feeling. When Zacchaeus met Jesus, he did not stop at being sorry; he went to the damage. "And Zacchaeus stood, and said unto the Lord; Behold, Lord, the half of my goods I give to the poor; and if I have taken any thing from any man by false accusation, I restore him fourfold." (Luke 19:8) Restitution to the people harmed is the Word’s own form of a recall. Price’s request that the old Bible be recalled and its fault acknowledged to the public is Zacchaeus applied to a printed note.

The Word also explains why a teacher’s note weighs so much: "My brethren, be not many masters, knowing that we shall receive the greater condemnation." (James 3:1) A commentator speaks to thousands at once and is trusted beside the text itself. And because a public error was taught in public, Scripture allows a public correction: "Them that sin rebuke before all, that others also may fear" (1 Timothy 5:20). Price did that with the door open, which honors the Lord’s word about reconciliation: "first be reconciled to thy brother" (Matthew 5:24). He offered the meeting; it was the other side that never came.

Finally, the Word puts a duty on every reader, not only on writers. Paul’s hearers in Berea were called noble because they checked even an apostle against the text: "These were more noble than those in Thessalonica, in that they received the word with all readiness of mind, and searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so." (Acts 17:11) The tragedy of the thirty reasons is that a generation trusted the margin over the verse. Price’s whole method was to send people back to the verse.

What does it mean for us now? Every family and every church has its margin notes: sayings from the dinner table, lessons from old Sunday school books, things we heard from trusted voices before we could test them. This lesson asks us to search the Scriptures daily, whether those things are so. When we find a faulty part, we name it in the place where it was taught. When someone admits a fault to us, we accept it as freely as Price did, without gloating. And when we are the ones who were wrong, we do what the Dake family began and what Zacchaeus finished: we confess it in writing if we must, and we fix it at our own expense.

TALK ABOUT IT TOGETHER. Parents, ask your children what the Dake family admitted in their own letter, and what forgiveness looked like, and listen to the end before you teach. Children, ask your parents or a grandparent what they remember. Friends, talk it over and share one wrong you will own in writing or in person.

## 9. Dr. Frederick K.C. Price — The only restriction: marriage, the believer line, and the acid test

*id: `pv-price-the-only-restriction` · 1543 words*

**The big idea.** Price argued that when two people marry, the Bible draws exactly one line between peoples: believer and unbeliever, never color. "If we adopt any other restriction than this, we are wrong." He said he was not advocating interracial marriage, only asking why it should make a difference, and he offered an acid test: ask a person how they would feel if their child married someone of another race. A Christian who is not a racist, he said, answers that as long as the two love each other and all things are equal, color does not matter.

To understand why Dr. Frederick K.C. Price spent so much of Race, Religion & Racism on marriage, you have to remember what set the series off. In 1992 a prominent white charismatic minister, a man Price knew well, preached a Sunday sermon on the home and family. On the tape that black ministers later brought to Price, the preacher told parents that if they did not want their children dating a certain group of people, they should control it long before their children reached dating age: we can be friends with everybody, he said, but we are not going to date this group. That sermon was heard by thousands. It told Price that some of the most respected teachers in the charismatic world still believed the Bible kept the races apart at the altar. When he began teaching the series on his Ever Increasing Faith broadcast in October 1997, he went straight to the question: where, exactly, does the Word draw the line?

He began by refusing to be misunderstood. "Again, I am not personally advocating interracial marriage. My only question is, why should it make a difference if a White marries a Black or if a Black marries a White or if anyone from any ethnic group intermarries with anyone of any other ethnic group?" He gave his own answer to his own question: it made a difference to many whites because they had been taught that Blacks were inferior. The issue, in other words, was not marriage at all. It was what people had been taught about the worth of other people. About his own household he was disarmingly plain: "I don’t care who my son marries." What he would counsel his son, he said, was that since the young man was saved and filled with the Holy Spirit, the woman he married ought to be saved and filled with the Spirit too.

That is the line. In the ninth chapter of his first volume, The Bible’s Acid Test, he states it as plainly as anything in the series: "Joining with another in marriage has nothing whatsoever to do with race; the only restriction is against joining Believers with unbelievers. If we adopt any other restriction than this, we are wrong; if we use any justification other than this for why two people cannot come together, then we are missing God." He built that on Paul’s words: "Be ye not unequally yoked together with unbelievers: for what fellowship hath righteousness with unrighteousness? and what communion hath light with darkness?" (2 Corinthians 6:14) And he showed that the verse cuts every way at once. In his second volume he spells it out: a white believer who marries a white unbeliever is unequally yoked; so is a red believer who marries a red unbeliever, and the same for black, yellow and brown believers. "It’s about Believers and unbelievers. Color does not matter to God because, out of one blood, God made all men."

Then he gave his hearers a tool. "Have you ever heard the term acid test? It is a test that jewelers use to find out whether something is gold and, if so, what degree of gold it is." Jewelers put nitric acid on the metal: real gold shows its color, while false gold bubbles up and turns green. His acid test was a single question: how do you feel about your child marrying someone of another race? "If people are not racists, they will say, “Fine! As long as the kids love each other and all things are equal, I don’t care.”" And then a sentence that shows his fairness: "You do not have to want to marry a person of color in order to not be a racist." He was not asking anyone to prefer anyone. He was asking them to look at what they believed about the worth of people. He asked white believers in particular to look within for a way of viewing other races that might have been handed down by parents and grandparents without their ever noticing it.

He went one step further, into territory few preachers would touch. "The underlying issue of race prejudice is sexual, and nobody wants to deal with that." In his second volume he calls the fear of interracial marriage between black and white people a primary motivation behind segregation, and the series description sums up his view that opposition to interracial marriage rests on "fear, ignorance, ethnic pride and a superiority based attitude." That is his reading of the history, and the record supplies its own evidence. Until 1967, sixteen states still made interracial marriage a crime. When Richard and Mildred Loving were convicted under Virginia’s Racial Integrity Act, the trial judge wrote in his ruling that "Almighty God created the races white, black, yellow, malay and red," and argued that because God had placed them on separate continents, He never meant them to mix. That is the same move Price exposed in the study Bibles: a human opinion about separation presented as the will of the Creator. On June 12, 1967, the Supreme Court struck down those laws without a single dissenting vote. Even then the teaching lingered. A well-known Christian university kept a ban on interracial dating until 2000, and in 2008 it declared itself "profoundly sorry" for having allowed "institutional policies to remain in place that were racially hurtful." When the institution that held the rule finally calls it hurtful, the record is telling on itself.

What did Price call his hearers to do? Hold the one line the Word draws, and only that line. Take the acid test privately and honestly. And stop using the Bible to build a wall it never built.

Weigh it by the Word. Price is right that Scripture guards marriage by faith, and the Old Testament says so in Yahweh’s own words. When He forbade Israel to intermarry with the nations of Canaan, He gave the reason: "Neither shalt thou make marriages with them; thy daughter thou shalt not give unto his son, nor his daughter shalt thou take unto thy son. For they will turn away thy son from following me, that they may serve other gods: so will the anger of the LORD be kindled against you, and destroy thee suddenly." (Deuteronomy 7:3-4) The danger named is that a spouse would turn the heart to other gods. Nothing in the command is about the color of anyone’s skin. The same is true when Ezra and Nehemiah confronted mixed marriages after the exile; the concern they name is the abominations and the strange gods those marriages brought into Israel.

Scripture also gives a test case that settles the matter. "And Miriam and Aaron spake against Moses because of the Ethiopian woman whom he had married: for he had married an Ethiopian woman." (Numbers 12:1) Moses married a Cushite woman, a woman from the land the Bible calls Ethiopia. His own sister and brother spoke against him for it. Yahweh did not rebuke Moses. Instead, "And the anger of the LORD was kindled against them; and he departed." (Numbers 12:9) If marriage across the lines of peoples were sin, this was the place for Yahweh to say so. He said the opposite, by His anger against the critics.

Ruth is a second witness. She was a Moabitess who chose Naomi’s people and Naomi’s God: "thy people shall be my people, and thy God my God" (Ruth 1:16). She married Boaz and became the great-grandmother of David, and her name stands in the genealogy of Jesus. Her faith, not her nation, decided her place.

One point needs careful framing. When Price says the only restriction is believer and unbeliever, he is answering the question of race and peoples. Scripture does guard marriage in other ways, for instance against marrying close kin, and Price is not denying that. On the question he was asked, his answer is exactly the Word’s answer.

Under it all is the verse Price kept returning to: Yahweh "hath made of one blood all nations of men" (Acts 17:26). And the verse that explains why the acid test works: "man looketh on the outward appearance, but the LORD looketh on the heart" (1 Samuel 16:7).

What does this mean for us now? For parents, it means guiding our children toward a spouse who loves the Lord, and letting go of every other wall. For young people, it means asking the Word’s question first: does this person belong to Jesus? For all of us, it means taking the acid test with honesty and mercy toward ourselves, because many of us were handed fears we did not choose. Yahweh, who rebuked Miriam and Aaron, also healed Miriam and brought her back into the camp. He is still correcting His people and restoring them. That is the grace in this lesson: the line He draws is narrow and clear, and everything outside it is room for love.

TALK ABOUT IT TOGETHER. Parents, ask your children what the one line is that the Bible draws for marriage, and listen to the end before you teach. Children, ask your parents or a grandparent what they remember. Friends, talk it over and tell each other what matters most in choosing a husband or wife.

## 10. Dr. Frederick K.C. Price — When religion perverts the Gospel: slavery and the Church

*id: `pv-price-religion-and-slavery` · 1505 words*

**The big idea.** Price charged that "Religion has been the most flagrant perpetrator of racism in the world, and the Christian Church in America has been the leading perpetrator," and he drew a sharp line between religion, which he called the word of man about the Word of God, and the Word itself. Slavery lasted 246 years in America, he argued, because preachers got into league with slave owners and the Church as a whole never committed itself to ending it. His call: stop hiding behind silence, because neutrality is not an option, and let the Church repent where it sanctioned the sin.

Of all the charges in Race, Religion & Racism, this is the one that has driven the most people away from the Church, and Dr. Frederick K.C. Price faced it without flinching. How could churches that read the Bible have blessed the buying and selling of human beings for two and a half centuries? Many Black Americans, then and now, concluded that Christianity must be the white man’s religion. Price, a Black pastor who loved the Word and had given his life to teaching it, took up the question in both volumes. The title of the second volume is itself his thesis: Perverting the Gospel to Subjugate a People. Its first chapter is called The White Distortion: Interweaving Racism With Religion.

He opens with a sentence built to make the reader sit up: "Religion has been the most flagrant perpetrator of racism in the world, and the Christian Church in America has been the leading perpetrator." He knew how that would sound to believers, so he asked the question himself. "At first glance, to people who know God, this may seem paradoxical." How can religion, which we associate with the Word of God, be the perpetrator of something as evil as racism? "The answer is that religion is not necessarily the Word of God; religion is very often the word of man about the Word of God, and that has been the problem." Then he made sure no one could misread his target: "I’m not talking about the enlightened Word of God. I’m talking about how men have distorted the Word of God to foster their own racist attitudes, beliefs and practices."

In his first volume he lays out the history step by step. Slavery, he argues, began to take hold when the preachers "got into league with the slave owners, and the Church sanctioned slavery. That is the reason slavery could exist — because the Church gave its approval for slave owners to practice slavery and call themselves Christians." He draws the consequence plainly: that approval is a major reason why many people of color have called Christianity the slave owners’ religion and have wanted nothing to do with it. He is careful to be fair: "There were, of course, genuine Christians who spoke out against slavery. But slavery persisted in America for 246 years, because the Church as a whole never committed itself to a positive program to end it." His footnote gives the span: "Two hundred and forty-six years encompasses the arrival of the first Africans in America in 1619 through the end of the Civil War in 1865."

The records of the time confirm his dates in their own words. In August 1619 a privateer landed captive Africans at Point Comfort, Virginia, and the colony’s secretary, John Rolfe, wrote to the Virginia Company that the ship brought "not any thing but 20. and odd Negroes, which the Governor and Cape Marchant bought for victualls." Human beings were traded for food, and the colonial secretary recorded it as an ordinary transaction. Slavery ended in law when the Thirteenth Amendment was proclaimed adopted as of December 6, 1865. Between those two documents lie the 246 years Price counts.

Price then shows how the theology was built to fit the trade. He walks through the interpretations that let slaveholders, ministers and even founders of the nation claim to be Christians and still justify slavery, the so-called curse of Ham among them, and he concludes: "This is the kind of garbage our theology has been built on, and unfortunately this kind of thinking is still alive in our country today." He also names the strategy that kept enslaved people from resisting. Slaveholders set them against one another, house against field: "If you can keep people scattered and fragmented, they can never join together, act as one and defend their rights; that way you can control them better." He argues that the habits of that division still live among us and must be named before they can be healed.

The record tells on itself here too, in ways that Price’s argument predicts. In 1807 a Bible was printed in London for the enslaved people of the British West Indies under the title Select Parts of the Holy Bible for the use of the Negro Slaves. Its editors removed most of the Old Testament and about half of the New. They cut the Exodus. They cut the law against stealing a man. They cut the verse that says in Christ there is neither bond nor free. They kept the verses about servants obeying masters. No one edits out a verse that agrees with him. The scissors themselves are a confession of what the full Word said. And in America the Southern Baptist Convention was organized in 1845 by Baptists who supported slavery. In 1995, at its one hundred fiftieth anniversary, it adopted a resolution renouncing those roots and apologizing for its defense of slavery and segregation. Price wrote of such confessions: "These confessions of racism are laudable, and it is significant that finally some church leaders are accepting responsibility and are open to change." That is grace. He did not mock the apology; he welcomed it.

What did he call his hearers to do? His sharpest word was for good people who said nothing. Citing a white Christian writer, Maurice I. Irvin, whose article Grace and Race says the parable of the Good Samaritan teaches that neutrality is not an option, Price added his own verdict: "Even though the majority of white people are not racist — and thank God they’re not — the problem is their silence." He asked the Church to repent where it had sanctioned the sin, to stop passing by on the other side, and to let the Word, not religion, speak.

Weigh it by the Word. Price’s central distinction, between the Word and men’s religion about the Word, is the distinction Jesus Himself made with the religious leaders of His day: "Making the word of God of none effect through your tradition, which ye have delivered: and many such like things do ye." (Mark 7:13) The Word was never silent on what the slave trade was. The Law of Moses said: "And he that stealeth a man, and selleth him, or if he be found in his hand, he shall surely be put to death." (Exodus 21:16) The Atlantic trade was man-stealing on a vast scale, and the Law named it a capital crime. Paul, writing to Timothy, lists the lawless and ungodly, and among them stand "menstealers" (1 Timothy 1:10). The gospel goes further still: "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither bond nor free, there is neither male nor female: for ye are all one in Christ Jesus." (Galatians 3:28) When Paul sent the runaway Onesimus back to Philemon, he asked that he be received "Not now as a servant, but above a servant, a brother beloved" (Philemon 1:16). The slaveholders’ religion had to cut or ignore every one of these. Price’s charge that men distorted the Word stands on the Word itself.

His charge against silence stands on Jesus’ own story. The priest and the Levite did no violence to the wounded man; they simply passed by. Jesus asked who proved to be the neighbor, and the lawyer answered, the one who showed mercy. "Then said Jesus unto him, Go, and do thou likewise" (Luke 10:37). James puts it in one line: "Therefore to him that knoweth to do good, and doeth it not, to him it is sin." (James 4:17) And Yahweh’s description of the fast He chooses is the program Price said the Church never committed to: "to loose the bands of wickedness, to undo the heavy burdens, and to let the oppressed go free" (Isaiah 58:6).

One caution keeps this lesson honest. Price’s phrase about religion being the most flagrant perpetrator in the world is his charge about religion as men twist it; he does not say the Word is guilty, and he names the genuine Christians who opposed slavery. The record bears out both halves: many churches blessed slavery, and many believers, Black and white, fought it from the Word.

What does it mean for us now? It means we never hand the Bible over to those who abused it. The very Book they edited is the Book that condemned them, and our ancestors knew it; they found Moses and Pharaoh in it and sang about them in songs no master could cut. It means we read the whole Word, especially the parts someone once tried to hide. It means we forgive those who confess, as Price did. And it means none of us may pass by on the other side.

TALK ABOUT IT TOGETHER. Parents, ask your children how religion can bend the Gospel, and how the Word corrects it, and listen to the end before you teach. Children, ask your parents or a grandparent what they remember. Friends, talk it over and name one tradition you will test by the Word.

## 11. Dr. Frederick K.C. Price — "I need a real Jesus": the women in His genealogy

*id: `pv-price-the-color-of-jesus` · 1533 words*

**The big idea.** Price observed that most pictures of Jesus in American Bible bookstores showed a white man with light hair, and most in Black bookstores showed a Black man. "I don’t need a black Jesus and I don’t need a white Jesus; I need a real Jesus." He argued that the idea of a white Jesus fed the lie of white supremacy, and he pointed to the genealogy in Matthew 1, where Yahweh named Rahab, a Canaanite, in the line of His Son: "Jesus has some of Ham in Him." The Word, he said, shows a Savior whose human family reaches across the nations.

In many American churches you may still find Him on the wall: a gentle man with fair skin, long light-brown hair, and blue eyes, holding a lamb or knocking at a door. For generations that image hung in white churches and Black churches alike, in family Bibles and on funeral programs. In Race, Religion & Racism, Dr. Frederick K.C. Price looked hard at that picture and asked what the Word actually says. The question was in the series from the start; its own description asks, "Was Jesus white or was He a composite man made up of the entire human race?"

Price began with what anyone could see. In the first volume, in a chapter titled In God’s Image, he wrote, "In most Bible bookstores in the United States — I don’t know about Bible bookstores in foreign countries because I haven’t had the opportunity to go to them — almost all the pictures of Jesus are of a white man with light-colored hair and blue or brown eyes." He did not spare his own community: "At black bookstores, you will find the other extreme: Almost all the pictures of Jesus are of a black man." Then came the line that frames this lesson: "I don’t need a black Jesus and I don’t need a white Jesus; I need a real Jesus. I don’t care if He had any color. But whatever color He was, why don’t we just tell the truth about it?" In a footnote he promised to trace the genealogy of Jesus further, and his third volume, Jesus, Christianity & Islam, published in 1999, carries a chapter titled The Color of Jesus.

Why did it matter? Price argued that the white Jesus on the wall was part of a much larger lie. White supremacy, he wrote, "stems from the concept that God is white, Adam was white, Abraham was white, Jesus was white and everyone else of any significance in the Bible was white." He quoted the old saying that summed it up, "White is right, and anything that is not white is not quite right," and he drew the consequence: "This lie gave the justification to enslave and put down people who were not white." If every holy face in the Bible was painted white, then a people could be taught, without a word spoken, that holiness had a color. Price called that antithetical to what the Bible tells us.

So he went to the first page of the New Testament. In the second volume, in a chapter titled A Revelation From God, he walked his readers through Matthew’s genealogy. It is a list of fathers, generation after generation, with five exceptions: five women named on purpose. Price stopped at one of them. "There it is: Rahab, a black woman, is part of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the Son of God." Rahab was the woman of Jericho who hid Israel’s spies and was spared with her household. She was a Canaanite, and the table of nations makes Canaan a son of Ham. Price drew the line straight: "All of us, of every race, have been lied to. Jesus has some of Ham in Him, because Rahab was a Canaanite. And very possibly Tamar, who is also in the ancestry of Jesus Christ, was also a Canaanite." He was astonished that the Church had read past it for centuries: "it’s absolutely astounding to me how the Church has walked past the revelation with closed eyes and refused to see it." And he saw design in it: "Not only did God make this choice, He went so far as to show everyone who reads the Bible that His own Son had some racial mixture in Him." He added a further possibility about Bathsheba: "Because Bathsheba’s husband was a Hittite, it is very possible that she was a Hittite."

What did he call his hearers to do? Tell the truth about Jesus. Stop painting holiness in one color. And see in His family line Yahweh’s own announcement that the nations were in the plan from the beginning.

Weigh it by the Word, and the Word explains the Word. Matthew opens: "The book of the generation of Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham." (Matthew 1:1) The line runs through Abraham and David, and Matthew deliberately names the women. "And Judas begat Phares and Zara of Thamar; and Phares begat Esrom; and Esrom begat Aram;" (Matthew 1:3) "And Salmon begat Booz of Rachab; and Booz begat Obed of Ruth; and Obed begat Jesse;" (Matthew 1:5) "And Jesse begat David the king; and David the king begat Solomon of her that had been the wife of Urias;" (Matthew 1:6) Rahab’s story is told in Joshua: "And Joshua the son of Nun sent out of Shittim two men to spy secretly, saying, Go view the land, even Jericho. And they went, and came into an harlot’s house, named Rahab, and lodged there." (Joshua 2:1) When Jericho fell, "And Joshua saved Rahab the harlot alive, and her father’s household, and all that she had; and she dwelleth in Israel even unto this day; because she hid the messengers, which Joshua sent to spy out Jericho." (Joshua 6:25) The New Testament honors her faith: "By faith the harlot Rahab perished not with them that believed not, when she had received the spies with peace." (Hebrews 11:31) And the table of nations places her people in Ham’s line: "And the sons of Ham; Cush, and Mizraim, and Phut, and Canaan." (Genesis 10:6) Price is right on the main point. A Canaanite woman and a Moabite woman, Ruth, stand by name in the human ancestry of the Messiah, and Matthew put them there on purpose.

Where Price goes beyond the text, the Word itself sets the limit, and he was honest enough to signal it. He calls Rahab a black woman; the Word names her nation and her faith, not her color. He says very possibly about Tamar, and Genesis 38 does not tell us her people, although it does say that Judah had married the daughter of a Canaanite. He says very possible about Bathsheba; the Word names her father as Eliam, "Bathsheba, the daughter of Eliam, the wife of Uriah the Hittite" (2 Samuel 11:3). The one other Eliam in David’s story is the son of Ahithophel the Gilonite, David’s counselor from Giloh in Judah. If that is her father, she came from an Israelite family. Her husband Uriah was a Hittite, and the Hittites descend from Heth, a son of Canaan: "And Canaan begat Sidon his first born, and Heth," (Genesis 10:15) These are honest limits. They do not touch the main point; they only keep us from saying more than the Word says.

And on the question of His appearance, the Word is deliberately silent. No apostle described His face, hair or skin. Historians agree that He was a Jewish man of first-century Galilee, a man of the Middle East. The paintings on our walls came from later centuries and the artists’ own peoples. The prophet even told us that His looks would not be the reason anyone came to Him: "For he shall grow up before him as a tender plant, and as a root out of a dry ground: he hath no form nor comeliness; and when we shall see him, there is no beauty that we should desire him." (Isaiah 53:2) That silence is not an accident; it is protection. If Scripture had painted Him, some people would have claimed Him by color. Instead the Word gives us His lineage and His mission. "God sent forth his Son, made of a woman" (Galatians 4:4). "And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us" (John 1:14). And the song of heaven tells us whom He came for: "And they sung a new song, saying, Thou art worthy to take the book, and to open the seals thereof: for thou wast slain, and hast redeemed us to God by thy blood out of every kindred, and tongue, and people, and nation;" (Revelation 5:9)

What does it mean for us now? It means no people owns Jesus, and no people is shut out from Him. Price’s line frees everyone at once. The child who grew up wondering whether a blue-eyed Jesus loved him less can see Rahab and Ruth in the genealogy and know that the nations were in the family from the start. The believer who has never questioned the picture can learn to hold it loosely and tell the truth. And all of us can do what Price asked: stop painting the Son of Yahweh to look like ourselves, and come to know the real Jesus, the Son of David and Son of Abraham, who took flesh from a family of many nations and gave His blood for every kindred and tongue.

TALK ABOUT IT TOGETHER. Parents, ask your children why Dr. Price said he needed a real Jesus, and who the women in His family line were, and listen to the end before you teach. Children, ask your parents or a grandparent what they remember. Friends, talk it over and read the names in Matthew 1 together.

## 12. Dr. Frederick K.C. Price — "This is not the white man’s religion": answering the poison-book charge

*id: `pv-price-not-the-white-mans-religion` · 1486 words*

**The big idea.** Price took up the charge, made by Elijah Muhammad of the Nation of Islam, that the Bible is a poison book that had poisoned Black people. He admitted that the Church’s own track record opened the door for it, and then he answered it: the poison comes from people who twisted the Book, not from the Book. If a criminal uses a Ford in a getaway, you do not shut down the Ford Motor Company. "Read the Bible right and use it right!" And he declared, "This is not the white man’s religion; that is the lie that has been disseminated through the centuries. This is God’s Word for everyone who is willing to receive it."

For much of the twentieth century, one charge pulled more Black Americans away from the Church than any other: Christianity is the white man’s religion, and the Bible is the slave master’s book. The Nation of Islam made that charge with special force. Its leader, Elijah Muhammad, led the movement from 1933 until his death in 1975, and his message reached many young people in Black neighborhoods, including some who had grown up in church. Dr. Frederick K.C. Price wrote that many Black people had left the Church over statements that made Christianity look like the white man’s religion. In Race, Religion & Racism he faced the charge head on, and the series itself put the questions bluntly: "Is Islam the black man’s religion? Whose report will you believe?" He later devoted a whole third volume, Jesus, Christianity & Islam, published in 1999, to Islam and Christianity, with chapters titled What Does Islam Say? and Slavery Islam and Christianity. But the heart of his answer is already in the first two volumes, and it is that answer this lesson teaches.

He began by quoting the charge in one of its strongest forms, and he named its author. In Message to the Black Man in America, published in Chicago in 1965, Elijah Muhammad wrote, "The Bible is now being called the Poison Book." He went on: "It has poisoned the very hearts and minds of the so-called Negroes so much that they can’t agree with each other." He blamed the white race for tampering with the Scripture from the first day they received it, to make it suit themselves and blind the Black man, and he concluded that the Book could not be recognized as the pure Word of God. Those are Elijah Muhammad’s words, not Price’s, and they condemn a whole race in a way the Word never allows.

Price’s first move was not a counterattack. It was a confession. "This is someone on the outside looking at the track record of the Church. And the track record of the Church opened the door for this." He knew the record well. In the same volume he examined a 1907 book by William Montgomery Brown, the Episcopal Bishop of Arkansas from 1898 to 1912, titled The Crucial Race Question, or Where and How Shall the Color Line Be Drawn? Brown wrote of his church as the "Church of the Anglo-Saxon race." Price called that attitude "totally un-Christian," and he connected the dots: "This is the reason so many black people have left so-called Christianity and Christian churches, because of statements like this, which, in essence, say that Christianity is the white man’s religion." A bishop had said with his own pen what the Nation of Islam later said about the Church. The record told on itself.

Then Price drew the decisive line between the Book and those who abused it. "But white people have not changed the Scriptures. The Bible is just as it was when it came from the original writers. The Scriptures say the same thing that they have always said. There’s not a thing in the world wrong with the Bible. Contrary to what Elijah Muhammad writes, the Bible is not a poison book. The poison comes from the pens of some of those who attempt to interpret it." He put it more simply still: "The poison comes from people, not from the Book." And then he gave the illustration that has stayed with his hearers: "Just because someone drives up in a Ford, robs a bank, kills everyone and drives off with the money doesn’t mean there’s something wrong with Fords. You don’t get rid of the whole Ford Motor Company just because some criminals used a Ford in a getaway. So don’t throw away the Bible and Christianity just because some people have misused it. Read the Bible right and use it right!"

His call to action is in that last line, and in the words with which he closed his first volume. "I love God, I love the Church I am part of, I love people of all nations." And: "I love my people, and I attempted for years to give them the Word. Many of them got it, and it has changed their lives. This is not the white man’s religion; that is the lie that has been disseminated through the centuries. This is God’s Word for everyone who is willing to receive it."

Weigh it by the Word, and by the record. Price is right that the Scriptures have not been changed to suit any race. Yahweh’s own promise stands behind them: "The grass withereth, the flower fadeth: but the word of our God shall stand for ever." (Isaiah 40:8) And "All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness:" (2 Timothy 3:16) The poison, as Price said, was in the interpreters who cut, skipped, and twisted, the same men the Law and the prophets condemned.

The record of the early Church also answers the charge that this is the white man’s religion. The gospel reached Africa in its very first generation. "And he arose and went: and, behold, a man of Ethiopia, an eunuch of great authority under Candace queen of the Ethiopians, who had the charge of all her treasure, and had come to Jerusalem for to worship," (Acts 8:27) That Ethiopian official read Isaiah in his chariot, heard Philip preach Jesus, and was baptized. At Pentecost there were Jews and converts from "Egypt, and in the parts of Libya about Cyrene" (Acts 2:10). The church at Antioch, where believers were first called Christians, had among its prophets and teachers "Simeon that was called Niger, and Lucius of Cyrene" (Acts 13:1). In the fourth century, Ezana, king of Aksum in the Horn of Africa, embraced Christianity, more than a thousand years before European ships began carrying captives from West Africa. The faith was African before it was ever English.

The record also shows that the sin of enslaving Africans did not run along the line of one religion. Historians estimate that between ten and eighteen million Africans were carried across the Sahara as slaves after the Muslim conquest, from the seventh century into the twentieth. This is said not to accuse our Muslim neighbors, who bear no guilt for what was done centuries ago, but to show that no religion of men has clean hands. Price’s answer was never to trade one religion for another. It was to come to Jesus Himself. "Jesus saith unto him, I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me." (John 14:6) "Neither is there salvation in any other: for there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved." (Acts 4:12)

One more word must be said plainly, because grace requires it. This lesson gives no license for contempt toward Muslims or toward any people. Elijah Muhammad’s son and successor, Warith Deen Mohammed, disbanded the Nation of Islam in 1976 and turned it toward mainstream Islam; the poison-book words belong to Elijah Muhammad, not to every Muslim. Many Muslim neighbors are devout, generous, and honest. Yahweh’s heart toward them is shown in the wilderness, where Hagar’s son lay crying: "God heard the voice of the lad" (Genesis 21:17). His heart toward the whole world is "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life." (John 3:16) Our task is the one Peter gave: "But sanctify the Lord God in your hearts: and be ready always to give an answer to every man that asketh you a reason of the hope that is in you with meekness and fear:" (1 Peter 3:15)

What does it mean for us now? It means we tell the truth about the Church’s record, without excuses, the way Price did. It means we refuse to surrender the Bible to those who abused it, because the Book itself condemned them. It means we read the Bible right and use it right, especially with the young people who are hearing the old charge in new forms online. And it means we love our Muslim neighbors, pray for them by name, and share the hope that is in us with gentleness and respect. Jesus is not the white man’s Savior or the Black man’s Savior. He is the Son of Yahweh, and the way, the truth, and the life for everyone who is willing to receive Him.

## 13. Dr. Frederick K.C. Price — Not reconciliation but recognition: public sin, public correction, love throughout

*id: `pv-price-recognition` · 1582 words*

**The big idea.** Price questioned the popular word reconciliation. To reconcile, by the dictionary, is to restore a friendship, and Black and white Americans, in the nation and in the Church, had never been accepted as equals or friends. "Only equals can be reconciled." The first step, he said, is not reconciliation but recognition: the white church accepting once and for all that Acts 17:26 means what it says. And because racism had been practiced in public, it had to be confronted in public, as Paul confronted Peter at Antioch (Galatians 2:11-14), always with the door open and with love as the motive.

By the late 1990s, racial reconciliation had become one of the most common phrases in American church life. There were reconciliation rallies, reconciliation statements, and reconciliation Sundays, and many of them were sincere. Dr. Frederick K.C. Price had spent years teaching that racism in the Church was sin, and he had personally lived the cost of saying so: a ministry he had been close to for years had apologized privately but would not publicly recant a sermon he could not ignore, and their fellowship had paused while he waited. So when he reached the eleventh chapter of his second volume, he did something few of his peers would have dared. He titled it The Necessity of Recognition, and he questioned the word everyone was using.

He began with the dictionary, the way a careful teacher does. Webster’s New International Dictionary, as he quoted it, gives the first meaning of reconcile as "To cause to be friendly again; to restore to friendship; to bring back to harmony." Price read that and stopped: "This definition pinpoints why I disagree with the idea of reconciliation between Blacks and Whites." The problem was in the little words, again and restore and back. Reconciliation assumes a friendship that once existed and was broken. And Webster’s, as he quoted it, defines a friend as "One who entertains for another such sentiments of esteem, respect, and affection that he seeks his society and welfare." Price held that definition up against American history and answered with one line: "Esteem? Respect? Affection? Jim Crow was how Whites respected us."

From there he made his central claim. "Only equals can be reconciled. You cannot reconcile a dog and a man. They are not equals. And black people in America and in the Church have never been accepted as equals by the society as a whole and by the Church as a whole. We have been accepted as inferiors. And you cannot reconcile an equal with an inferior." The picture is harsh, and he meant it to be; it describes how Black people had been treated, not what they are. His conclusion follows: "We cannot reconcile because we have never been equals, we have never been one, we have never been friends." And so he proposed a different first step: "The first step toward a good relationship between Blacks and Whites is not reconciliation, but recognition." He wanted the white church to accept once and for all what Acts 17:26 says, that Yahweh made all nations of one blood, and to treat Black believers accordingly, as full brothers and full equals.

He did not dismiss those working for reconciliation. In the same chapter he quoted Chris Rice, a white writer on the subject, who wrote that "Nothing will give reconciliation a surer foothold in the Church than one-to-one friendships of genuine trust formed across racial lines, especially between leaders." Price welcomed that kind of friendship. His insistence was on the order of things: recognition first, because friendship cannot stand on a foundation of inequality.

He also explained why he had taken all of this public, and here he went to the Word. In his first volume he walks through Galatians 2. Peter had eaten freely with Gentile believers at Antioch. When certain men came from James, he withdrew and separated himself, fearing those of the circumcision; the other Jewish believers followed him, and even Barnabas was carried away. Paul confronted him to his face, before everyone. Price drew the principle: "Because Peter’s acts of hypocrisy and prejudice were done publicly, and therefore needed to be dealt with in public." That, he said, was why he was exposing "the minister’s racism publicly, the Church’s racism publicly, society’s racism publicly, because that is where racism has been practiced." Racism "has been talked about privately and planned privately, but executed in the public arena. And that is where it must be challenged if it is to be fixed."

What did he call his hearers to do? Name racism as sin: "We have seen that racism is a sin and must be repented of and stopped." Confront public wrong in public. Ask those who committed the wrong to admit it, repent of it, and make it right. Begin every relationship with recognition. And do it all in love. He closed his first volume in exactly that spirit, ready "to respond positively whenever the minister is ready to recant and repent of what he said on the tape," and adding: "I yearn to see our ministries once again united, as I yearn to see racism eliminated from the Body of Christ and for everyone of every race to live with the knowledge of what God has told us: that we are all of one flesh and one blood, and that inside the dust of our flesh bodies we are all spirits created in the image of God."

Weigh it by the Word. Price’s use of Galatians 2 is exactly right. "But when Peter was come to Antioch, I withstood him to the face, because he was to be blamed." (Galatians 2:11) "For before that certain came from James, he did eat with the Gentiles: but when they were come, he withdrew and separated himself, fearing them which were of the circumcision." (Galatians 2:12) "But when I saw that they walked not uprightly according to the truth of the gospel, I said unto Peter before them all, If thou, being a Jew, livest after the manner of Gentiles, and not as do the Jews, why compellest thou the Gentiles to live as do the Jews?" (Galatians 2:14) Peter’s sin was not a private opinion; it was a public act of separation at the table, and it threatened the truth of the gospel. Paul corrected it in public. Elsewhere Paul gives the same rule: "Them that sin rebuke before all, that others also may fear." (1 Timothy 5:20) And Price had first gone privately to the minister, as Jesus teaches in Matthew 18:15, before he spoke in public.

Price is also right that the Word refuses a peace that skips the truth. Jesus said, "Therefore if thou bring thy gift to the altar, and there rememberest that thy brother hath ought against thee; Leave there thy gift before the altar, and go thy way; first be reconciled to thy brother, and then come and offer thy gift." (Matthew 5:23-24) Reconciliation in His teaching begins with facing the wrong. The prophet asks, "Can two walk together, except they be agreed?" (Amos 3:3) Recognition is the agreement Amos is talking about: agreement on who we are, one blood, equal before Yahweh.

But here the Word also carries us beyond Price’s slogan, and it should be said plainly and kindly. In Scripture, reconciliation is the very word for what Yahweh did with people who were not His equals, and who were His enemies. "For if, when we were enemies, we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son, much more, being reconciled, we shall be saved by his life." (Romans 5:10) "And all things are of God, who hath reconciled us to himself by Jesus Christ, and hath given to us the ministry of reconciliation;" (2 Corinthians 5:18) If only equals could be reconciled, none of us would be saved. The Cross reconciles the unequal, and it does so by making them new. Paul says Christ "For he is our peace, who hath made both one, and hath broken down the middle wall of partition between us;" (Ephesians 2:14) He did it "for to make in himself of twain one new man, so making peace" (Ephesians 2:15), "And that he might reconcile both unto God in one body by the cross, having slain the enmity thereby:" (Ephesians 2:16) So Price’s recognition is not a substitute for biblical reconciliation. It is its first step, the moment when the wall is admitted and torn down and the other is seen as a full brother. The Cross is what makes the rest of the road possible. Read that way, Price and Paul stand together: no cheap peace, no pretended friendship, and no despair, because the One who reconciled enemies to Himself can make brothers out of strangers.

What does it mean for us now? It means our unity services must carry the truth or they are only theater. It means public wrongs, including the ones in our church histories, are confessed in public, as Peter’s was corrected before all. It means every believer is recognized, out loud and in practice, as an equal. It means the friendships Chris Rice described, one by one and leader by leader, are built on that recognition. And it means we do all of it the way Price did, in love: "By this shall all men know that ye are my disciples, if ye have love one to another." (John 13:35) Price asked once whether heaven would be segregated. The Word answers him: "a great multitude, which no man could number, of all nations, and kindreds, and people, and tongues, stood before the throne, and before the Lamb" (Revelation 7:9). Recognition now is simply learning to see each other the way we will stand together then.

TALK ABOUT IT TOGETHER. Parents, ask your children why Dr. Price asked for recognition and not only reconciliation, and listen to the end before you teach. Children, ask your parents or a grandparent what they remember. Friends, talk it over and tell each other one way you will honor someone as an equal.
