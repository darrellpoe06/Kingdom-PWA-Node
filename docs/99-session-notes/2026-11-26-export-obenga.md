## 1. Dr. Théophile Obenga — the tongue that carried the truth

*id: `pv-obenga` · 1873 words*

**The big idea.** Dr. Théophile Obenga, born in Brazzaville in 1936 and trained in philosophy, history, linguistics and Egyptology in France, Switzerland and the United States, carried Cheikh Anta Diop’s case into the study of language. At the UNESCO symposium in Cairo in 1974 he argued, by the comparative method of regular sound-laws, that ancient Egyptian and Coptic share one origin with the living languages of Black Africa, and he spent the next half century building that case into what he named the Negro-Egyptian family. His message is that a people’s speech keeps a record no one can fully erase, and that the record must be read by rule, not by slogan.

Dr. Théophile Obenga was born in 1936 in Brazzaville, on the north bank of the Congo River, in what is now the Republic of the Congo. He grew up in a century when much of the world’s scholarship told Africa’s story from outside, and, as UNESCO’s own record would later admit, wrote the history of the Nile as if a civilized Egypt stood apart from a south treated as black and primitive. Egypt, whose writing is among the earliest known on earth, was explained as if it belonged somewhere other than Africa. Obenga spent his life testing that story against the evidence, and the instrument he chose was language. The opening of this lesson is simply who he is; the three lessons after it let him speak.

His training was wide on purpose. According to the published summary of his career, he earned a master’s degree in philosophy at the University of Bordeaux, a master’s in education at the University of Pittsburgh in the United States, and a master’s in history at the Sorbonne in Paris. He did advanced study in history, linguistics and Egyptology at the University of Geneva, in prehistory at the Institut de Paléontologie Humaine in Paris, and in linguistics, philology and Egyptology at the Sorbonne and the Collège de France. He holds a doctorate in letters, arts and humanities from the University of Montpellier. He joined the French society of Egyptologists, contributed to UNESCO’s General History of Africa, led the International Centre for Bantu Civilizations in Libreville, Gabon, until the end of 1991, edited the journal Ankh, and taught in the Africana Studies program at San Francisco State University, where he is professor emeritus. His books, more than two dozen of them, run from history and language to philosophy, geometry and poetry. He is a scholar of many rooms, and he walked into each one carrying the same question: what does the evidence of Africa itself say about Africa?

The setting that made him known to the world was a meeting in Cairo. From 28 January to 3 February 1974, UNESCO gathered twenty specialists in Egypt for a symposium on the peopling of ancient Egypt and the deciphering of the Meroitic script. It was held because Cheikh Anta Diop had asked for it before the volume on ancient Africa was written. The participant list printed in UNESCO’s own volume names "Professor Th. Obenga (People’s Republic of the Congo)." He was in his late thirties. He and Diop came to defend the thesis that Egypt was an African civilization in its people and its culture, and they came prepared.

Why did that matter so much? Because the history of the Nile had been written for a century and more on an assumption, and UNESCO’s own record tells on itself here. Summarising the paper of Ms Blanc, one of the specialists, the report of the discussions says that the history of the Nile valleys "had been based on the assumption that there was a civilized Egyptian valley providing a wealth of historical evidence, and another valley further south, which was black and primitive, and of interest only to anthropologists." That sentence is not Obenga’s complaint. It is the symposium describing the habit of its own field. Obenga’s work was aimed at the root of that habit, and he went after it with the tool that is hardest to fake: the grammar and sound of living speech.

His message can be heard in three movements, and the next three lessons take them one at a time.

The first movement is his Cairo paper, which he titled The genetic linguistic relationship between Egyptian (ancient Egyptian and Coptic) and modern Negro-African languages. In it he states his task plainly: "Our problem, therefore, consists in establishing the existence of a genetic linguistic relationship between Egyptian (ancient Egyptian and Coptic) and modern Negro-African languages." He insists that the question be settled by rule, not by looks. He separates languages that merely look alike in structure from languages that truly descend from one parent, and he gives the test he will accept: "Genetic linguistic relationship is established on the basis of ‘sound-laws’, that is to say enduring correspondences and regular similarities between complete forms, morphemes, and phonemes in the language being compared." Then he lays out his evidence, series after series, from the way ancient Egyptian and Wolof and Kongo build abstract words like goodness and badness, to the verb to be, to the way they say no. He ends the paper in one line: "Conclusion: a single linguistic type, the same formal and grammatical structure."

The second movement is what the room did with it. The same UNESCO report that records sharp disagreement on skeletons and on the colours in Egyptian painting records something different on language: "This item, in contrast to those previously discussed, revealed a large measure of agreement among the participants. The outline report by Professor Diop and the report by Professor Obenga were regarded as being very constructive." Professor S. Sauneron of France, no ally of every claim Diop made, said that Egyptian "could not be isolated from its African context." And the rapporteur, closing the report, said that not all the participants had prepared work "comparable with the painstakingly researched contributions of Professors Cheikh Anta Diop and Obenga." The record also keeps the other side, and we keep it too: a scholar from Sudan, Professor Abdalla, argued that the likenesses were accidental, and Sauneron named the hard problem of the five thousand years between ancient Egyptian and the languages spoken today.

The third movement is the rest of his working life. Obenga took the Cairo argument and built it into a proposed family he called Negro-Egyptian, in books such as Origine commune de l’égyptien ancien, du copte et des langues négro-africaines modernes (1993) and L’égyptien pharaonique: une langue négro-africaine (2010). He also wrote on African philosophy, beginning with Egypt. That proposed family is where the honest line has to be drawn: mainstream linguistics today classifies Egyptian as a branch of the Afroasiatic family and does not accept a Negro-Egyptian family. We will say that plainly in its place, and we will also say plainly what he got right.

What did he call the hearer to do? At Cairo, as the rapporteur summarised him, he held that "there was a sound basis for the development of future studies." When Sauneron hoped someone would try to rebuild an ancient African mother-tongue from the languages living today, the record says, "Professor Obenga considered this method to be acceptable." His call is a call to work: learn your own languages, read them by rule, keep the record, and let the evidence carry the argument.

Now weigh it by the Word, because the Word is older than every linguist and explains itself. Obenga’s deepest conviction is one Scripture states first: "The lip of truth shall be established for ever: but a lying tongue is but for a moment." (Proverbs 12:19) A chronology carved on a wall can lie for a season; the tongue a grandmother passes to a grandchild keeps older testimony. And the Word does not treat language as an accident. It records that the languages of the earth have a history under Yahweh’s own hand: "Therefore is the name of it called Babel; because the LORD did there confound the language of all the earth: and from thence did the LORD scatter them abroad upon the face of all the earth." (Genesis 11:9) From Babel the families of mankind went out each with its speech, and the Word counts them that way: "These are the sons of Ham, after their families, after their tongues, in their countries, and in their nations." (Genesis 10:20) Notice who stands in that line. "And the sons of Ham; Cush, and Mizraim, and Phut, and Canaan." (Genesis 10:6) Mizraim is the Hebrew name for Egypt, and Cush is the land south of it. Long before any modern scholar argued about where Egypt belongs, the Word set Egypt in one family with its southern brother. We must not make that verse say more than it says. The same list names Canaan as a son of Ham, and the Word nowhere draws a linguist’s family tree; it is a table of families and nations, and where the Word is quiet about which language descends from which, we stay quiet too. But it does show that the Bible never treated Egypt as a stranger to Africa.

The Word also weighs the way an argument is carried. Obenga wrote of one series of his evidence that it was irrefutable, and he meant it. Scripture gives every bold case its test: "He that is first in his own cause seemeth just; but his neighbour cometh and searcheth him." (Proverbs 18:17) Obenga himself welcomed that searching at Cairo, and it is the posture we keep in these lessons. And the Word reminds us why the whole question matters. "And hath made of one blood all nations of men for to dwell on all the face of the earth, and hath determined the times before appointed, and the bounds of their habitation;" (Acts 17:26) No people is lifted up or put down by the family of its language. The goal is not a better rank in the world’s order; it is the truth, and the truth before the throne, where a great multitude stands "of all nations, and kindreds, and people, and tongues" (Revelation 7:9).

There is grace in this history, and we must not miss it. The same record that once treated the south of the Nile as black and primitive was corrected, in part, by the people who kept it. Vercoutter, Sauneron and the rapporteur wrote down what they found, even where it cut against the picture they had been trained in. That is Yahweh’s mercy at work in the slow repair of a record: He does not leave the truth buried, and He is patient with scholars as He is patient with us all. It also calls us to answer the old contempt without contempt. The men who held the old picture were not our enemies to be hated; they were men who needed light, as we do. The Word is plain: "Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good." (Romans 12:21) Obenga did that in Cairo. He overcame a century of dismissal not with insult but with careful, excellent work.

What it means for us now is close to home. The record of who we are is not only in archives others control. It is also in the words our grandparents used, the way our churches pray, the songs our mothers sang, and the languages our African brothers and sisters still speak. Obenga’s life is an invitation to treat that record as evidence and to read it carefully. And it is an invitation to pray the way the psalmist did, because the light that uncovers a hidden history does not come from us: "O send out thy light and thy truth: let them lead me" (Psalms 43:3). What was hidden will be known, as Jesus said: "For nothing is secret, that shall not be made manifest; neither any thing hid, that shall not be known and come abroad." (Luke 8:17)

## 2. Dr. Théophile Obenga — sound-laws, not look-alikes: his Cairo paper (1974)

*id: `pv-obenga-sound-laws` · 1917 words*

**The big idea.** At the UNESCO symposium in Cairo in 1974, Obenga argued that the question of where Egypt belongs could be answered by language, and only by language read according to strict rule. He set aside resemblance by shape, chance or borrowing, demanded regular sound-laws, and then laid out series of correspondences between ancient Egyptian, Coptic and living African languages, from abstract nouns like goodness to the verb to be and the ways of saying no. His conclusion was one line: a single linguistic type, the same formal and grammatical structure.

Picture the room. It is the end of January 1974, in Cairo, and UNESCO has gathered twenty specialists for a symposium on the peopling of ancient Egypt. Professor Vercoutter of France has written the working paper that frames the meeting, and in it he has named the kinds of evidence that could settle the question: bones, pictures, writings, language. Théophile Obenga, then in his thirties, from the young Republic of the Congo, has come with a paper on the third kind. He tells the room exactly what he is doing: "The purpose of this article is to provide details, under the third category of sources available as enumerated by Professor Vercoutter, for the study of the cultural origin of the Egyptian people who created the civilization of the Pharaohs." He does not come to make a speech. He comes to make an argument, and he builds it in steps.

The first step is the question itself. Why language? Because, he says, the founder of modern linguistics settled that long ago: "Since Ferdinand de Saussure, it has been accepted that the most obvious, relevant and conclusive method of proving that there exists a cultural connection between two, or more than two, peoples is to adduce linguistic evidence." Bones can be argued over, paintings can be read two ways, but the deep grammar of a language is passed from mother to child without anyone planning it, and it does not lie about its ancestors. So he states his task: "Our problem, therefore, consists in establishing the existence of a genetic linguistic relationship between Egyptian (ancient Egyptian and Coptic) and modern Negro-African languages." Notice the two names for Egyptian. Ancient Egyptian is the language of the hieroglyphs; Coptic is the same language in its last stage, written in a Greek-based alphabet and still used in the worship of Egypt’s Christians. Obenga will compare both with languages spoken in Africa today.

The second step clears the ground. The American linguist Joseph Greenberg had placed Egyptian, with Semitic and Berber, in a family he called Afro-Asiatic. Obenga answers him directly, leaning on the Hungarian linguist István Fodor: "The work of the American linguist Professor Greenberg, particularly in connection with his ‘Afro-Asiatic’ language family, cannot be regarded as invalidating the argument, for he ignored one vitally important methodological rule, namely the establishment of phonetic correspondences." His complaint is about method. Lining up many languages and counting words that look alike, he holds, cannot prove a family. Only regular, rule-bound correspondences of sound can.

The third step is a distinction every student of his paper must keep. Two languages can resemble each other because they are built alike, or because they descend from one parent, and those are different things. He gives an example anyone can follow: "Modern English, which is an isolating language, has affinities with Chinese from the typological point of view; but from the genetic point of view English and Chinese belong to different language families". Likeness of build tells you the type, not the family; as he puts it, "At the most, typological comparison shows to which linguistic type the languages being compared belong." He applies this to Semitic and Egyptian, listing features such as the Semitic conjugation prefixes that Egyptian does not share, and he warns against a lazy escape: "I should like to clarify one further notion, that of a mixed language, which is in fact linguistic nonsense." French borrowed heavily from Germanic, he notes, yet "French remains a Romance language belonging to the Indo-European family." Borrowed words do not change a language’s parentage.

The fourth step is his rule, and it is the heart of the paper: "Genetic linguistic relationship is established on the basis of ‘sound-laws’, that is to say enduring correspondences and regular similarities between complete forms, morphemes, and phonemes in the language being compared." A single look-alike word proves nothing; it could be chance. A pattern that repeats by rule, across sounds, word-pieces and whole forms, is evidence of descent. He names the method plainly: "This comparative and inductive method is the one used for the purpose of establishing genetic links between two or more languages."

The fifth step answers the obvious objection. Ancient Egyptian was written down five thousand years ago; most African languages were written down only recently. May we compare them at all? He asks the question himself, "Are we entitled to compare ancient Egyptian and modern Negro-African languages?", and answers yes, because "Language has an oral tradition which is independent of its written form." His example comes from the European languages: "Lithuanian, which has existed in written documents only since the sixteenth century (1540), nevertheless provides an image of Indo-European which is, generally speaking, just as authentic as that provided by Latin in the third century before our era." If a language first written in 1540 can testify about ancient Indo-European, a language first written last century can testify about ancient Africa.

The sixth step rules out borrowing. Correspondences established this way, he argues, "cannot be fortuitous, but necessarily imply a common original identity." He gives three reasons: the oldest member of the comparison, Pharaonic Egyptian, anchors it; the peoples involved lived far apart, and "the fact of geographical discontinuity militates against the probability of borrowings in these ancient times"; and the split from the common stock happened so long ago that grammar could not have been borrowed across it.

Then comes the evidence. He starts with grammar. Egyptian makes feminine words with a -t; many African languages have simplified that away, as Egyptian itself began to do, but he lists survivals in Oromo, Sidamo, Ronga, Zulu and Hausa. Egyptian makes plurals with -w; he finds kindred forms in Kanuri, Ewe, Bambara, Dyula, medieval Soninke and Dogon, and draws a principle from them: "a linguistic tradition never disappears entirely if a given language family is considered as a whole." He shows the old Egyptian imperative m or mi, take, beside Coptic ma and the ma of Mbosi, a language of his own Congo, and says, "Thus the identity is striking."

His showcase is the way abstract nouns are made. Ancient Egyptian puts the word bw, place, before an adjective to make an abstract noun, and he cites the Maxims of Ptahhotep, which he translates "good has become evil." Then he sets the African forms beside it: in Kongo, mbote is good and bu-bote is goodness; mbi is bad and bu-bi is badness; in Wolof, rafet is beautiful and bu-rafet is beauty; in Luganda, bi is bad and obu-bi is badness. "In ancient Egyptian and Wolof, furthermore, the parallelism is complete". Here he speaks at full strength: "The interpretation is complete: morphological, lexicological and syntactical. The evidence is irrefutable." And he throws down a challenge: "I maintain that it would be impossible to demonstrate such a clear parallelism between Semitic, Berber and Egyptian as we have just established between Egyptian and Negro-African".

He goes on through the verb to be: "It is extremely significant that the common archaic form in Bantu is exactly the same as the form of the verb ‘to be’ in the most archaic form of ancient Egyptian, the Egyptian of the Pyramids." Then negation: "All the negative affixes and other negative morphemes are also to be found in Negro-African." Then the emphatic future and the small linking words. And he closes the paper with one sentence: "Conclusion: a single linguistic type, the same formal and grammatical structure." In his spoken talk, the rapporteur adds, he traced the Egyptian word KM, black, through Coptic kame and kemi to the Bantu ikama, charred by heat, and the Azer kame, cinder, and he concluded that "The same phonemes have the same functions in the different languages compared."

What did he call his hearers to do? Not to applaud, but to keep working. The rapporteur records his judgment that "there was a sound basis for the development of future studies," and when Sauneron hoped someone would try to rebuild an ancient African parent language from the living ones, Obenga accepted the method. His call is to study African languages with the same rigour scholars had long given to Latin, Greek and Sanskrit.

Now weigh it by the Word, which explains itself. Obenga’s first premise, that speech testifies to origin, is a premise Scripture uses. At the fords of the Jordan the men of Gilead knew an Ephraimite by one consonant: "Say now Shibboleth: and he said Sibboleth: for he could not frame to pronounce it right" (Judges 12:6). In the high priest’s courtyard Peter was known by his accent: "And after a while came unto him they that stood by, and said to Peter, Surely thou also art one of them; for thy speech bewrayeth thee." (Matthew 26:73) The Word does not treat a people’s speech as noise; it treats it as a witness.

His rule, that one likeness proves nothing and only repeated, regular correspondences count, is close kin to the Word’s rule for any matter of truth: "at the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses, shall the matter be established" (Deuteronomy 19:15). A single look-alike word is one witness. A sound-law running through many forms is many witnesses. Where he insisted on that, he was right, and he was right against the habit of his field. And his patient labour honors the work Scripture calls royal: "It is the glory of God to conceal a thing: but the honour of kings is to search out a matter." (Proverbs 25:2)

The Word also tests the strength of our words. When Obenga wrote irrefutable, and when he said no such parallel could be shown with Semitic and Berber, he went further than the evidence has persuaded most of his colleagues to go; mainstream linguistics still classes Egyptian with the Afroasiatic family. Scripture does not tell us which language family Egyptian belongs to, and we will not pretend it does. It tells us how to carry a case: "He that is first in his own cause seemeth just; but his neighbour cometh and searcheth him." (Proverbs 18:17) Obenga laid his work open to be searched, and the next lesson shows what the room found when it searched.

And see the grace that runs under the whole subject. At Babel Yahweh scattered the tongues in judgment. But He did not leave them scattered. At Pentecost the same Lord, Yahweh, gathered them in mercy, and men from many nations, Egypt and the parts of Libya among them, heard the gospel each in the language he was born to: "And how hear we every man in our own tongue, wherein we were born?" (Acts 2:8) The Lord who confounded the languages is the Lord who redeems the speakers of every one of them. No tongue is beneath His notice, and none is beyond His grace. That is why the careful study of African languages is not a matter of pride against anyone; it is honouring what Yahweh made and what He means to redeem.

What this means for us now is practical. In an age of memes, a single look-alike word is passed around as proof of almost anything. Obenga’s paper is a rebuke to that habit, including when the meme flatters us. Ask for the rule, the pattern, the second and third witness. Learn your people’s languages and sayings as evidence worth studying, not as broken speech to be corrected. And do it all in the fear of Yahweh, who gave mankind its tongues and who searches every heart.

## 3. Dr. Théophile Obenga — a large measure of agreement: what the Cairo record itself says

*id: `pv-obenga-large-measure` · 1691 words*

**The big idea.** The strongest witness to Obenga’s work at Cairo is not his own paper but the report the symposium kept, prepared by UNESCO’s rapporteur and printed in UNESCO’s own history. On bones and paintings it records that neither side gave ground; on language it records a large measure of agreement, calls his report very constructive, and records Professor S. Sauneron of France saying Egyptian could not be isolated from its African context. The same record keeps the objections and the unsolved five-thousand-year gap, and it closes by admitting that the most painstakingly researched papers in the room were Diop’s and Obenga’s.

The last lesson let Obenga speak in his own paper. This lesson does something different, and in a way more powerful. It lets the other side of the table speak, because the most reliable witness to what happened at Cairo is the record the symposium kept about itself.

First, the setting of the record. The symposium met in two stages, and the report says so precisely: "The symposium was held in two stages: the first took place from 28 to 31 January 1974 and concerned ‘The peopling of ancient Egypt’, the second dealt with ‘The deciphering of the Meroitic script’ and took place from 1 to 3 February 1974." Twenty participants are listed: specialists from Egypt, Sudan, France, Germany, Sweden, Finland, Malta, Canada and the United States, and, from West and Central Africa, Diop of Senegal and "Professor Th. Obenga (People’s Republic of the Congo)." The report printed in UNESCO’s General History of Africa says of itself that it is an abridged version of the final report, prepared by the committee’s rapporteur, Professor J. Devisse of France. In other words, this is not a pamphlet by Obenga’s friends. It is the institution’s own minutes. When such a record grants something, the grant carries weight.

Second, the objection, which the record keeps in full and which we must hear first. Before Obenga spoke, Professor Abdelgadir M. Abdalla of Sudan had answered Diop’s linguistic examples. The report summarises him: "He concluded that the Egyptian language was not a purely African language; it belonged to a proto-Semitic group, as could be abundantly demonstrated by supporting examples." He judged that the comparison of a dead language with living ones was bound to be inconclusive, that "the similarities which had been pointed out were fortuitous," and that the evidence was "far more consistent with the theory of the spread of ancient Egyptian throughout Africa than of its kinship with present-day African languages." He asked a sharp question: "Why should it be assumed that ancient Egyptian and Wolof were related, but not ancient Egyptian and Meroitic, for example?" And he ended in a way any honest scholar can respect: "Professor Abdalla hoped that the inquiry would be pursued in the strictest fashion."

Third, Obenga’s answer, as the rapporteur heard it. The report says he reverted to the linguistic demonstration Diop had begun, criticised Greenberg’s method, warned against confusing typological likeness with genetic relationship, and rejected the notion of a mixed language as linguistic nonsense. He then showed his grammatical series and, in the rapporteur’s words, drew the conclusion that the similarities amounted to convincing proof of a close relationship, adding: "This kind of parallelism was impossible between Semitic, Berber and Egyptian." He traced sound-series word by word, KM, black, in ancient Egyptian, beside Coptic kame and kemi, Bantu ikama, charred by heat, and Azer kame, cinder, and concluded that "The same phonemes have the same functions in the different languages compared." And the report records his hope: "Professor Obenga inferred from these comparisons that it would be possible in the future to identify a ‘negro-Egyptian’ language, analogous to ‘Indo-European’."

Fourth, the verdict of the room on that item, and here the record tells on itself. On the earlier items, the peopling of the valley, the migrations, the colours in Egyptian painting, the report records hard disagreement. It is blunt about the colours: "During the discussion, nothing was conceded by either side." It also records that the experts who did not accept the theory of Diop and Obenga held that the population had come largely from the Sahara, from north and south, and that "In opposition to this theory, Professors Diop and Obenga submitted their own theory to the effect that the valley was peopled uniformly by black people and that the movement had been from south to north." But when the discussion reached language, the tone changed: "This item, in contrast to those previously discussed, revealed a large measure of agreement among the participants. The outline report by Professor Diop and the report by Professor Obenga were regarded as being very constructive."

The voice that carried that agreement belonged to Professor S. Sauneron of France, a scholar who was not Diop’s ally on every point. In the same session he affirmed the standard reading of a disputed word, KMTYW, as Egyptians, against Diop’s reading, and the report records that plainly too. Yet on the larger question he said this, as the report gives it: "Egyptian remained a stable language for a period of at least 4500 years." Borrowing from neighbours was to be expected, "but the Semitic roots numbered only a few hundred as compared with a total of several thousand words." And then the sentence that has been quoted ever since: "The Egyptian language could not be isolated from its African context and its origin could not be fully explained in terms of Semitic; it was thus quite normal to expect to find related languages in Africa."

He did not stop there, and neither may we. "However, a rigorous methodical approach required the difficult problem of the 5000-year gap to be faced: this was the period separating ancient Egyptian from present-day African languages." Obenga answered on the spot: "Professor Obenga drew attention to the fact that a language which was not fixed by a written form and which developed normally might retain certain ancient forms". Sauneron then noted that the method was of considerable interest, "since it could not be purely fortuitous that there was a similarity between the third person singular suffixed pronouns in ancient Egyptian and in Wolof," and he proposed the next step: "he hoped that an attempt would be made to reconstitute a palaeo-African language, using present-day languages as a starting point. This would facilitate comparison with ancient Egyptian. Professor Obenga considered this method to be acceptable."

Fifth, the closing judgment. The general conclusion of the report says: "It is to be expected that the overall results of the symposium will be very differently assessed by the various participants." Then it admits something remarkable about the room itself: "Although the preparatory working paper sent out by Unesco gave particulars of what was desired, not all participants had prepared communications comparable with the painstakingly researched contributions of Professors Cheikh Anta Diop and Obenga. There was consequently a real lack of balance in the discussions." And the volume’s own conclusion, written by its editor, keeps the language question open and conditional: "The theory of the genetic kinship between ancient Egyptian and the African languages has been discussed. If research confirms this theory, it will prove the deep-rooted linguistic unity of the continent."

What did Obenga call the room to do? To keep going. He accepted the method of reconstruction, and later in the symposium, as the record shows, he pressed for the systematic study of African languages. His call is to finish the work the room agreed was worth doing.

Now weigh it by the Word, with a just balance. "A false balance is abomination to the LORD: but a just weight is his delight." (Proverbs 11:1) That verse cuts both ways, and we hold it both ways. What Cairo recorded is what we should say: agreement that Egyptian belongs in its African setting, agreement that Obenga’s method was constructive, an unsolved gap, and a frank admission that the two African scholars came better prepared than anyone else.

The Word also shows us how to read a record. "These were more noble than those in Thessalonica, in that they received the word with all readiness of mind, and searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so." Read the report. And notice the kind of witness it is. Scripture establishes a matter "at the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses" (Deuteronomy 19:15); when the institution that once doubted becomes, in its own minutes, the witness to the agreement, that testimony is the strongest kind.

## 4. Dr. Théophile Obenga — the Negro-Egyptian family and the wisdom of Egypt: his later work

*id: `pv-obenga-negro-egyptian` · 1699 words*

**The big idea.** After Cairo, Obenga spent decades turning the method the symposium had found constructive into a full proposal: that Africa’s languages fall into three families, and that ancient Egyptian, Coptic, Chadic, Cushitic, Niger-Kordofanian and Nilo-Saharan form one of them, which he called Negro-Egyptian. Alongside it he wrote on African philosophy beginning with Egypt, and on Egyptian geometry and Egypt’s place in Greek learning. His message across that work is that Africa’s oldest civilization must be read as African, through its own language and thought, and that Africans must build the scholarship to do it.

When the symposium in Cairo closed in February 1974, Théophile Obenga was in his late thirties and had perhaps fifty years of work ahead of him. The room had agreed that his method was constructive, Professor S. Sauneron of France had proposed rebuilding an ancient African parent language from the languages living today, and the record says, "Professor Obenga considered this method to be acceptable." This lesson follows what he did with that acceptance: the long building of what he called the Negro-Egyptian family, and his writing on the wisdom of ancient Egypt. Because we could not fetch the text of these later books, we will say plainly where our account comes from, and we will not put words in his mouth.

First, the setting. Obenga did not do this work in a quiet study. In the years after Cairo he taught, wrote history and poetry, served in the public life of his country, and until the end of 1991 directed the International Centre for Bantu Civilizations in Libreville, Gabon,. He edited the journal Ankh, and he taught in the Africana Studies program at San Francisco State University, where he is professor emeritus. His bibliography tells the story of his priorities by its titles alone. Before Cairo he had already published L’Afrique dans l’Antiquité: Égypte pharaonique, Afrique noire (1973), Africa in antiquity, Pharaonic Egypt and Black Africa, set side by side in the title itself. After Cairo came Les Bantu: langues, peuples, civilisations (1985); La Philosophie africaine de la période pharaonique, 2780-330 avant notre ère (1990), later in English as African Philosophy: The Pharaonic Period; a student handbook in English, Ancient Egypt and Black Africa (1992); Origine commune de l’égyptien ancien, du copte et des langues négro-africaines modernes, with the subtitle Introduction à la linguistique historique africaine (1993); La Géométrie égyptienne (1995); a book on Cheikh Anta Diop’s contribution to world history (1996); L’Égypte, la Grèce et l’école d’Alexandrie (2005), on the Egyptian sources of Greek philosophy; and L’égyptien pharaonique: une langue négro-africaine (2010).

Second, his argument, step by step, as published summaries of his work describe it. He begins where he began at Cairo, with method. A summary of his approach explains that the historical linguist must test every likeness between languages to decide whether it is fortuitous, borrowed, convergent, or inherited, and that only regular, inherited likenesses, whose development can be traced through time and space by rule, establish a genetic relationship. That is his Cairo rule, sharpened. He then turns it against the classification that had become standard. The American linguist Joseph Greenberg had grouped Egyptian with Semitic and Berber in a family called Afro-Asiatic, and the standard map that followed counts four families in Africa; UNESCO’s own volume charts them in East Africa as Nilo-Saharan, Afroasiatic (taking in the Cushitic languages), Niger-Congo (taking in the Bantu languages) and Khoisan. Obenga criticised Greenberg’s mass comparison, as he had in Cairo, as unable to prove a genetic relationship.

Third, his proposal. According to the published summary, Obenga proposes three major families for Africa instead of four: Berber; Khoisan; and a large family he calls Negro-Egyptian, developed from what Diop had called Paleo-African. Into Negro-Egyptian he places Ancient Egyptian, Coptic, Chadic, Cushitic, Niger-Kordofanian and Nilo-Saharan. Notice the move. He takes Egyptian, Chadic and Cushitic out of the Afro-Asiatic family and joins them to the great families of Black Africa, leaving Berber and Semitic outside. That is the Cairo claim, that Egyptian is closer to Wolof and Kongo than to Semitic and Berber, carried out across the whole continent. The same summary reports, citing a later scholar, Kambon, that Obenga identified 101 putative cognates, shared words, spread across languages Greenberg had placed in different families, and argues that their spread across the continent makes borrowing an unlikely explanation. His 1993 book on the common origin of ancient Egyptian, Coptic and the modern Negro-African languages carries the subtitle Introduction to African historical linguistics: in other words, he meant it as the start of a field, the very work Sauneron had proposed at Cairo.

Fourth, the wisdom of Egypt. Obenga did not think language was the end of the matter. If Egyptian was an African language, then the thought written in it, the moral teaching of the Instructions, the reasoning about order, justice and the good, belonged to African intellectual history too. In his Cairo paper he had already chosen as his showcase a line from the Maxims of Ptahhotep, which he translated "good has become evil," to show how Egyptian builds the abstract idea of goodness the same way Kongo and Wolof do. His later book on African philosophy in the Pharaonic period develops that conviction at length. We have not been able to read it, so we quote only how its publisher describes it: "The first book to be written on African philosophy beginning with Ancient Egypt and relating it to modern and traditional African societies, as well as the rest of the world." The same description goes further and claims the book proves the precedence of Egyptian philosophy over all others in the world. That is the publisher’s claim, and we hold it at arm’s length.

What did he call his hearers to do? His titles answer: study the Bantu peoples in their own languages; write the history of Congo and of central Africa from African documents; build an African university able to do this research; and fight what one of his titles calls Eurocentric Africanism, the habit of studying Africa only through European eyes. His call to Africans and to the African diaspora is the call he answered himself: become the scholars of your own past.

Now weigh it honestly. Two things are established, and they matter. Egyptian is an African language, rooted in Africa, as the Cairo record agreed when Sauneron said it "could not be isolated from its African context." Established: Egyptian is an African language whose setting is Africa, as the Cairo record agreed, and the comparative method by regular correspondences, which he champions, is the right test.
