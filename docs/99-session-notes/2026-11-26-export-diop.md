## 1. Dr. Cheikh Anta Diop — the scientist who put Kemet back in Africa

*id: `pv-diop` · 1565 words*

**The big idea.** Dr. Cheikh Anta Diop (Senegal, 1923-1986) was a physicist and historian who, while Africa was still ruled from Europe, set out to show by evidence, not by myth, that ancient Egypt belonged to Africa and that Africa therefore had a history worth writing. He put the argument into Nations nègres et culture (1954), founded the radiocarbon laboratory at IFAN in Dakar, and carried it to the 1974 UNESCO symposium in Cairo, whose own report says his and Théophile Obenga’s papers were the painstakingly researched ones in the room. This lesson lets him speak, keeps the record the other side wrote, and weighs his claims by the Word.

The first scholar in this part of the study is Dr. Cheikh Anta Diop, and the place to begin is the world he was born into. He was born on December 29, 1923, at Diourbel in Senegal, which was then a colony in French West Africa. The schools, the courts, the textbooks and the museums answered to Paris. And the lesson that system taught, sometimes in so many words and more often by silence, was that Africa had no history of its own: that the continent had waited, without writing or states or science, until Europe arrived. Ancient Egypt was the one great problem for that lesson, because no one could deny the pyramids, the calendar, the medicine or the monumental writing. So Egypt was quietly moved. It was taught as a civilization of the Mediterranean or of Asia that happened to stand on African soil, its builders an imported people. Diop described the result years later in the UNESCO General History of Africa. In most textbooks, he wrote, "it is simply and flatly asserted that the Egyptians were white and the honest layman is left with the impression that any such assertion must necessarily have a prior basis of solid research." Then he answered it: "But there is no such basis, as this chapter has shown. And so generation after generation has been misled." That sentence is the reason he belongs among the voices that were not heard. In 1946, at twenty-three, Diop went to Paris to study. He took his first degree in philosophy and then studied chemistry, and later nuclear physics in the laboratory of Frédéric Joliot-Curie at the Collège de France. He was not only in the library. In his own account of his life’s work, the preface he called The Meaning of Our Work, he says he began his research in September 1946 and served from 1950 to 1953 as Secretary General of the students of the RDA, the African Democratic Rally, the movement pressing for independence in French Africa. For him, recovering Africa’s history and winning Africa’s freedom were one fight, and he saw the colonial classroom as part of what had to be undone. In 1951 he registered a thesis subject at the University of Paris on who the pre-dynastic Egyptians were. The research became his first book, Nations nègres et culture, published in 1954. The doctorate he received in 1960 was on a different subject, a comparison of the political and social systems of Europe and Africa, which he published as L’Afrique noire précoloniale, in English Precolonial Black Africa. A story is often told that a Paris jury refused his Egypt thesis on political grounds; we could not verify that story from a source we could read, so we do not tell it here, and the record we can read is strong enough without it. From 1961 he was on the staff of IFAN, the Fundamental Institute of Black Africa in Dakar, where, in his translator’s words, he directed the radiocarbon laboratory which he founded. Think about what that means. The man who argued about the age and origin of civilizations built the instrument that measures age. In 1966, at the First World Festival of Negro Arts, he shared a special award with W. E. B. DuBois as the writer who had exerted the greatest influence on Black thought in the twentieth century. After his death in 1986, the University of Dakar was renamed Cheikh Anta Diop University. Now his message, in overview, because the lessons that follow take it piece by piece. First, he held that ancient Egypt was an African civilization, born in the Nile Valley among African peoples. In a 1956 address in Paris, quoted by his translator, he said, "we have come to discover that the ancient Pharaonic Egyptian civilization was undoubtedly a Negro civilization." The word Negro was the ordinary scholarly word of his time and language; he meant Black African. Second, he held that this was not a matter of pride but of method: African history cannot be written correctly while its oldest and best-documented chapter is cut away. He compared it to a European historian trying to write Europe’s history without Greece and Rome. Third, he refused to win the argument with myth. This is the line we should remember him by. He wrote that it could seem too tempting "to delude the masses engaged in a struggle for national independence by taking liberties with scientific truth, by unveiling a mythical, embellished past." He would not do it. He brought physical anthropology, the reports of Greek travelers, Egyptian art, language comparison and a laboratory test of skin samples from mummies, and he asked his readers to check him. Fourth, he called for workers, not fans. He wanted teams of honest, bold researchers, and in 1971 he asked UNESCO for an international meeting in Egypt where Egyptologists and Africanists would compare their evidence face to face for the first time. UNESCO accepted. That meeting was held in Cairo from 28 January to 3 February 1974, with twenty scholars present, and its report is printed in UNESCO’s own General History of Africa. The report does not say Diop won every point. It records that most of the experts did not accept his claim that the Nile Valley population was homogeneous from the earliest times, and that on the colours in Egyptian art nothing was conceded by either side. But it also records this, in the rapporteur’s own words: not all participants had prepared communications comparable with "the painstakingly researched contributions of Professors Cheikh Anta Diop and Obenga." And it adds, "There was consequently a real lack of balance in the discussions." That is the record telling on itself, and it is the kind of evidence this study values most. Today the mainstream states plainly that Egyptian civilization was an indigenous development of the Nile Valley, an African civilization; that was the heart of what he was mocked for saying. Some of his particular claims remain contested, and we name them narrowly in each lesson. Now we weigh him by the Word, and we do it honestly in both directions. Where the record was rewritten, the Word is not surprised. Daniel saw a power that would "think to change times and laws" (Daniel 7:25), and our Lord promised the opposite end: "For nothing is secret, that shall not be made manifest; neither any thing hid, that shall not be known and come abroad." (Luke 8:17) Where Diop insisted that Egypt belongs to Africa’s family, the Word said it first, in the table of nations: "And the sons of Ham; Cush, and Mizraim, and Phut, and Canaan." (Genesis 10:6) Mizraim is the Hebrew name for Egypt, and the Psalms call Egypt "the land of Ham" (Psalms 105:23). The Word even tells on Egypt’s greatness itself: Moses "was learned in all the wisdom of the Egyptians" (Acts 7:22), and Solomon’s wisdom is praised as greater than "all the wisdom of Egypt" (1 Kings 4:30). Scripture never pretended Egypt was small. But Diop also went beyond the record into claims the Word corrects. In the same preface he claimed that Judaism and Islam are rooted in Egyptian cosmogony, that the Egyptian god osiris is a figure "essentially identifiable with Christ," and that "certain Biblical passages are practically copies of Egyptian moral texts." Those are his claims, and they are wrong. The Scriptures did not come from Egypt’s temples: "For the prophecy came not in old time by the will of man: but holy men of God spake as they were moved by the Holy Ghost." (2 Peter 1:21) The worlds were not explained by Egypt’s cosmogony; they were framed by the Word of Yahweh: "Through faith we understand that the worlds were framed by the word of God, so that things which are seen were not made of things which do appear." (Hebrews 11:3) And Jesus is not a copy of a river god; He is the Lamb of Yahweh, the Eternal Son, who died and rose in history before witnesses. His first lesson takes those claims one by one. What does Diop mean for us now? He shows us a scholar who loved his people enough to refuse to lie to them, and that is a rare kind of love. He teaches us to ask of every textbook, every documentary and every preacher, what is the evidence? And he reminds us that a record rewritten by power does not stay rewritten, because Yahweh is the One we ask, "O send out thy light and thy truth" (Psalms 43:3). We honour him for the evidence he gathered and the courage it took, and we weigh every word he said, as we weigh every word anyone says, by the Word that does not change. And we hold all of it in grace. The record that was hidden is not a reason for bitterness toward any people, European or Egyptian; it is an occasion for Yahweh’s mercy, which restores what was taken: "I will restore to you the years that the locust hath eaten" (Joel 2:25). His mercy even reaches the land at the centre of this story; the prophet heard Yahweh say "Blessed be Egypt my people" (Isaiah 19:25). We tell the hard history plainly, we forgive as we have been forgiven, and we keep the truth, because the truth told in love builds up every nation.

## 2. Dr. Cheikh Anta Diop — Ancient Egypt was an African civilization: the thesis and the call

*id: `pv-diop-african-origin` · 1717 words*

**The big idea.** In The African Origin of Civilization (his 1954 and 1967 chapters, in English in 1974) Diop argued that ancient Egypt was a Black African civilization, that the history of Africa cannot be written correctly until it is connected to Egypt, and that the modern West had falsified the record by inventing a foreign, white Egypt. He refused to answer the falsification with a flattering myth, and he called his readers to become research teams, not passive readers. The Word confirms the record he fought for and corrects the claims he made about the origin of the faith.

The African Origin of Civilization: Myth or Reality is the book most English readers know Cheikh Anta Diop by, and it is worth knowing exactly what it is. Mercer Cook, the translator, explains in his preface that it presents, with the author’s consent, ten chapters from Diop’s first book, Nations nègres et culture (1954), and three from Antériorité des civilisations nègres (1967), leaving out most of the technical linguistic passages. It came out in 1974, the same year as the Cairo symposium. The chapter titles lay out the case: What Were the Egyptians?; Birth of the Negro Myth; Modern Falsification of History; Could Egyptian Civilization Have Originated in the Delta?; Could Egyptian Civilization Be of Asian Origin?; The Egyptian Race as Seen and Treated by Anthropologists; Arguments Supporting a Negro Origin; Arguments Opposing a Negro Origin; Peopling of Africa from the Nile Valley; Political and Social Evolution of Ancient Egypt; Contribution of Ethiopia-Nubia and Egypt; Reply to a Critic; Early History of Humanity. Notice two of those titles. He gave a whole chapter to the arguments against him, and a whole chapter to replying to a critic. That is not how a man writes who wants only admirers. Now the setting. Diop wrote the core of this work in Paris between 1946 and 1954, in the last decade of French rule in West Africa. In the same years he was helping lead the African students of the RDA, the movement for independence, and by his own account the problem was posed in terms of restoring what he called the collective national African personality. Colonial schooling had taught that Africa had no history worth the name. Diop believed that a people cannot stand up straight on that lie. But he also believed something harder: a people cannot stand on a new lie either. In his preface, The Meaning of Our Work, dated July 1973, he names the temptation in so many words. It could seem too tempting, he writes, "to delude the masses engaged in a struggle for national independence by taking liberties with scientific truth, by unveiling a mythical, embellished past." He goes on to say, in summary, that a people’s collective personality has a psychic side that poets can address, and a historical and linguistic side that can be studied scientifically, and that he deliberately kept to the side that can be tested. His argument then runs in steps. The first step is the thesis itself: "Ancient Egypt was a Negro civilization. The history of Black Africa will remain suspended in air and cannot be written correctly until African historians dare to connect it with the history of Egypt." Negro was the ordinary scholarly word of his time and language for Black Africans; he uses it as a description, and so should we read it. The second step is the comparison that makes the point for anyone schooled in the West: "Imagine, if you can, the uncomfortable position of a western historian who was to write the history of Europe without referring to Greco-Latin Antiquity and try to pass that off as a scientific approach." If Europe’s story begins with Greece and Rome, Africa’s story begins with the Nile Valley, and an African history that skips Egypt is like a house hanging in the air without a foundation. The third step is the charge of falsification. In chapter III he credits the French traveler Volney, who had seen the Egyptians and their monuments with his own eyes, and says Volney’s conclusions "should have ruled out the subsequent invention of a hypothetical white Pharaonic race that allegedly imported Egyptian civilization from Asia at the start of the historical period." In other words, the white Egypt of the nineteenth-century textbooks was not discovered; it was invented after the eyewitness evidence pointed the other way. The rest of the book brings the evidence: what the Greek writers said they saw, what the Egyptians painted of themselves, what the anthropologists measured and then explained away, how language, kingship, circumcision, totems and customs run from the Nile into West and Central Africa. The fourth step is the call. Diop did not want a following; he wanted a workforce. "Today, what interests me most is to see the formation of teams, not of passive readers, but of honest, bold research workers, allergic to complacency and busy substantiating and exploring ideas expressed in our work." At the end of the book he calls the whole thing a progress report, written to show "the direction in which future generations of Black African scholars must continue calmly to work." Calmly is the word to notice. He wanted the work done without rage and without flattery. How has the record treated his central claim? The heart of it has been vindicated. The current position of mainstream scholarship is that Egyptian civilization was an indigenous development of the Nile Valley, an African civilization. That is what he was mocked for saying in 1954. Some of his particular claims are still contested, and honesty requires naming them narrowly: whether Egypt was one homogeneous people from beginning to end, and whether modern race categories fit an ancient people at all. Even Immanuel Wallerstein, summarizing him in 1961, called the theory a bold hypothesis "which is not presented without supporting data." Now we weigh the whole message by the Word, and the Word explains itself. On the record, Scripture stands with the truth he fought for. The table of nations names Egypt’s founding father, Mizraim, as a son of Ham and a brother of Cush: "And the sons of Ham; Cush, and Mizraim, and Phut, and Canaan." (Genesis 10:6) The Psalms call Egypt "the land of Ham" (Psalms 105:23). The Word never shrank Egypt. It says Moses "was learned in all the wisdom of the Egyptians" (Acts 7:22), and it measures Solomon’s wisdom against "all the wisdom of Egypt" (1 Kings 4:30). And the Word warns against the very fault that produced the falsified textbooks: "He that answereth a matter before he heareth it, it is folly and shame unto him." (Proverbs 18:13) But in the same preface Diop makes three claims about religion that go beyond the record and against the Word, and we must name them plainly, because a voice is weighed whole. He claims that Judaism and Islam, along with Greek philosophy and modern science, "are rooted in Egyptian cosmogony and science." He says the Egyptian god osiris, the dying and rising figure of their myths, is "essentially identifiable with Christ." And he says that "certain Biblical passages are practically copies of Egyptian moral texts." Each of these the Word corrects. First, the faith of Israel did not grow out of Egypt’s account of creation. Yahweh called Abram out of Ur, revealed His own name to Moses in the wilderness, "I AM THAT I AM" (Exodus 3:14), and then brought Israel out of Egypt while He judged its gods: "against all the gods of Egypt I will execute judgment: I am the LORD" (Exodus 12:12). The worlds were not explained by any nation’s cosmogony; "the worlds were framed by the word of God" (Hebrews 11:3), and that Word is the Son Himself: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. The same was in the beginning with God. All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made." (John 1:1-3) Second, Jesus is not a copy of a myth. A myth happens nowhere and to no one; the cross happened under Pontius Pilate before witnesses, and Paul could point to living eyewitnesses of the risen Lord: "he was seen of above five hundred brethren at once" (1 Corinthians 15:6). Peter says it plainly: "we have not followed cunningly devised fables" (2 Peter 1:16). Jesus is the Lamb of Yahweh, the Eternal Son, and there is no other name. Third, where an Egyptian moral text sounds like a proverb of Scripture, the Word explains why without any copying: Yahweh wrote His law on the conscience of every nation. "For when the Gentiles, which have not the law, do by nature the things contained in the law, these, having not the law, are a law unto themselves: Which shew the work of the law written in their hearts, their conscience also bearing witness, and their thoughts the mean while accusing or else excusing one another;)" (Romans 2:14-15) A likeness of conscience is the Maker’s fingerprint, not the Bible’s source. The Scriptures themselves tell us where they came from: "For the prophecy came not in old time by the will of man: but holy men of God spake as they were moved by the Holy Ghost." (2 Peter 1:21) One more claim in the preface needs the Word’s light: his statement that all races descended from the Black race. Scripture does not sort humanity by race at all. It says Yahweh "hath made of one blood all nations of men" (Acts 17:26), and it stays silent about the colour of the first man, so we stay silent too. What, then, does this lesson ask of us now? It asks us to take Diop’s challenge and Diop’s discipline together. Be a researcher, not a passive reader; check the textbook, check the documentary, check the preacher, and check Diop. Refuse the flattering myth even when it would feel good, because a people who were lied to do not need a new lie. Keep the record he recovered: Egypt is African, and the Word said so first. And let the Word, not any scholar, be the measure that corrects every claim, his included. One last word, about grace. It would be easy to meet the men who invented a white Egypt with contempt, and easy to meet Diop’s errors about the Bible with contempt too. The Word gives us neither. It tells the truth about error and offers mercy to the one who erred: "he retaineth not his anger for ever, because he delighteth in mercy" (Micah 7:18). And it holds out a promise for the very peoples of this story, that "Ethiopia shall soon stretch out her hands unto God" (Psalms 68:31). Our part is to tell the record straight and to pray that every reader of it, of every people, comes home to the Lamb.

## 3. Dr. Cheikh Anta Diop — Origin of the ancient Egyptians: the evidence, laid out for the world

*id: `pv-diop-unesco-chapter` · 1592 words*

**The big idea.** UNESCO opened its own General History of Africa volume on the ancient civilizations with Diop’s chapter, Origin of the ancient Egyptians (1981). When UNESCO undertook its General History of Africa, the project itself was a statement. For generations the history of Africa had been written from outside, by the colonizing powers, and the new project set out to write it from the inside, with African scholars among its authors and on its international committee, Diop among them. In 1981 the second volume appeared, Ancient Civilizations of Africa, edited by the Egyptian scholar Gamal Mokhtar. The world’s own body for education and culture opened its account of ancient Africa with the man much of the mainstream had spent more than twenty-five years dismissing. The chapter is Diop’s case, laid out for any reader to check, and it is built like a case at law, one exhibit at a time. The first exhibit is the human story itself. Because the oldest human remains are African, he argues, "of necessity the earliest men were ethnically homogeneous and negroid," and the peopling of Egypt came down the river: "From the Upper Palaeolithic to the dynastic epoch, the whole of the river’s basin was taken over progressively by these negroid peoples." The second exhibit is the anthropologists’ own work. He reviews the skull measurements and classifications of the European scholars, and he does not hide how messy they were; he points to the arbitrary nature of the criteria used to sort Egyptians into races. ## 4. Dr. Cheikh Anta Diop — Cairo 1974: the meeting he asked for, and the report that told on itself

*id: `pv-diop-cairo-1974` · 1434 words*

**The big idea.** In 1971 Diop asked UNESCO for an international meeting in Egypt where Egyptologists and Africanists would compare their evidence on who the ancient Egyptians were, and UNESCO agreed. It met in Cairo from 28 January to 3 February 1974. The official report, printed in UNESCO’s own history, records real disagreement (most experts did not accept his claim of a homogeneous population, and on the colours in Egyptian art nothing was conceded), a large measure of agreement on his and Obenga’s linguistic method, and a conclusion in the rapporteur’s own words: not all participants had prepared work comparable with the painstakingly researched contributions of Diop and Obenga, so there was a real lack of balance in the discussions.

Few events in this study are told more often, and read less, than the UNESCO symposium in Cairo in 1974. It is often told as a legend: two African scholars walked into a room of European experts and won. The real record is more instructive than the legend, because the real record was written by the other side, and it tells on itself. Begin with how the meeting came to be. In 1971 Diop sat on UNESCO’s International Scientific Committee for the drafting of a General History of Africa. At the committee’s plenary session in Paris in April and May of that year, he made three requests before the volume on African antiquity could be prepared. In his own account, at the close of The African Origin of Civilization, he writes, "I proposed three preconditions to the preparation of volume II," and then, "All three proposals were accepted." Sit with the words for the first time. In 1971, the scholars who wrote about Egypt and the scholars who wrote about Africa had never been made to sit at one table and compare their evidence. Egypt had been studied as if it stood apart from the continent it stands on. Diop did not ask for a platform for himself; he asked for a table where the evidence would be laid out by both sides. That is the posture of a man who believes his case can bear examination. The symposium was held in Cairo in two stages: from 28 to 31 January 1974 on the peopling of ancient Egypt, and from 1 to 3 February 1974 on the deciphering of the Meroitic script of ancient Nubia. Twenty scholars took part, from Egypt, Sudan, France, Malta, Finland, West Germany, Sweden, Canada, the United States, Senegal and the Congo; Diop came from Senegal, and Théophile Obenga from the People’s Republic of the Congo. ## 5. Dr. Cheikh Anta Diop — The two cradles: why patriarchy is not the measure of civilization

*id: `pv-diop-two-cradles` · 1570 words*

**The big idea.** In The Cultural Unity of Black Africa (French 1959; subtitled, in English, the domains of patriarchy and of matriarchy in classical antiquity) Diop argued that humanity’s civilizations grew up in two early cradles: a southern one, the Nile Valley and Africa, where settled farming life produced matrilineal societies, the territorial state and a collective outlook; and a northern, Eurasian one, where a harsher life produced patriarchy, the city-state and individualism. From this he argued that Europe’s patriarchy was not the universal higher stage every people must reach, and that Africa’s ways with family and kingship were not primitive but different. The record of matrilineal African kingdoms stands; his climate-made binary and his claim that monotheism was born of farming life are weighed and corrected by the Word.

This lesson takes us into one of Diop’s boldest ideas, and into a corner of history that touches every family. To hear it rightly, start with the theory he was answering. In the nineteenth century, a line of European thinkers, among them J. J. Bachofen, Lewis Henry Morgan and Friedrich Engels, taught that all human societies begin in a stage they called matriarchy, organized around the mother, and then advance into patriarchy, organized around the father. In their scheme patriarchy was the higher stage, Europe stood at the top of the ladder, and peoples who traced descent through the mother, as many African peoples did, were simply further down. That ladder was taught as science in Diop’s day, and it gave a learned voice to the colonial claim that Africa was humanity’s childhood. Diop’s answer is the book first published in French in 1959 and later in English as The Cultural Unity of Black Africa, subtitled the domains of patriarchy and of matriarchy in classical antiquity. We could not read its full text, so we will hear Diop through his own statement of the same idea in The African Origin of Civilization, and through the book’s publisher. The publisher describes it as situating "the geographical and cultural origins of patriarchy and matriarchy in Europe and Africa respectively," and showing "that social systems evolve out of specific climatic and environmental factors." Diop’s own statement is this: "The history of humanity will remain confused as long as we fail to distinguish between the two early cradles in which Nature fashioned the instincts, temperament, habits, and ethical concepts of the two Subdivisions before they met each other after a long separation dating back to prehistoric times. The first of those cradles, as we shall see in the chapter on Egypt’s contribution, is the valley of the Nile, from the Great Lakes to the Delta." His argument runs in four steps. First, the material conditions of life, climate and the way people get their bread, shape social systems. Second, in the southern cradle, the Nile Valley and Africa, settled farming in an abundant valley produced, he argues, descent through the mother, a high standing for women, the large territorial state rather than the walled city, a welcome for strangers, and a collective outlook. Third, in the northern cradle, the harsh Eurasian steppe, a roaming, pastoral life produced patriarchy, the city-state, suspicion of strangers, and individualism. Fourth, and this is the point of it all, patriarchy is therefore not the universal higher stage of human development. It is the custom of one cradle. The matriarchal ways of Africa are not childhood; they are the other cradle. Diop rereads the classics of Greece and Rome in that light. Where Bachofen saw in the Orestes and the Aeneid a "universal transition from matriarchy to patriarchy," Diop saw "the encounter and conflict of two different conceptions." Immanuel Wallerstein, summarizing Diop in 1961 in a passage his translator reprints, described the two outlooks in nearly the same terms: in summary, one tied to harsher climates, patriarchy, war and the city-state, the other to matrilineal families, peace, the territorial state and social collectivism. Diop draws a practical conclusion for Africa: its family and kingship customs are not backward stages to be outgrown on the way to becoming European, but an inheritance with its own logic, to be understood and built upon. Now we must sort what Diop brings, because three different kinds of claims are mixed together. The first kind is established fact. Matrilineal succession, kingship and inheritance passing through the mother’s line, is widely documented across African kingdoms, and Diop himself documents matrilineal succession in ancient Ghana and Mali from the medieval Arabic sources in his Precolonial Black Africa. African women’s standing in many of those societies is a matter of record. And the European ladder, with patriarchy as the higher stage and Africa below, is not held by serious scholars today. On that, Diop was right to fight, and history has come his way. The second kind is debated theory, and we name it narrowly. Whether climate shapes the character of a whole people as strongly and as neatly as the two-cradle binary says is contested. Whether matriarchy is the right word is contested; many scholars prefer matrilineal or matrilocal, because descent through the mother is not the same as rule by women, and in many matrilineal kingdoms men still reigned. And the historian Stephen Howe faults Diop for building on the very nineteenth-century theorists he was answering and failing to take modern research into account. Those are honest debates about a theory, and we leave them as debates. The third kind is a claim about religion that the Word corrects. On the very next page Diop writes, "Because of the requirements of agricultural life, concepts such as matriarchy and totemism, the most perfect social organization, and monotheistic religion were born." He goes on to trace circumcision to beliefs about the Egyptian god amon. That is his claim, and it is the opposite of what the Word teaches. Belief in the one true God was not grown in a field. Yahweh revealed Himself, and the human story, as Paul tells it, is not an ascent from many gods to one but a fall from knowing the one God into idols: "Because that, when they knew God, they glorified him not as God, neither were thankful; but became vain in their imaginations, and their foolish heart was darkened. Professing themselves to be wise, they became fools, And changed the glory of the uncorruptible God into an image made like to corruptible man, and to birds, and fourfooted beasts, and creeping things." (Romans 1:21-23) That passage could have been written over the temples of the Nile, with their images of birds and beasts. Yahweh’s own name is not a farmer’s idea; He gave it: "And God said unto Moses, I AM THAT I AM: and he said, Thus shalt thou say unto the children of Israel, I AM hath sent me unto you." (Exodus 3:14) And circumcision in Israel was not a custom drifting north from an Egyptian myth; it was the sign of a covenant Yahweh spoke to Abraham: "And ye shall circumcise the flesh of your foreskin; and it shall be a token of the covenant betwixt me and you." (Genesis 17:11) The Word does not deny that Egyptians practised circumcision; it lists Egypt among the circumcised nations in "Egypt, and Judah, and Edom" (Jeremiah 9:25-26). What the Word supplies is the meaning: a cut in the flesh is nothing without a covenant with the living God and a circumcised heart. One more correction belongs here, gently. In Diop’s scheme the southern cradle can sound almost innocent, gentle and just by nature, and the northern one harsh by nature. The Word will not let any cradle off the hook: "For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God;" (Romans 3:23) Climate did not make the north wicked or the south righteous; every heart in every valley needs the Lamb of Yahweh. Now hear how the Word itself answers the ladder Diop was fighting, because it answers it more deeply than he did. It begins with man and woman made together, equally, in Yahweh’s image: "So God created man in his own image, in the image of God created he him; male and female created he them." (Genesis 1:27) It commands honour to both parents in one breath: "Honour thy father and thy mother: that thy days may be long upon the land which the LORD thy God giveth thee." (Exodus 20:12) It puts the mother’s teaching beside the father’s: "My son, hear the instruction of thy father, and forsake not the law of thy mother:" (Proverbs 1:8) It praises a woman whose own works praise her in the gates, a woman of trade, land and wisdom: "Give her of the fruit of her hands; and let her own works praise her in the gates." (Proverbs 31:31) And it rebukes domination at its root by commanding husbands to love their wives "even as Christ also loved the church, and gave himself for it" (Ephesians 5:25). The Word never ranks peoples by their family customs on a ladder of progress; it measures every family, African and European, by righteousness and love. What does this lesson ask of us now? It asks us to refuse the old lie that our family ways were backward by definition. Our grandmothers who held churches together, and the African queen mothers whom history records, were not a lower stage of humanity. It asks us to keep the record Diop recovered: African kingdoms with ordered, mother-line succession, as documented as any European dynasty. It asks us to hold his theory of cradles loosely, as a theory still being debated. And it asks us to let the Word, not Europe’s ladder and not Diop’s cradles, define the family: father and mother honoured, man and woman made in Yahweh’s image, and every people called to the same Lord. In that Lord there is grace for every family that was ranked low, and grace for every family that did the ranking: "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither bond nor free, there is neither male nor female: for ye are all one in Christ Jesus." (Galatians 3:28) The old ladder ends at the foot of the cross, where no people stands higher than another.

## 6. Dr. Cheikh Anta Diop — Precolonial Black Africa: kingdoms with constitutions, and his last word, Civilization or Barbarism

*id: `pv-diop-precolonial-africa` · 1441 words*

**The big idea.** In Precolonial Black Africa (his 1960 doctoral work, in English in 1987) Diop set out to bring Africa’s past back to life from the documents, refusing a history of dates "as dry as laundry lists": he laid out the constitutions of the Mossi and Cayor, mother-line succession in Ghana and Mali, the administration, armies, courts, economies and universities of the African empires, and compared them with Europe’s. His last book, Civilization or Barbarism (French 1981; English 1991, after his death), carried that work into human origins, cultural identity and African philosophy; we know it here only through its publisher’s description and catalogue contents, and we say so. The record of ordered African states stands; his claim to laws that let a people master history is weighed by the Word, which says Yahweh changes the times and sets up kings.

To understand this lesson, hold one fact in mind that surprises many people: Cheikh Anta Diop’s Paris doctorate, granted in 1960, was not about Egypt. His subject was a comparative study of the political and social systems of Europe and Black Africa, from antiquity to the formation of modern states, and it was published that year in French as L’Afrique noire précoloniale and in English in 1987, translated by Harold J. Salemson, as Precolonial Black Africa. Remember the setting. In 1960 much of Africa was becoming independent, and the new nations were told, in a thousand ways, that before the Europeans came there had been tribes and villages but not states, not law, not learning; that self-government was a European gift Africans were only now learning to use. Diop set out to show from the documents that this was false. His preface begins with the problem. "Until now [1960, date of the first edition], the history of Black Africa has always been written with dates as dry as laundry lists, and no one has almost ever tried to find the key that unlocks the door to the intelligence, the understanding of African society." The sources existed. Arab historians and travelers such as Ibn Khaldun and Ibn Battuta had described the West African empires, and African scholars in Timbuktu had written chronicles of their own. But that history lay unread. In his words, "Therefore, it had become indispensable to unfreeze, in a manner of speaking to defossilize that African history which was there at hand, lifeless, imprisoned in the documents." He describes his method plainly: "In that sense, it is a study in African historical sociology." His argument runs in steps, and his table of contents is the clearest map of it. First, he clears the ground by examining the concept of caste and the social evolution of the ancient city, and then the formation of the modern European states, so that Europe and Africa can be compared on the same terms. Second, he lays out the political organization of Black Africa: the Mossi Constitution, the Constitution of Cayor in Senegal, matrilinear succession in Ghana and Mali, and the power of the African empires, with their administrative, military and judicial organization. Third, he examines economic organization. Fourth, what he calls the ideological superstructure, Islam in Black Africa. Fifth, the intellectual level, teaching and education, including the university. Sixth, the technical level. Seventh, the migrations and formation of the African peoples of today, the Yoruba, the Peul, the Serer, the Wolof and others. His sources include the medieval Arab writers, Ibn Khaldun and Ibn Battuta, and the Timbuktu chronicles, the Tarikh es-Sudan and the Tarikh el-Fettach. He notes, for instance, that the university in Timbuktu took its name from the Sankore quarter of the city. What did he want this to do for his readers? He tells us in the same preface: "Upon absorbing any such human experience, we sense deep within ourselves a true reinforcement of our feeling of cultural oneness." And in the preface to The African Origin of Civilization he states the book’s two aims: first, "to demonstrate the possibility of writing a history of Black Africa free of mere chronology of events"; second, "to define the laws governing the evolution of African sociopolitical structures," in order "to try henceforth to dominate and master that historical process by knowledge, rather than simply to submit to it." Now sort what is established from what is debated. Established, and stated plainly: Ghana, Mali and Songhai were large, organized states, with kings and councils, governors, tax collection, armies, courts, and centres of scholarship such as Timbuktu. This is known from written sources, Arabic and African, that Diop cites. The old claim that precolonial Africa had no states, no law and no learning is false, and the documents prove it false. Diop’s first aim, a history of Africa that is more than a list of dates, he accomplished, and many have followed him. Debated, and named narrowly: his second aim, to define the laws of African social evolution, rests on a comparative sociology shaped by Marxian categories, and many historians doubt that history runs on laws of that kind at all. His last book needs its own honest word. Civilization or Barbarism: An Authentic Anthropology was published in French in 1981 and in English in 1991, five years after his death. We could not read its text, so we will not put words in his mouth. Its publisher describes it as representing "the late Senegalese scholar’s final contribution to rethinking African and world history from an Afrocentric perspective." Its catalogue contents show its range: prehistory, race and the origin of humanity; the latest discoveries on the origin of Egyptian civilization; clan and tribal organization; the birth of states; revolutions in history and the conditions for their success or failure; how to define cultural identity; Africa’s contribution to the sciences; whether an African philosophy exists; and Greek vocabulary of Black-African origin. Some of those chapters, especially the Greek vocabulary and parts of the chapters on race and origins, are disputed, and one critic, Robert Todd Carroll, called the book pseudohistory; that is his opinion, not a settled finding, and we report it as such. Now weigh the whole by the Word, and let the Word explain itself. On the record of African statecraft, Scripture was never surprised. It records an African kingdom with a royal treasury and an official over it, the Ethiopian "a man of Ethiopia, an eunuch of great authority under Candace queen of the Ethiopians, who had the charge of all her treasure" (Acts 8:27). The Word teaches that ordered government with judges in the gates is a good gift, not a European invention: "Judges and officers shalt thou make thee in all thy gates" (Deuteronomy 16:18). And it measures every kingdom, African or European, by one standard: "Righteousness exalteth a nation: but sin is a reproach to any people." (Proverbs 14:34) On Diop’s hope to master history by knowledge, the Word speaks gently but plainly. Knowledge is a gift Yahweh gives, and it is precious; the Resources He supplies for building are named together: "Through wisdom is an house builded; and by understanding it is established: And by knowledge shall the chambers be filled with all precious and pleasant riches." (Proverbs 24:3-4) But the Lord of history is not a law of social evolution, and no people masters the historical process by knowing it. "And he changeth the times and the seasons: he removeth kings, and setteth up kings: he giveth wisdom unto the wise, and knowledge to them that know understanding:" (Daniel 2:21) The empires Diop described rose and fell under that hand; so did the colonial empires that tried to erase them. And on the title of his last book, the Word answers a question the Greeks first asked and Europe repeated for centuries: who is civilized and who is a barbarian? Paul, a citizen of the Greek-speaking world, refused the line: "I am debtor both to the Greeks, and to the Barbarians" (Romans 1:14). And in Christ the wall falls entirely: "Where there is neither Greek nor Jew, circumcision nor uncircumcision, Barbarian, Scythian, bond nor free: but Christ is all, and in all." (Colossians 3:11) What does this mean for us now? It means our children should be able to name the Mossi and Cayor constitutions as easily as they name the Magna Carta, and know that Timbuktu was a city of books. It means we should read history as Diop asked, not as dry dates but as the life of real peoples with law, learning and order. It means we hold his theories as theories, and say honestly that his last book is one we have not yet read. And it means we receive Knowledge, Understanding and Wisdom as Yahweh’s Resources for building our houses and communities, while confessing that it is He, not any law of history, who removes kings and sets up kings. And His purpose in all that rising and falling is mercy. The kingdoms Diop recovered, and the empires that tried to erase them, will both be represented at the end, not as conquerors and conquered but as worshippers: "After this I beheld, and, lo, a great multitude, which no man could number, of all nations, and kindreds, and people, and tongues, stood before the throne, and before the Lamb, clothed with white robes, and palms in their hands;" (Revelation 7:9) That is why we tell this history without bitterness. The goal is not to win back a place on the world’s ladder; it is to stand, with every nation, before the Lamb.
