## 1. Dr. Chancellor Williams — the destruction, and the knowledge to rebuild

*id: `pv-williams` · 1454 words*

**The big idea.** Dr. Chancellor Williams (1893-1992), a son of Bennettsville, South Carolina, a Howard University graduate and a Ph.D. in sociology, gave sixteen years of research and field study, two of those years spent gathering oral history in 26 African nations and 105 language groups, to one question he first asked as a fifth-grade boy: how was a great Black civilization so completely destroyed? His answer has two edges. Nature, invasion, enslavement and the loss of the written record came from without; disunity, which he names the principal internal factor, came from within. Unity is strength, division is vulnerability, and a people that loses its history is in danger of losing itself.

In a small South Carolina town surrounded by cotton fields, a Black boy in the fifth grade began pestering his teachers, his preachers, his parents and his grandparents with a question none of them could answer. Chancellor Williams later wrote it down exactly as he asked it: "How is it that white folks have everything and we have nothing?" He had grown up in Bennettsville as the last of five children; his father had been born into slavery, and the family lived under the Jim Crow laws that took back the vote and the dignity that emancipation had promised. The question would not leave him. It followed him to Washington, where the family moved in 1910, through Armstrong Technical High School, through Howard University, where he earned a degree in education in 1930 and a master’s in history in 1935, and through American University, which gave him a Ph.D. in sociology in 1949 for a study of the storefront church movement. He worked as a school principal, a public-school teacher, a statistician and an economist for the federal government. He wrote novels and studies. But the boy’s question was always the real work.

The setting matters. When Williams was young, the textbooks available to Black students taught, openly or quietly, that Africa had no history worth telling. In the Preview to his great book he names the message he and his students were rebelling against, the subtle message even of the most liberal white authors and their Black followers: "You belong to a race of nobodies. You have no worthwhile history to point to with pride." That is not an exaggeration of the era; it describes the way African history was written for most of his lifetime, as the story of what Arabs and Europeans did in Africa, with Africans as the background. So the adult scholar turned the boy’s question into a sharper one. In his words, "How was this highly advanced Black Civilization so completely destroyed that its people, in our times and for some centuries past, have found themselves not only behind the other peoples of the world" and, he goes on, made the color of their skin a badge of the slave.

His answer took sixteen years. He calls his book a summary of research and field studies "which were intended for a 2-volume history of the African people." The two volumes never came; his eyesight failed, and he decided the people could not wait five more years for a larger book. His method was four steps, and he lists them in the Preview. First, a slow review of world history, European, Arab and Islamic, because the history of a race cannot be understood in isolation. Second, the study of Africa in Europe itself, in the museums and archives of the colonial powers, where, he says, much of Africa’s historical material had been hauled away. Third, documentary sources: the reports of colonial administrators, parliamentary debates, the letters of explorers and slave-ship captains, and especially the letters missionaries wrote home, with the Greek and Roman writers for the oldest records. Notice what that means. He built his case first from the records kept by the very powers that ruled Africa, sources that tell on themselves. Fourth, the field. "The field studies covered two years of work in Africa." And then the line that measures the man: "Every region of Black Africa was covered: 26 nations in East, West, Central and Southern Africa, and 105 language groups." He went to the villages far from the cities, to the elders who carried the oral tradition, and he held himself to a rule he states plainly in the section on his field studies: "The fact is that neither written nor unwritten records should be accepted as true without verification."

What did he find? His argument runs in two lines that he refuses to separate. The first line is the destruction from without. He describes the forces of nature that blockaded African progress for thousands of years, the expanding deserts, the scarcity of water, the hostile climate, disease and famine, which drove peoples to scatter and split into new languages. He describes the invaders from Asia and from Europe across centuries, the enslavement that concentrated slavery on Africans alone, and the loss of the written record through destruction, displacement and the dying out of African scribes. The second line is the one that makes him a prophetic voice rather than only an aggrieved one. Writing of the fall of the great Mwanamutapa empire in southern Africa, he says that to blame Asians and Europeans alone, or even mainly, for the destruction of all the great African states would be to ignore what he calls "the principal internal factor: disunity." And then the sentence that stings because it is meant to: "They did not have to divide and conquer even, for the Blacks were already divided, just as though they were waiting for the foreign conquerors to come." His law is the one this study has carried from the start: unity is strength, division is vulnerability.

He did not stop at diagnosis. He wrote the book, he says, so that "black John Doe, cab driver or laborer, and Jane Doe, housemaid or waitress, as well as college students and professors, could read and understand the message from their forefathers and foremothers." He gathered, from customary law across the continent, what he called the fundamental rights of the African people, the right to equal protection of the law, to a home, to land for a living, to aid in trouble, to petition for redress, to a fair hearing with no punishment greater than the offense. And in his last chapters he laid out a MASTER PLAN for organizing a scattered people for research, education and economic strength, offered not as an order from a leader but as a proposal for the people to revise. He dedicated the whole work "To the Black youth of the Nineteen Sixties for Beginning the Second Great Emancipation — the Liberation of Our Minds and thus Changing the Course of History."

Now weigh it by the Word. Williams is right, plainly right, that a people can be destroyed by the loss of knowledge, and Yahweh said it first through Hosea: "My people are destroyed for lack of knowledge" (Hosea 4:6). The record of what was taken, what was hidden and what was rewritten is not a grievance invented for the moment; it is documented, and he documented much of it from the conquerors’ own papers. He is right again about the house divided, and the Lord Jesus spoke that law before any historian found it in the ruins of an empire: "And if a house be divided against itself, that house cannot stand." (Mark 3:25). And his rule of verification is the Word’s own discipline: "Buy the truth, and sell it not" (Proverbs 23:23). Pay what the true record costs, and never trade it for a flattering story. Williams himself warned against replacing one set of racial lies with a glorified history built on wishful thinking; he wanted the whole truth, good and bad.

There is one place where the Word must answer him, and we will do it fully in the fourth lesson of his series. Near the end of the book, looking at thousands of years of conquest, he concludes that a whole race is the bitter enemy of the Blacks. The history of racial oppression he recorded is real, and nothing here softens it. But the Word names the true enemy differently: "For we wrestle not against flesh and blood" (Ephesians 6:12). The oppression was real; the people who did it were real and accountable; and the war behind it is spiritual, which is exactly why it cannot be won by turning a people into an enemy for ever. Yahweh "hath made of one blood all nations of men" (Acts 17:26).

What does it mean for us now? It means the boy’s question deserves an answer, and the answer is not shame. It means the knowledge must be recovered, taught to children, and checked, not merely repeated. It means the Body of Christ, of all people, must refuse the division that makes every household and congregation easy prey. And it means that recovering the record is the beginning, not the end; the end is a people built up together in truth, rebuilding the old waste places. In the lessons that follow we hear Williams in his own words on each part of his message: the question and the method, Egypt and Ethiopia, the enemy within, and the plan to organize. Each time we will let him speak, and each time we will let the Word have the final word.

## 2. Dr. Chancellor Williams — They lost their history: the question, the method, and the race of nobodies

*id: `pv-williams-lost-their-history` · 1709 words*

**The big idea.** Williams opens Part I of his book with an epigraph he labels a Sumer legend: a traveller asks what became of the Black people of Sumer, and an old man answers, "They lost their history, so they died." From that warning he builds his question, how a highly advanced Black civilization was so completely destroyed, and his method: world history, Africa’s own treasures studied in Europe’s museums, the colonial powers’ own documents, and two years of oral history in the field, with no record accepted without verification. The epigraph itself carries no source and its claim about Sumer is not the consensus; the warning it carries is the Word’s own.

Every book has a doorway, and Chancellor Williams chose his with care. Before the first chapter of The Destruction of Black Civilization, on the page that opens Part I, he set a short exchange that he labels a Sumer legend. A traveller asks an old man, "What became of the Black People of Sumer?" and adds, "for ancient records show that the people of Sumer were Black." The old man sighs and answers in one line that has followed readers of this book for fifty years: "They lost their history, so they died." Williams put that line at the door because it states his whole fear and his whole purpose. A people can survive war, famine and conquest, and still die as a people if they forget who they are.

We should be honest about the doorway before we walk through it. Williams gives no source for this legend anywhere in the book, and we could not find one. Most historians hold that Sumer, in southern Mesopotamia, was settled by a West Asian people who spoke Sumerian, a language that belongs to no known family, what linguists call a language isolate. So the claim that ancient records show the people of Sumer were Black is not the consensus, and we do not teach it as documented history. It is Williams’s framing. But the warning the old man speaks is not in dispute at all, because the Word said it long before any epigraph did, and we will come to that.

Now the setting. Williams finished this book as an old man, after sixteen years of research. He tells us in the Preview that it was meant to be a two-volume history, and that the writing would have needed at least five more years even without the serious failure of his eyesight. He decided the people could not wait. He had taught history classes himself, and he describes them as part of a wider rebellion against the only textbooks available, against what he calls the subtle message of even the most liberal white authors and their Black followers: "You belong to a race of nobodies. You have no worthwhile history to point to with pride." That is the cultural ground this book stands on. For most of Williams’s life, Africa was written about as a continent without history, its story told as the story of the Arabs and Europeans who came there. So he wrote quickly and plainly, so that, in his words, "black John Doe, cab driver or laborer, and Jane Doe, housemaid or waitress, as well as college students and professors, could read and understand the message from their forefathers and foremothers."

Then he tells us where the question came from. In a small South Carolina town surrounded by cotton fields, he writes, a little Black boy in the fifth grade began troubling teachers, preachers, parents and grandparents with questions none of them could answer. The first was this: "How is it that white folks have everything and we have nothing?" He asked how his people had become slaves in the first place. He asked why white children went to fine brick and stone schools nine months a year while he went to a ramshackle building for five and a half months and then to the cotton fields. In sixth grade a teacher, Miss Alice Crossland, helped him sell the Crisis and the Norfolk Journal and Guide, and the book lists in the back of the Crisis sent him reading everything he could find about Africa. What he read raised a harder question, and as a grown scholar he wrote it this way: "How was this highly advanced Black Civilization so completely destroyed that its people, in our times and for some centuries past, have found themselves not only behind the other peoples of the world" but also, he continues, branded by the color of their skin as a sign of inferiority and the badge of the slave.

His argument begins with a decision about what to study. He tells us he was concerned with Black civilization alone, with what Africans achieved on their own, apart from Europe and Asia, and that this meant separating traditional African institutions from those later shaped by Islam and by European Christianity. His reason was simple. A people cannot have a real identity with its heritage until it knows what that heritage actually was. He lists the questions that drove the sixteen years: how the record of African achievement was blotted out on so wide a scale, how a people that had once written lost the art of writing almost completely, whether the Africans are one people despite their many languages, and why there seemed to be so much disunity and mutual antagonism among them.

Then the method, in four steps, which he numbers himself. First, a slow review of world history, European, Arab and Islamic, because the history of a race cannot be understood in isolation. Second, the formal study of Africa in Europe, because the museums of the colonial powers held so much of what had been carried out of Africa; to know the scale of it, he says, you have to see it with your own eyes. Third, documentary sources: the reports of colonial administrators, parliamentary debates, the letters of geographers, explorers and slave-ship captains, and, most rewarding of all, he says, the letters missionaries wrote home, together with the Greek and Roman writers for the oldest records. This is worth underlining. Williams built his first case from records kept by the people who ruled and traded in Africa. Those are sources that tell on themselves: when a colonial report or a slave captain’s letter describes a kingdom, a court or a law, no one can say the description was invented to flatter Africans. Fourth, the field. "The field studies covered two years of work in Africa." And, he adds, "Every region of Black Africa was covered: 26 nations in East, West, Central and Southern Africa, and 105 language groups." He scheduled his visits months ahead, went into the countryside far from the westernized cities, and sought the old African tradition beneath the later layers.

He was careful about the evidence he gathered there. He noticed that many historians who dismissed oral tradition as unreliable still used it themselves when it helped them. His answer was not to trust the elders blindly or to trust the archives blindly, but to test both: "The fact is that neither written nor unwritten records should be accepted as true without verification." And he was honest about his own limits. A project that large, he says, should never have been undertaken by one person. "That was my mistake," he writes, and he says a team of trained specialists, in history, archaeology, anthropology, medicine, linguistics, agriculture and political science, should have done the work that cost him sixteen years alone.

So what did he call his reader to do? To refuse the race-of-nobodies lie. To learn the real history, good and bad. To test what is taught. And to do the work together, in teams, where he had worked alone. The rest of his book, and the rest of these lessons, are the fruit of that method.

Now weigh it by the Word. On the heart of this lesson, Williams is plainly right, and the Word is stronger than his epigraph. Moses commanded Israel: "Remember the days of old, consider the years of many generations: ask thy father, and he will shew thee; thy elders, and they will tell thee." (Deuteronomy 32:7) The psalmist tells why the record must be handed down: "That the generation to come might know them, even the children which should be born; who should arise and declare them to their children:" (Psalms 78:6) "That they might set their hope in God, and not forget the works of God, but keep his commandments:" (Psalms 78:7) The aim of remembering is not pride; it is that the next generation set its hope in Yahweh and not forget His works. And the Word shows what happens when that chain breaks: "there arose another generation after them, which knew not the LORD, nor yet the works which he had done for Israel" (Judges 2:10). That is the old man’s warning, written in Scripture. A forgetting can also come from outside, from those who hold power: "Now there arose up a new king over Egypt, which knew not Joseph." (Exodus 1:8) Oppression in Egypt began with a ruler who did not know the history.

The Word also blesses his method. Job’s friend counsels, "For enquire, I pray thee, of the former age, and prepare thyself to the search of their fathers:" (Job 8:8) That is the elder, the archive and the field study in a single verse. Searching is honorable work: "the honour of kings is to search out a matter" (Proverbs 25:2). And his rule of verification is the Word’s own rule of fairness: "He that is first in his own cause seemeth just; but his neighbour cometh and searcheth him." (Proverbs 18:17) The first account, whether a colonial textbook or a flattering legend, seems right until someone examines it. That cuts both ways, and Williams knew it. The same rule that exposes the race-of-nobodies lie also asks us to hold his unsourced epigraph loosely.

What does it mean for us now? It means our families need their records, and our churches need their archives, and both need someone willing to check them. It means the history of Africa and of its scattered children is worth studying seriously, with the colonial records, the oral tradition and modern scholarship all put to the test. It means the work should be done in teams, not by lonely heroes. And it means that the deepest history we hand down is not only what was done to us or what we built; it is what Yahweh has done, so that the generation to come might set its hope in Him. And there is mercy in that. A people told it was nobody can learn from the Word that it was never nobody to Him: the God who keeps a book of remembrance for those who fear Him has not forgotten a single name the textbooks left out.

## 3. Dr. Chancellor Williams — Ethiopia’s Oldest Daughter: Egypt, and the great deception

*id: `pv-williams-ethiopias-oldest-daughter` · 1563 words*

**The big idea.** In his chapter titled Ethiopia’s Oldest Daughter: Egypt, Williams argues that Egypt was first the north-eastern region of an ancient Ethiopian (Nile Valley) civilization, that its wealth drew peoples from Asia and later Europe into the delta, and that wherever the newcomers gained control the Blacks were pushed to the bottom of the social, economic and political ladder. He calls the later teaching of Egypt as something other than African the great deception. Established: Egypt was an indigenous African Nile Valley civilization, Kush was a great power whose kings ruled Egypt, and the Word names Cush and Mizraim as brothers. Contested: an Ethiopian empire that contained Egypt at its founding, and an all-black Egypt turned lighter by a deliberate policy.

If you want to know why Chancellor Williams wrote about Egypt the way he did, you have to remember what he was answering. When he was growing up, and through most of his career, the schoolbooks and the museum placards of the English-speaking world presented ancient Egypt as a civilization that happened to sit in Africa but was not of Africa. Its builders were drawn and described as anything but Black, and the rest of the continent was presented as a place without history. Williams named that move in the heading of the first section of his second chapter: The Great Deception. The chapter itself is titled Ethiopia’s Oldest Daughter: Egypt, and that title is his thesis.

He opens bluntly. Egypt, he writes, was the north-eastern "region of ancient Ethiopia." By Ethiopia he does not mean only the modern nation of that name; he means what the Greeks called Aethiopia, the lands of the Black peoples up the Nile, what the Bible calls Cush. He pictures the six cataracts of the Nile as the great watermarks of the heartland of the Blacks, from which culture spread across the continent, and Egypt as its northern sector, sitting at the crossroads where Asia and Europe met Africa. That location, he argues, was both Egypt’s glory and its danger. The rich soil of the delta and the valley, the great agricultural system along the flooding river, and the gold mines below the First Cataract made Egypt wealthy, and that wealth drew outsiders. He says Asians came to occupy and control Lower Egypt, the delta, before the two lands were united in the third millennium before Christ, and that as the desert spread, Africans crowded into the valley and the delta where the soil was richest.

Then comes the mechanism he believed explained everything that followed. As newcomers from Asia and later from Europe increased in number and variety, he says, the undermining of Black power accelerated, and a melting pot of peoples began around the northern edge. His summary sentence on p. 59 is this: "The end result was always the same: The Blacks were pushed to the bottom of the social, economic and political ladder whenever and wherever the Asians and their mulatto offsprings gained control." In the next chapter he argues that over time the very word Egyptian came to mean the mixed population of the north, as distinct from the Black population of the south, so that later historians could speak of the Egyptians as if they had never been African at all. That, in his view, is the great deception: not only that power shifted over the centuries, but that the record was later told as though Egypt’s founders had never been Black.

His evidence, in these chapters, is drawn from the ancient writers and from the geography and history of the Nile. In his Summing Up he pays a tribute that is worth hearing, because it shows how he weighed sources. Africa, he says, lost most of its own written records through destruction, displacement and the loss of its scribes. "Having lost most of our own written records through destruction, endless displacement, and the gradual loss of African scribes," he writes, the black world owes a debt of "everlasting thanks to those early Greek and Roman writers, but mostly Greek, who had included much of our ancient history with their own and passed it down through the centuries. We salute them!" Notice that. Williams, who was hard on European historians of his own day, salutes the ancient Europeans who wrote down what they saw. He is not anti-European in his method; he is against the deception, and he honors any witness, of any people, who told the truth.

What did he call the reader to do? To reclaim Egypt as part of African history, not as a foreign wonder that happened to stand on African soil; to see in the long story of the Nile a pattern of displacement that must be understood if it is not to be repeated; and to read the old records, the Greek ones included, for themselves.

Now weigh it by the record and by the Word, and let us be precise, because precision is how we honor him. Much of what he argued is now plainly established. Modern scholarship holds that Egyptian civilization was an indigenous development of the Nile Valley, an African civilization, not an import. Kush, with its centres at Kerma, Napata and Meroe, was a major African power, and its kings ruled Egypt as the 25th Dynasty. Upper Egypt, the south, and Nubia were close kin in culture, and the most recent volume of the UNESCO General History of Africa places Upper Egypt as an origin point of the unification of the two lands. The deception he named was real: for generations Egypt was taught as if it stood outside Africa.

Some of his specific claims, however, are contested and not the consensus, and we teach them as his. Mainstream scholars do not hold that Egypt began as a province of a larger Ethiopian empire, and they hold that Egypt’s people were varied in skin colour from the start, from the darker south to the lighter north, rather than all one colour and then changed by a deliberate mixed-race policy. His boyhood conviction, recorded on p. 18, that the very name Egypt came from the Blacks is not supported by any source we fetched, and we do not teach it.

Now the Word. Scripture puts Egypt and Ethiopia inside its story from the beginning. In the table of nations, "And the sons of Ham; Cush, and Mizraim, and Phut, and Canaan." (Genesis 10:6) In the Hebrew of that verse, Mizraim is the name of Egypt and Cush is the name the King James translators render Ethiopia. The Word does not call Egypt Ethiopia’s daughter; it calls them brothers, sons of one father. And it marks them as neighbours with a border between them: Yahweh speaks of Egypt laid waste "from the tower of Syene even unto the border of Ethiopia" (Ezekiel 29:10). On that point the Word explains the Word, and we stay with what it says.

The Word also bears witness, as a record that tells on itself, to the power of Cush in Egypt’s later history. When Assyria came against Jerusalem, Sennacherib "heard say of Tirhakah king of Ethiopia, Behold, he is come out to fight against thee" (2 Kings 19:9). That king is generally identified with Taharqa, a Kushite pharaoh of the 25th Dynasty. The UNESCO General History of Africa tells the same story from the side of modern scholarship: it calls Taharqa glorious, says the dynasty sprang from the soil of Kush itself, and notes the appeals from Jerusalem that drew the Kushites toward Asia against Assyria. The Bible was naming an African king of Kush on the world stage while the textbooks of Williams’s youth were saying Africa had no history. And it does more than name kings. Moses married a Cushite woman, and when Miriam and Aaron spoke against him, "because of the Ethiopian woman whom he had married" (Numbers 12:1), "the anger of the LORD was kindled against them" (Numbers 12:9). Ebedmelech the Ethiopian saved the prophet Jeremiah from the dungeon. An Ethiopian treasurer "had come to Jerusalem for to worship" (Acts 8:27). And the psalmist sees the future: "Princes shall come out of Egypt; Ethiopia shall soon stretch out her hands unto God." (Psalms 68:31)

That same Word guards us from turning any people into the hero or the villain of history. Egypt was Israel’s house of bondage, and yet Yahweh promises a day when He says, "Blessed be Egypt my people" (Isaiah 19:25). The Word speaks of nations and families, not of modern race categories, and it grounds all of them in one act of creation: "And hath made of one blood all nations of men for to dwell on all the face of the earth" (Acts 17:26). Numbers 12 is especially worth holding next to Williams’s chapter. He wrote with suspicion of mixed peoples as agents of displacement; Yahweh defended a Cushite wife against her husband’s own family. The injustice Williams describes, a people pushed to the bottom of the ladder, is a real wrong wherever it happens, and the Word condemns it. But the answer is not suspicion of anyone’s ancestry; it is justice, and one blood.

What does it mean for us now? It means we can say plainly what was hidden: Egypt and Kush were African civilizations, and the Bible knew it. It means we hold his contested specifics honestly, as his, without letting their uncertainty erase what is established. It means we teach our children that their history runs through the pages of Scripture, through Cush and Mizraim, Tirhakah, Ebedmelech and the Ethiopian treasurer. And it means we stretch out our own hands to Yahweh, as the psalm says Ethiopia will, not to boast in an empire, but to worship the King who made every nation and will gather them all. The prophet Zephaniah heard Yahweh say it: "From beyond the rivers of Ethiopia my suppliants, even the daughter of my dispersed, shall bring mine offering" (Zephaniah 3:10). Worshippers from beyond the rivers of Ethiopia are named in that promise, and the Ethiopian treasurer reading Isaiah in his chariot was its first fruit in the book of Acts.

## 4. Dr. Chancellor Williams — The principal internal factor: disunity, and the constitution of the villages

*id: `pv-williams-the-enemy-within` · 1602 words*

**The big idea.** What sets Williams apart is that he refused to blame outsiders alone. Writing of the fall of the Mwanamutapa empire, he says that to make Asians and Europeans solely or mainly responsible would ignore "the principal internal factor: disunity," for the conquerors did not have to divide and conquer when the people were already divided. He traces the disunity to a political tradition that kept splintering into small states and languages, and to centralization that broke faith with the people. Yet he also recovers what was good: customary laws that guarded equal protection, a home, land, aid in trouble, petition, and a fair hearing. The Word confirms the diagnosis and supplies the cure.

Most histories of a fallen people are written in one of two voices. The conqueror’s history explains that the fallen deserved to fall. The grieving history explains that the fallen were innocent and the conqueror did everything. Chancellor Williams refused both voices, and that refusal is why this study counts him among the prophetic voices. He believed that a people who blamed only their enemies would never learn the lesson that could keep them from falling again.

The setting of this lesson is late in his book, in his account of the Mwanamutapa empire of southern Africa, which he calls the last of the Black empires. Williams describes the factors that should have held it together: the roads cut through the forests that could have become permanent highways for trade and government, the similar social institutions of its peoples, and what he believed was the sameness of their constitutional system. Yet, he says, after the death of the king Matope the empire began to break up. Why? His answer is the heart of this lesson. The traditional political system, he writes, was "fundamentally and structurally anti-empire." Its very habit, the endless process "of forever splintering off to form little independent mini-states, developed a built-in disunity, reinforced by the attending growth of different languages." A group unhappy with its chief could leave and found a new village, and in time a new dialect, and in time a new people.

He sees the other side of the same coin too. Chiefs and elders in the old system were leaders, advisors and representatives of the people, he says, not their rulers. When states joined to form kingdoms and kingdoms joined to form empires, centralization tended to take the chiefs out of the people’s control and put them under the central government, and in conquered lands that change was abrupt and painful. That, he argues, was one of the principal reasons for later rebellions and the breakup of kingdoms. So the disunity he describes has two roots: a habit of splitting, and a centralizing power that broke faith with the people.

Then comes the sentence for which this chapter is remembered. To say that Asians and Europeans were solely or even mainly responsible for the destruction of all the great African states, he writes, would be to gloss over or ignore "the principal internal factor: disunity." What the foreign powers did, he says, was to study that continent-wide disunity and cash in on it. And he ends with a line meant to sting: "They did not have to divide and conquer even, for the Blacks were already divided, just as though they were waiting for the foreign conquerors to come."

He is not careless about the outside forces. In his Summing Up he lists them in order. First, what he calls "the combined forces of nature": the spreading deserts, the scarcity of water, the hostile climate and the diseases that came with famine, which scattered peoples in search of places to survive and split them into new languages and dialects. Second, the invaders from Asia and Europe, whose wars against Africa ran for thousands of years. Third, the loss of the written record. He names all of them. But having named them, he turns back to the inside. He contrasts peoples who forget their quarrels and close ranks when outsiders attack with the disunity and noncooperation he found characteristic of the societies he studied, and he says he returned to this theme again and again because it was a matter of such grave concern.

Here is what keeps the chapter from being only a rebuke. Williams also went looking for what was strong in the old societies, and he found it in their law. In chapter VI he sets out what he calls the African constitution and lists the fundamental rights of the African people, "drawn from customary laws" he gathered in the field. The list begins, "(1) The right to equal protection of the law." It includes the right to a home, to land sufficient for a livelihood, to aid in times of trouble, and "(5) The right to petition for redress of grievances." It includes the right to criticize the authorities, recognized opposition groups, and the right to withdraw from the community unmolested. On justice it says: "There must be no punishment greater than the offense, or fines beyond ability to pay." And it ends with education: "(12) The right to a general education covering morals and good manners, family rights and responsibilities" and on through the knowledge of one’s people and land. The picture is of societies where the people held real protections against their rulers. It is also, notice, the root of the very splintering he laments: a people who could always walk away were free, and they were also always dividing.

What did he call his reader to do? To stop telling the story of Africa as a story only of what others did. To see the internal factor honestly. To recover the old protections of justice and the old dignity of elders as servants. And to build a unity strong enough that no outsider could cash in on division again.

Now weigh it by the Word, and much of it stands plainly. In his own case studies, Ghana, Mali, Songhay, the Kuba kingdom, Kongo-Angola and Mwanamutapa, he traces outside pressure and inside division working together, and that two-sided picture is not in dispute. One specific claim goes further than the evidence: in his Preview he says a single constitutional system prevailed throughout all Black Africa, as if the whole race lived under one government. That is contested and not the consensus; scholars regard it as an over-generalization from his field interviews, and we teach it as his synthesis, not as settled fact.

On the diagnosis, the Word is with him, and it spoke first. The Lord Jesus said: "Every kingdom divided against itself is brought to desolation; and every city or house divided against itself shall not stand" (Matthew 12:25). Williams found that law written across the ruins of African empires; the Son of Yahweh had stated it as a law of every kingdom. And the Word even traces the same two roots Williams found. When Rehoboam came to the throne of Israel, the elders counselled him, "If thou wilt be a servant unto this people this day, and wilt serve them, and answer them, and speak good words to them, then they will be thy servants for ever" (1 Kings 12:7). He chose the young men’s counsel instead and promised a heavier yoke, and the people answered, "to your tents, O Israel" (1 Kings 12:16). The kingdom split in a day, and the two halves fought for generations while Assyria and Babylon watched. A centralizing power that broke faith with the people, and a people quick to walk away: it is Williams’s Mwanamutapa in the book of Kings.

The Word also goes deeper than history can. History can describe disunity; the Word explains where it comes from: "From whence come wars and fightings among you? come they not hence, even of your lusts that war in your members?" (James 4:1) The splintering is not finally a matter of political structure; it is a matter of the heart, pride and envy and the desire to rule one’s own small kingdom. That is why the Word’s cure is not only a better constitution, good as that would be, but a new heart and a new Head. Paul writes to a church in danger of splitting: "that there be no divisions among you; but that ye be perfectly joined together in the same mind and in the same judgment" (1 Corinthians 1:10). And he tells the Ephesians the unity is a gift to be guarded: "Endeavouring to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace." (Ephesians 4:3) The psalmist sings what it looks like when it is kept: "Behold, how good and how pleasant it is for brethren to dwell together in unity!" (Psalms 133:1)

The village constitution he recovered also has deep echoes in the Word. Moses was told to choose leaders who were "able men, such as fear God, men of truth, hating covetousness" (Exodus 18:21). The Law forbade partial judgment, rich or poor: "in righteousness shalt thou judge thy neighbour" (Leviticus 19:15). Equal protection, a fair hearing, punishment that fits the offense, aid in trouble, education in morals and family duty: these are not foreign to the Word; they are its justice, written into the customs of peoples who, as Paul says, show the work of the law written in their hearts.

What does it mean for us now? Look at our churches, our families and our communities, and ask whether we are still splintering into mini-states, every disagreement a new congregation, every grievance a new organization. Ask whether our leaders serve or rule. Name the outside wrongs honestly, as Williams did. Then do the harder thing he asked: refuse the inside division that gives every outside wrong its opening. Unity is strength; division is vulnerability. And unity among the people of Yahweh is not a political strategy; it is the prayer of Jesus Himself, that we may be one. There is grace even here. The divided kingdom of Israel did not have the last word; through Ezekiel, Yahweh promised the two broken halves, "they shall be no more two nations, neither shall they be divided into two kingdoms any more at all" (Ezekiel 37:22). What pride splits, His mercy can join. No family, church or people is too divided for Him to make one again.

## 5. Dr. Chancellor Williams — The Master Plan: organize a scattered people, and know who the enemy is

*id: `pv-williams-master-plan` · 1515 words*

**The big idea.** Williams did not end with a diagnosis. In his last chapters he offers "the specifics of a MASTER PLAN" for a unifying Race Organization: representative, cooperative rather than separatist, built in divisions that begin with directed research and training, and offered as a proposal for the people to revise rather than a leader’s decree. Along the way he states the conclusion his history had led him to, that "The white man is their Bitter Enemy," while warning that facing it does not call for increasing hatred. The Word affirms his call to organize, to plan and to lead as servants, and it answers his conclusion plainly: we wrestle not against flesh and blood, and Yahweh made of one blood all nations.

By the time a reader reaches the last chapters of The Destruction of Black Civilization, Chancellor Williams has walked through six thousand years: the Nile, the empires of the Sudan, the kingdoms of the Congo and the south, the slave trade, the colonial partition. He has named the outside forces and the inside one. And he asks the question every honest diagnosis must face: so what now? The final part of his book is his answer, and to understand it we have to hear the whole of it, the plan and the conclusion that drove it, before we weigh it by the Word.

Remember the setting. Williams finished this book in the early 1970s, an old man who had lived from the age of lynching through the civil rights years into the season of Black Power. He dedicated it "To the Black youth of the Nineteen Sixties for Beginning the Second Great Emancipation — the Liberation of Our Minds and thus Changing the Course of History." He had watched movements rise and splinter, organizations compete for the same people, and leaders build followings around themselves. He believed that history had shown him why: there had never been an organization that truly represented the people as a whole. In his summary, no such organization had ever existed, and so no real unity existed either.

His answer begins with humility about plans. Others had offered plans before him, he says, and their weakness was that instead of being offered as a proposal for the people to revise, they were "too often presented authoritatively as fait accompli, a reflection of the leader’s ego, infallible wisdom and power." Then he states his own: "In this section are the specifics of a MASTER PLAN. It is to be the functioning framework of a unifying Race Organization of a kind never presented to the black race before." He means an organization that would be truly representative, the voice of Black America, kept honest by polling the people on the questions that affect them. He insists it is not a call to leave: "Far from being a separatist movement," he says in substance, the organization would be "cooperative in the fullest and most humane meaning of that word." The Black masses, he argues, are not going to abandon four hundred years of investment in America, paid in blood and labor; they will stay and keep fighting for equal employment and advancement where they are. His chapter then turns practical, with sections headed How to Begin—And by Whom? and Structure by Divisions, and the first division he lists is a foundation for directed research, field studies and the training of workers. The man who regretted doing his research alone wanted a people that researched together.

Now the hard part, which we must not skip. Two chapters earlier, in The Black World at the Crossroads, Williams sets out the conclusion that his long study had brought him to. He says a truth may slowly emerge over centuries of evidence until it becomes impregnable, a truth so repugnant that hardly any historian would want to state it, and he states it. He recalls his earlier line that the whites are the implacable foe, the traditional and everlasting enemy of the Blacks, and he writes that the re-education of Blacks can begin only when they fully realize "this central fact in their lives: The white man is their Bitter Enemy." He calls this not wild-eyed militancy but the calm verdict of thousands of years of documented history, and he adds that his case studies of ten states show "each and every one of those states was destroyed by whites." Then, on the next page, he adds a guardrail that matters: "Facing this reality does not call for increasing hatred or screaming and utterly futile denunciations." He wanted calm, clear thinking and planning, not rage.

So what did he call his reader to do? To organize: one representative, cooperative body for a scattered people. To plan, beginning with research and training. To lead without ego, offering proposals the people could amend. To stay and build where they were. And to see clearly who had done them harm, without being consumed by hatred.

Now weigh it by the Word, and begin with what is plainly true. The history he summarized was real. The Atlantic slave trade, the colonial conquest of Africa, and the legal segregation he lived under in America were documented crimes carried out by white-ruled nations and institutions, and the Word does not ask us to pretend otherwise. Yahweh is the God who said, "I have surely seen the affliction of my people which are in Egypt, and have heard their cry by reason of their taskmasters; for I know their sorrows" (Exodus 3:7). He sees, He hears, He knows, and He judges.

The Word also affirms his call to organize and plan. "Write the vision, and make it plain upon tables, that he may run that readeth it" (Habakkuk 2:2). Jesus Himself asks, "which of you, intending to build a tower, sitteth not down first, and counteth the cost" (Luke 14:28)? Nehemiah rebuilt a wall around a defeated people because "the people had a mind to work" (Nehemiah 4:6). Williams’s warning against plans that reflect a leader’s ego is the Lord’s own rule: "whosoever will be great among you, shall be your minister" (Mark 10:43). And his refusal of separatism has a word behind it too. To exiles in a land that had conquered them, Yahweh said, "seek the peace of the city whither I have caused you to be carried away captives, and pray unto the LORD for it" (Jeremiah 29:7).

But on the conclusion that a race is the everlasting enemy, the Word speaks a different verdict, and it speaks it plainly. Paul names the real enemy: "For we wrestle not against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers, against the rulers of the darkness of this world, against spiritual wickedness in high places." (Ephesians 6:12) The oppressors were flesh and blood, accountable before Yahweh for what they did. But the war behind the oppression is older than any people and darker than any skin; it is the war of the powers of darkness against the image of Yahweh in every human being. Make a race the enemy, and you have mistaken the soldiers for the general, and you will be fighting the wrong war for ever.

The Word also denies the premise that one people is by nature the enemy of another. "And hath made of one blood all nations of men for to dwell on all the face of the earth" (Acts 17:26). In Christ the deepest enmity of the ancient world, between Jew and Gentile, was broken: "For he is our peace, who hath made both one, and hath broken down the middle wall of partition between us;" (Ephesians 2:14) "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither bond nor free, there is neither male nor female: for ye are all one in Christ Jesus." (Galatians 3:28) Notice that Paul did not pretend the enmity had never existed; he said it was slain at the cross. And Williams’s own history argues against a single enemy: his p. 283 chapter says the principal internal factor was disunity, and his own Summing Up names the forces of nature as well as invaders. His p. 310 sentence does not fit his own p. 283.

What, then, of the wrongs? The Word does not ask the wronged to forget them. It asks them to give vengeance to the only Judge who can repay justly, and to fight evil with a weapon evil cannot use. "Recompense to no man evil for evil" (Romans 12:17). "Dearly beloved, avenge not yourselves, but rather give place unto wrath: for it is written, Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord" (Romans 12:19). "Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good." (Romans 12:21) Williams was right that hatred clouds the mind; the Word goes further, and commands love for enemies, which is not weakness but the strongest strategy a people has ever been given. And this is where grace enters the hardest history. The same cross that judges every oppressor offers him mercy if he repents, and offers the oppressed a freedom no oppressor can take: freedom from the hatred that would otherwise keep them bound to the one who wronged them. That is the second emancipation finished, the liberation of the mind made whole.

What does it mean for us now? Take his plan seriously: organize, research, train, plan, and lead as servants. Take his warning about ego and division seriously. Tell the history of oppression truthfully and do not let anyone shrink it. And then refuse the one conclusion the Word refuses: no people is our everlasting enemy, because our enemy is not flesh and blood, and because Yahweh has made of one blood all nations and is gathering a people out of every one of them. That is the liberation of the mind his dedication longed for, completed by the Word.
