## 1. Dr. Myles Munroe — the Bahamian who preached the Kingdom to the nations

*id: `pv-munroe` · 1852 words*

**The big idea.** Myles Munroe was born in 1954 in Nassau, in a Bahamas that was still a British colony, and he grew up poor, one of eleven children, singing to a queen he had never seen. He read the Bible with a colonial subject’s eyes and saw in it a King, a Kingdom and a colony: Yahweh extending the rule of Heaven to the earth through people made to have dominion. From Bahamas Faith Ministries International he carried that message to the nations, the Kingdom and not a religion, dominion over the earth and never over other men, and the buried potential of every person and of every formerly colonized people, until he, his wife Ruth and seven others died in a plane crash on 9 November 2014.

Dr. Myles Munroe was born in Nassau on 20 April 1954, and the place he was born in explains almost everything he later preached. The Bahamas he was born into was not yet a nation. It had been a British crown colony since 1718. Most of its people were the descendants of Africans brought to the islands in chains. Britain abolished the slave trade in 1807 and slavery itself across its empire on 1 August 1834. In The Burden of Freedom Munroe gives 1827 as the year British slavery ended; the record says 1834, and that is what we teach, naming his figure as his own. After emancipation the islands remained a colony for more than another century. Self-rule came in stages: internal self-government in 1964, Lynden Pindling as the first Black premier in 1967, and full independence on 10 July 1973, when Myles was nineteen. He grew up a subject of a crown he never saw. In Kingdom Principles he remembers it plainly: "While I was growing up, I never visited England. I never visited Parliament or saw the queen. But like the rest of my countrymen, I knew they were there." In Rediscovering the Kingdom he recalls that "when we were a colony of the United Kingdom of Great Britain, whenever the Queen or any member of the royal family were scheduled to visit our island territory, preparations began months in advance." The streets were swept and the lights were cleaned for a sovereign who came and went. That boyhood picture of an absent king, a present governor, and a whole territory shaped by a throne it could not see became the lens through which he read the Bible. He grew up poor. In The Burden of Freedom he describes thirteen people, eleven children with their mother and father, living in "a little four-room house," pumping water by hand in the morning and bathing in a tin tub. His parents, Matt and Louise, are named in the dedication of Understanding Your Potential. Wikipedia places the family in the Bain Town district of Nassau, a detail it does not source. When the family finally built a new house on the east side of Nassau, with running water, he writes, "We had a brand-new porcelain tub, but we didn’t know how to bathe in it!" He drew from that bathroom a principle he carried for the rest of his life: "Position does not guarantee disposition." A family can be moved into a better house and still live in the old one in their heads. A nation can lower one flag and raise another and still think like a colony. That single observation is the seed of his book The Burden of Freedom, written to his own country and to every people who came out of empire. He went to Oklahoma to study, at Oral Roberts University and the University of Tulsa; his publisher’s biography says he earned bachelor’s and master’s degrees there, and Wikipedia dates them 1978 and 1980. In Overcoming Crisis he calls Oral Roberts "my father in the faith." He married Ruth in 1978; they had two children, Charisa and Myles Jr. He went home, and in the early 1980s founded Bahamas Faith Ministries International in Nassau. From that base he became a teacher of leaders heard in many nations. He wrote many books, led the International Third World Leaders Association, and was appointed an Officer of the Order of the British Empire in 1998 for services to religion, an honour from the same crown his island had once belonged to. Why does the colonial setting matter so much? Because he saw the Bible as a document about government. When he opened Genesis, he saw a King extending His rule to a new territory. When he opened the Gospels, he saw the King’s Son arriving to announce that the government had come. When he read of the Holy Spirit, he saw the Governor who stays in the territory and carries out the King’s will. He was honest that this language is uncomfortable. In God’s Big Idea he writes, "I understand that most people today think of colonization in very negative terms, particularly those who have lived under colonial rule, as I have." He did not use the image because he admired empire. He used it because he had lived under one, and he knew from the inside both what a bad kingdom takes from a people and what a good one could give. Here is the shape of his message, in summary, before the lessons that follow let him speak at length. First, the Kingdom, not a religion. He argues that religion is human beings reaching up for Yahweh, whom they cannot find, while the Kingdom is Yahweh reaching down. In Rediscovering the Kingdom he puts it in one line: "Religion, therefore, is simply man’s search for God." Jesus, in his reading, did not come to found a religion but to restore a government. Second, dominion. Yahweh made humanity to rule the earth as His representatives. Munroe pressed one detail of Genesis 1 that, for a descendant of the enslaved, is anything but abstract: "God gave man dominion over creation and earth, not other men." Third, potential. Everything Yahweh made carries more inside it than it yet shows, the way a seed holds a forest, and the tragedy of the world is gifts that are buried unused. Fourth, purpose. In Uncover Your Potential he defines it this way: "Purpose is defined as the original reason and intent for the creation of a thing." Only the Maker knows why a thing was made. Fifth, the developing nations. He dedicated Understanding Your Potential "to all the ‘Third World’ peoples throughout the world for whom I live and breathe that you may come to know Jesus Christ," and he told them, "your potential is limitless." And he called his hearers to act. He told his own nation that independence was a beginning, not an ending. In The Burden of Freedom he writes, "My own country has enjoyed emancipation from the British crown since 1973," and then he tells what happened next: "At first we had parades and danced, just like Miriam and the Israelites did on the other side of the Red Sea. But when the music died down, we saw how much we had to learn before actually becoming free." That is the burden Paul laid on the Galatians, who had been set free and were tempted back: "Stand fast therefore in the liberty wherewith Christ hath made us free, and be not entangled again with the yoke of bondage." (Galatians 5:1) His call, across every book, comes to this: seek the Kingdom first, take up the dominion Yahweh gave you over your own gifts and your own ground, renew the mind that oppression trained, and do not die with your gift still in you. Now weigh it by the Word. Begin with his centre, because it is right. Jesus’ own description of His errand was the Kingdom: "And he said unto them, I must preach the kingdom of God to other cities also: for therefore am I sent." (Luke 4:43) After His resurrection He spent forty days with His disciples "speaking of the things pertaining to the kingdom of God" (Acts 1:3). His instruction about what comes first is not vague: "But seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness; and all these things shall be added unto you." (Matthew 6:33) Munroe’s dominion teaching is the plain sense of Genesis: "And God said, Let us make man in our image, after our likeness: and let them have dominion over the fish of the sea, and over the fowl of the air, and over the cattle, and over all the earth, and over every creeping thing that creepeth upon the earth." (Genesis 1:26) And again, at the blessing: "And God blessed them, and God said unto them, Be fruitful, and multiply, and replenish the earth, and subdue it: and have dominion over the fish of the sea, and over the fowl of the air, and over every living thing that moveth upon the earth." (Genesis 1:28) Read the list. Fish, birds, cattle, the earth, every creeping thing. Another human being is not on it. Munroe is right that slavery was never in Yahweh’s design, and he is right to say so loudly, from an island built on it. Two things the Word adds, kindly and plainly. The first concerns the word religion. When Munroe says the Kingdom is not a religion, he means human effort to climb to Yahweh by ritual. That is a true contrast. But the Bible itself does not despise the word: "Pure religion and undefiled before God and the Father is this, To visit the fatherless and widows in their affliction, and to keep himself unspotted from the world." (James 1:27) What the Word condemns is the shell with nothing in it, people "Having a form of godliness, but denying the power thereof" (2 Timothy 3:5). So the line falls between living faith and dead form, not between the Kingdom and every practice of devotion. The second concerns the way in. A Kingdom message can be heard as a leadership philosophy, a set of principles that work for anyone. The Word does not allow that. No one enters the Kingdom except through the King’s Son, the Lamb of Yahweh who takes away the sin of the world. Munroe himself said so: "The cross of Christ is the beginning point of life in the Kingdom of God, not the ending point." Hold him to his own line, and the rest of his teaching stands on firm ground. On 9 November 2014, flying from Nassau to a conference in Freeport, the private Learjet carrying Myles Munroe, his wife Ruth and seven others struck a crane at the Grand Bahama Ship Yard on its approach to Grand Bahama International Airport. All nine aboard died. He was sixty. He had spent his life telling people not to take their gifts to the grave, and he went to his own still teaching, on the way to one more conference. What does he mean for us now? For Black families in America, his story rhymes with ours: a people carried across the water in chains, freed by law long before they were free in practice, and handed a hard question about what to do with freedom once it came. He did not answer that question with resentment or with escape. He answered it with the Kingdom: a King who never enslaved anyone, a dominion that was never over people, and a gift in every person that the world needs. And he answered it with stewardship, the measure the Word itself sets: "Moreover it is required in stewards, that a man be found faithful." (1 Corinthians 4:2) Not how much we were given, and not how much was taken from us, but whether we were found faithful with what is in our hands. In the lessons ahead he speaks for himself, book by book. Hear him, and weigh every line by the Word.

## 2. Dr. Myles Munroe — Rediscovering the Kingdom: the Kingdom, not a religion

*id: `pv-munroe-kingdom-not-religion` · 1640 words*

**The big idea.** In Rediscovering the Kingdom Munroe argues that religion is man’s search for Yahweh, whom he has lost, while the Kingdom is Yahweh’s answer: His government extended from Heaven to the earth. Humanity was created to rule the earth for Him; at the Fall it lost not Heaven but dominion; and Jesus came preaching one message, the Kingdom, to restore it. Those who receive Him are not adherents of a religion but citizens and ambassadors of a government.

Rediscovering the Kingdom is the book that opened Myles Munroe’s series on the Kingdom of God; its publisher later called Kingdom Principles the second book in that series. It was written for a world he believed was watching its systems fail. The publisher framed it as a guide for a time when governments collapse and philosophies fail, and its description says, "All of the past ideologies have failed; humanism, communism, totalitarianism, fascism, socialism and even democracy." That framing is the publisher’s, not his text, but it shows the question the book was meant to answer: if every human system disappoints, what government is left? Munroe’s answer is the Kingdom of Heaven, and his first move is surprising. Before he attacks any political system, he attacks religion. The pages we read are from the 2010 large-print edition, and the page numbers below are that edition’s. Here is his argument, step by step, in summary with his own lines. Step one: religion is a response to emptiness. He writes that "religion is a result of man’s response to a deep spiritual vacuum in the recesses of his soul." Human beings were made for Yahweh, lost Him, and feel the hole. Religion is what they build to fill it. He states it as a definition: "Religion, therefore, is simply man’s search for God." Then he warns that religion can do worse than fail; it can distract. In his words, "religion preoccupies you in order to distract you—to distract you from your hunger and emptiness for the Kingdom." A person can be kept so busy with religious activity that he never notices that he is still starving. Step two: the Kingdom is the end of the search. "With the Kingdom the search is over; God has revealed Himself to man and sent His Son to set us free from our sin and restore us to Himself." The movement is not upward from man but downward from Yahweh. Step three: go back to the beginning, and read it as a government. Munroe defines his terms carefully, as a man who grew up in a colony would. "Colonization is a process whereby a government or ruler determines to extend his kingdom, rulership, or influence to additional territory with the purpose of impacting that territory with his will and desires." Yahweh’s purpose in creation, he argues, was to "colonize earth with heaven and establish it as a visible territory of an invisible world." Humanity was placed on the earth as its governing authority; he writes that Yahweh "established mankind as the only legal authority on earth." He offers a long definition of dominion that begins, "To be given dominion means to be established as a sovereign, kingly ruler, master, governor, responsible for reigning over a designated territory," and he tells his readers it should be memorized. And he draws the boundary that a descendant of the enslaved could never forget: "God gave man dominion over creation and earth, not other men." Step four: the Fall was a loss of government, not of a location. "When Adam and Eve disobeyed God, they fell not from heaven but from dominion." Adam, he writes, "abandoned his position as ambassador, and lost his dominion over earth." Mankind had never lived in Heaven; it lived on the earth as Heaven’s representative, and it lost the post. Step five: Jesus came preaching one message, the Kingdom. Munroe points to the first words of Jesus’ public preaching in Matthew 4:17 and defines repentance as "a complete change of mind and thinking—a whole new mentality—and a complete change of life direction." Repentance in his reading is not only feeling sorry; it is changing governments in the mind. Then he makes the claim that most unsettles his readers: "Jesus’ primary purpose was not to heal the sick, raise the dead, cast out demons, or perform other miraculous signs. Those things were only signs that the Kingdom of God had come to earth." The miracles were real, but they were evidence, not the errand. He is just as clear that the Kingdom is entered through Jesus’ death: "The cross of Christ is the beginning point of life in the Kingdom of God, not the ending point." Step six: those who receive the King are citizens and ambassadors. Of the believer’s new identity he writes, "This is not a religious designation, but a governmental one." He illustrates from his own passport: "I am still a Bahamian citizen whether I am in the United States, Europe, or South America. Likewise, we do not have to be in heaven to be citizens there." And from diplomacy: "When we meet an ambassador, we are meeting more than just a person; we are meeting a nation." An ambassador has a discipline: "It is completely improper for an ambassador to express his personal opinion while acting in his official capacity as the representative and voice of his government." His colonial boyhood gives the illustration its weight. He remembers that "when we were a colony of the United Kingdom of Great Britain, whenever the Queen or any member of the royal family were scheduled to visit our island territory, preparations began months in advance." A people who sweep their streets for a visiting monarch know what it means to live conscious of a throne. What does he call the reader to do? First, put the Kingdom first. "This is why Jesus said the priority of all men is to seek His kingdom." Second, change the mind, not just the behaviour. Third, stop thinking of faith as a private religious hobby and start thinking of it as citizenship with duties, privileges and a King to represent. Now weigh it by the Word. Begin where he begins, with Jesus’ own preaching: "From that time Jesus began to preach, and to say, Repent: for the kingdom of heaven is at hand." (Matthew 4:17) Mark’s account says the same: "And saying, The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand: repent ye, and believe the gospel." (Mark 1:15) And in the Sermon on the Mount the order is explicit: "But seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness; and all these things shall be added unto you." (Matthew 6:33) Munroe is right that the Kingdom was Jesus’ message, first and last; Acts closes with Paul in Rome "Preaching the kingdom of God, and teaching those things which concern the Lord Jesus Christ" (Acts 28:31). He is right about citizenship and ambassadors: "For our conversation is in heaven; from whence also we look for the Saviour, the Lord Jesus Christ:" (Philippians 3:20) The old English word conversation there means citizenship, the life of a people under its government. And again: "Now then we are ambassadors for Christ, as though God did beseech you by us: we pray you in Christ’s stead, be ye reconciled to God." (2 Corinthians 5:20) Notice what the ambassador says. Not his opinion, but his King’s appeal: be reconciled to Yahweh. That is exactly Munroe’s point about an ambassador’s discipline. On the Fall, Hebrews confirms his reading and then completes it. Quoting the Psalm about man’s dominion, it says "But now we see not yet all things put under him" (Hebrews 2:8), and then immediately: "But we see Jesus, who was made a little lower than the angels for the suffering of death, crowned with glory and honour; that he by the grace of God should taste death for every man." (Hebrews 2:9) The dominion lost in Adam is restored in the Son, the Lamb of Yahweh, who tasted death for every man. Where does the Word add to what he said? In three places, gently. First, on religion. The Bible actually goes further than Munroe about man’s search: "There is none that understandeth, there is none that seeketh after God." (Romans 3:11) Left to ourselves we do not even search well; it is the Son who comes "to seek and to save that which was lost" (Luke 19:10). That strengthens his argument. But the Word does not throw away the word religion; it calls caring for the fatherless and widows pure religion and undefiled. The line falls between living faith and dead form. Second, on the Fall. The loss was dominion, yes, but the Word names something deeper: "Wherefore, as by one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin; and so death passed upon all men, for that all have sinned:" (Romans 5:12) That is why entry into the Kingdom is by new birth through the cross and not by learning Kingdom principles, which is what Munroe himself wrote on page 201. Third, on the miracles. They were signs, as he says; Jesus Himself said that if He cast out devils by the Spirit of God, the Kingdom of God had come. But the Gospels also show the heart behind them: "And Jesus went about all the cities and villages, teaching in their synagogues, and preaching the gospel of the kingdom, and healing every sickness and every disease among the people." (Matthew 9:35) And Matthew 14 says He was moved with compassion and healed their sick. The King’s signs were never mere demonstrations; they were love. What does this mean for us now? For a people who know what it is to be governed badly, Munroe’s book is good news stated in political terms: there is a good King, His government has come near, and He gives His citizens back the rule over their own lives and gifts that sin and oppression took. For a church that has sometimes become a busy building, it is a call to ask of every program whether the King’s rule is reaching anyone through it. And for each of us, it is the reminder that we are on assignment. When people meet us, they are supposed to meet a nation.

## 3. Dr. Myles Munroe — Kingdom Principles: how a kingdom works

*id: `pv-munroe-how-a-kingdom-works` · 1422 words*

**The big idea.** In Kingdom Principles, the second book of his Kingdom series, Munroe takes the Kingdom apart like a civics teacher: a king who is born, not elected, whose word is law; a Lord who owns what He rules; a territory; a commonwealth in which the King provides; and a culture His citizens learn. He argues that believers who do not understand how a kingdom works will keep living as anxious owners and religious subjects instead of secure stewards and citizens.

Kingdom Principles, published by Destiny Image in 2006, is the second book in Myles Munroe’s Kingdom series. Where Rediscovering the Kingdom argued that the gospel is a Kingdom and not a religion, this book asks the next question: how does a kingdom actually work? Its publisher lists the ground it covers: kings and lords, territory and law, citizens and royal privilege, culture and economy, and destiny. Munroe wrote it because he believed that people raised in democracies, which includes most of the believers who read him, cannot understand the language of Jesus without first understanding the language of kingdoms. He had a head start. He grew up in one. "The Bahamas, where I live, was once part of the United Kingdom of the British Empire," he writes, and he remembers, "While I was growing up, I never visited England. I never visited Parliament or saw the queen. But like the rest of my countrymen, I knew they were there." The publisher’s description opens with a sentence of his that explains the urgency behind the book: "It is my conviction that nothing is more dangerous than a destructive ideology sustained and validated by a theology." Anyone who knows the history of the Atlantic world knows how true that is; preachers quoted Scripture to defend the enslavement of Africans, as Dr. Price’s lesson in this study shows. Munroe’s answer to bad theology was not less theology but the right one: a clear picture of the King and His Kingdom from the Word. Here is his argument, step by step, in summary with his own lines. The page numbers are the 2006 edition’s. Step one: define a kingdom. "A kingdom is the governing impact of a king’s will over a territory or domain." A king’s will, a territory, and the impact of the one on the other: that is the whole thing in a sentence. Step two: a king is born, not elected. "A king is never voted into power. His power is inherent from birth. Democratic leaders are elected to power; totalitarian dictators seize power; but a king is born into power." And because his authority is inherent, his word carries the force of law: "His word is the law. His word is the government." In a democracy the constitution sits above the leader. In a kingdom, he argues, the king’s word is the constitution. Step three: King and Lord are different titles. He writes that "king relates to dominion, while lord relates to domain." A king rules a people; a lord owns property. In earthly kingdoms these could be different people. In Yahweh’s Kingdom they are one: "the King of all is also the Lord of all; He owns everything because He made everything." That is the hinge of the book, and he presses it home: "If Jesus is Lord, He must receive first priority in your life." He points to the disciple in Matthew 8 who asked to go and bury his father first, and to Jesus’ answer, follow Me. Lordship is not a compliment we pay Jesus on Sunday. It is a statement about who owns our time, our money and our decisions. Step four: territory. "The word kingdom derives from the phrase ‘king domain.’" And "Territory is vital to a kingdom because without territory no kingdom can exist." Yahweh’s territory is the earth, which He made, and He placed humanity on it to administer it for Him. Step five: the commonwealth. "The wealth in a kingdom is common." In a good kingdom the king’s wealth is the security of his citizens. Munroe describes the King’s economy in the strongest terms: "In the Kingdom of Heaven, there is no economic crisis and there are no shortages." And he names what happens when citizens forget that and act as owners: "What happens when we think of ourselves as owners? In our dog-eat-dog culture it means we feel we have to fight for what we get, hoard what we have, and guard it anxiously." The owner lives anxious; the steward lives secure, because the King is responsible for the one who manages His property faithfully. Step six: culture. "Once you understand the culture of a people, you understand the people." And: "We are born into a culture, but we are not born with a culture." His colonial boyhood is the proof. Bahamian children of African descent sang British songs, wore British clothes and learned British manners, because culture is taught by whoever governs. The Kingdom of Heaven also has a culture, and its citizens learn it. He even noted that the Bahamian constitution recognizes "the biblical principles of the historic Judaic-Christian faith," a sign that a nation can write the King into its founding words and still need to learn His ways. What does he call the reader to do? He says, "The secret to a full and fulfilled life is discovery." Discover how the Kingdom works: what citizenship means, how the King provides, how worship and protocol and representation work in His government. Then live like it: put the Lord first, manage His property faithfully, trust His provision instead of hoarding, and learn His culture until it shows. Now weigh it by the Word. The foundation is the Word’s own: "The earth is the LORD’s, and the fulness thereof; the world, and they that dwell therein." (Psalms 24:1) Yahweh says the same of His creatures: "For every beast of the forest is mine, and the cattle upon a thousand hills." (Psalms 50:10) The Psalm Munroe points to on dominion says it outright: "For the kingdom is the LORD’s: and he is the governor among the nations." (Psalms 22:28) Jesus was born a King; the wise men asked "Where is he that is born King of the Jews?" (Matthew 2:2) His word is final: "Heaven and earth shall pass away, but my words shall not pass away." (Matthew 24:35) And His own question turns lordship from a title into obedience: "And why call ye me, Lord, Lord, and do not the things which I say?" (Luke 6:46) Munroe’s ownership point carries the Word’s economics with it. Yahweh told Israel "The land shall not be sold for ever: for the land is mine; for ye are strangers and sojourners with me." (Leviticus 25:23) That is why the jubilee could return land to families every fiftieth year: no one truly owned it but Yahweh. And the measure He sets for His managers is plain: "Moreover it is required in stewards, that a man be found faithful." (1 Corinthians 4:2) Where does the Word add balance? In two places, kindly and plainly. First, kingship. Munroe’s argument that a king is better than an elected leader holds for one King only. When Israel demanded a human king like the nations, Yahweh told Samuel, "they have not rejected thee, but they have rejected me, that I should not reign over them" (1 Samuel 8:7). Human kings were often cruel; Munroe’s own island suffered under a crown. The argument is not for monarchy but for Yahweh’s rule. Second, shortages. He writes that in the Kingdom of Heaven there are no shortages. In the age to come that will be fully true. In this age the Word shows faithful believers in real need. Paul wrote, "I know both how to be abased, and I know how to abound: every where and in all things I am instructed both to be full and to be hungry, both to abound and to suffer need." (Philippians 4:12) When famine struck Judea, the answer was the commonwealth itself: "Then the disciples, every man according to his ability, determined to send relief unto the brethren which dwelt in Judaea:" (Acts 11:29) So the Kingdom does not exempt its citizens from hard times; it supplies them, often through one another. Paul taught it as a principle of equality: "that now at this time your abundance may be a supply for their want" (2 Corinthians 8:14). That is Munroe’s commonwealth, rightly read. What does it mean for us now? For families who had to fight for every inch, it is a release from the anxiety of ownership without a licence for carelessness. The King owns it all; we manage it faithfully; He provides; we share. For a church, it is a picture of what our money and buildings are for. And for anyone who was handed a twisted theology that served someone else’s power, it is the right theology in its place: a King who owns everything, enslaves no one, and governs His people for their good.

## 4. Dr. Myles Munroe — God’s Big Idea: Earth a colony of Heaven, and what the governor took

*id: `pv-munroe-colony-of-heaven` · 1781 words*

**The big idea.** In God’s Big Idea (later reissued as Reclaiming God’s Original Purpose for Your Life) Munroe argues that Yahweh’s original intent was to extend His Kingdom from Heaven to the earth, not by conquest but by planting a garden and filling it with people who would live Heaven’s life on earth. The Fall was a declaration of independence that let a pretender seize the colony; and from his Bahamian boyhood he shows what a bad governor takes from a people, their language, their history and their culture, as a picture of what sin stole from humanity and what the Kingdom restores.

God’s Big Idea was published by Destiny Image and later reissued in 2012 as Reclaiming God’s Original Purpose for Your Life. It is the book in which Myles Munroe most fully joins his reading of Genesis to his own colonial boyhood, and it is where he says most plainly what he believed the whole Bible is about. In his introduction he asks, "What is this idea?" and answers, "It’s the divine conception of the colonization of Earth by the Kingdom of Heaven." The aim of that colonization, he writes, is "producing a colony of citizens who exhibit the nature, values, morals, and lifestyle of Heaven on Earth. This is not a religious idea but a global invasion of love, joy, peace, goodness, kindness, justice, patience." To understand why a Bahamian would choose that word, colony, remember where he was raised. His islands were a British crown colony from 1718 until independence on 10 July 1973. He was nineteen when the flag changed. In this book he speaks of more than two centuries of governors who administered the islands in the name of a crown. So when he uses the word colony for Yahweh’s plan, he is not naive about it. He uses it the way a survivor uses the name of the thing that hurt him: carefully, and in order to say what it was a counterfeit of. Here is his argument, step by step, in summary with his own lines. Step one: kingdoms expand. Historically, rulers have extended their realms "by one of two means: outright conquest or colonization." Yahweh, the Creator of all, chose to extend His rule from the invisible realm to the visible earth by establishing an outpost of Heaven there. Step two: He did it with a garden. Munroe notes that Yahweh did not use war or a code of laws to begin with. In Munroe’s words, "when God set out to bring Heaven to Earth," then "He planted a garden." The garden was Heaven’s first territory on earth, and the man and woman were placed in it to tend it and to extend it until the whole earth bore Heaven’s life. Step three: he faces the word head on. "Simply stated, God’s original intent was to make Earth a colony of Heaven. I understand that most people today think of colonization in very negative terms, particularly those who have lived under colonial rule, as I have." He then names what human colonialism has meant: "coercion, brutality, greed, exploitation, victimization, and oppression." And he refuses to let anyone blame Yahweh for it. Those traits, he writes, "reflect the nature and tactics of satan, the original enemy of humanity, who illegally seized control of God’s original garden ‘colony’." In other words, the colonial history of the Caribbean and Africa was not Yahweh’s design in miniature; it was the pretender’s imitation of a design he had stolen. Step four: gardens grow quietly. Like a garden transforming the ground around it, the influence of the Kingdom on earth, he writes, "grows gradually and often invisibly." He points to Jesus’ parables of the yeast in the dough and the mustard seed. The Kingdom does not arrive like an invading army. It spreads like a living thing. Step five: the Fall was a revolt against a government. Adam and Eve’s disobedience "was, in fact, a declaration of independence from God and His righteous, loving, and benevolent rule." Mankind decided to govern itself, and in the vacuum the pretender seized the throne of the earthly colony. Step six: what a bad governor takes. Here he turns to his own life, and the book becomes testimony. He writes that "a succession of governors took away from us three important ingredients of our identity as a people." The first was language. Most Bahamians are of African descent, but, he writes, "We speak ‘the King’s English’." The second was history: "instead of learning about Shaka Zulu and other leaders and events of African history and heritage, we learned about King Henry VIII and his six wives." The third was culture: "we began each school day waving little British flags and singing, ‘Rule Britannia.’ We sang to a queen and country we had never seen." He does not say every colonial officer was evil. He says the system, simply by doing its work, stripped a people of themselves. Then he lays that picture over the Fall. The pretender, he argues, took the same three things from the whole human race. He took our language with Yahweh, the open, intimate speech Adam had with his Maker. He took our history, the knowledge that we were made in Yahweh’s image to rule the earth with Him. And he took Heaven’s culture, putting his own in its place. Step seven: the Kingdom culture. "It was never God’s desire or intent that there be a British culture, or an American culture, or a Bahamian culture," he writes. "He wanted a Kingdom culture, one culture throughout the entire created realm." What does he call the reader to do? To recognize which governor has been shaping him, to receive back from Jesus what the pretender took, and to take part in the planting. His publisher describes readers becoming "directly involved in God’s Big Idea by helping plant and multiply His garden communities." Now weigh it by the Word. The garden is the Word’s own beginning: "And the LORD God planted a garden eastward in Eden; and there he put the man whom he had formed." (Genesis 2:8) And the man’s commission there: "And the LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to dress it and to keep it." (Genesis 2:15) The aim of the whole story is Heaven’s will on earth; Jesus taught us to pray for it: "Thy kingdom come. Thy will be done in earth, as it is in heaven." (Matthew 6:10) He described the Kingdom’s quiet growth in the words Munroe cites: "Another parable spake he unto them; The kingdom of heaven is like unto leaven, which a woman took, and hid in three measures of meal, till the whole was leavened." (Matthew 13:33) And the prophets saw where the garden is going: "For the earth shall be filled with the knowledge of the glory of the LORD, as the waters cover the sea." (Habakkuk 2:14) Munroe is right that the Fall was a grab for self-rule. The tempter’s offer was exactly that: "ye shall be as gods, knowing good and evil" (Genesis 3:5). Isaiah describes the result in every life since: "All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned every one to his own way; and the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all." (Isaiah 53:6) And Munroe is right that what was taken is given back in Jesus. The lost language with the Father is restored: "For ye have not received the spirit of bondage again to fear; but ye have received the Spirit of adoption, whereby we cry, Abba, Father." (Romans 8:15) Even his colony picture has a foothold in Scripture. Paul wrote to the believers at Philippi that their citizenship is in Heaven, and Acts calls their city a colony: "Philippi, which is the chief city of that part of Macedonia, and a colony" (Acts 16:12). Still, the Word’s own words for the believer are kingdom, citizen and ambassador, and those are the words to lead with; colony is his illustration. Where does the Word correct him? In one phrase, and it matters. He hoped for "one culture throughout the entire created realm." The Word shows the Kingdom’s end differently: "After this I beheld, and, lo, a great multitude, which no man could number, of all nations, and kindreds, and people, and tongues, stood before the throne, and before the Lamb, clothed with white robes, and palms in their hands;" (Revelation 7:9) The nations are still nations before the throne. Their tongues are still tongues. And "And the nations of them which are saved shall walk in the light of it: and the kings of the earth do bring their glory and honour into it." (Revelation 21:24) The Kingdom’s values are one; Paul says the Kingdom of God is righteousness, and peace, and joy in the Holy Ghost. But its peoples are many, and their glory is brought in, not erased. Munroe’s own testimony proves why this matters. The colonial governor’s sin was precisely that he replaced Shaka Zulu with Henry VIII. A Kingdom that flattened every people into one culture would be doing the same thing on a grander scale. The true King does not. When the Spirit came at Pentecost, every man heard the apostles speak in his own language. He redeems languages; He does not abolish them. There is grace in this history too, and it must be told as plainly as the loss. The islands that sang to a queen they had never seen also heard the gospel, and many of their people came to sing to the King of kings; Munroe himself was raised in a Christian home in that colony. Yahweh was at work inside a system that took much from His people. And Munroe carried no contempt for the British people. He says plainly that he is not accusing every colonial official of evil intent, and in 1998 he accepted an honour from the crown his islands had once belonged to. That is what mercy looks like after long injustice: the wrong named without softening, and the people who did it not hated. Joseph said it to the brothers who sold him: "But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive." (Genesis 50:20) Naming what was stolen and refusing bitterness are not opposites. Paul joins them: "Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good." (Romans 12:21) What does it mean for us now? For Black families on both sides of the water, whose languages, histories and cultures were taken by one governor or another, Munroe’s book offers more than a lament. It tells us who the real Governor is, and it tells us what He gives back: speech with the Father, the truth of where we came from and whose image we bear, and a way of life that is Heaven’s own. It also gives us work. Plant the garden where you are. Tell your children the history the schoolroom left out, and tell it under the King who made all nations of one blood. And bring what is good in your heritage, cleansed and redeemed, to lay before the throne with every other nation.

## 5. Dr. Myles Munroe — The Holy Spirit, Governor of the Kingdom on earth

*id: `pv-munroe-the-governor` · 1537 words*

**The big idea.** Munroe taught that the Holy Spirit is the Governor of Yahweh’s Kingdom on earth: the Master Gardener of Eden, who carries out the will of the King, speaks only what the King says, and transforms the earthly realm into the heavenly. Adam lost that relationship when he abandoned his post; in Jesus the Spirit returned in full; and unlike the colonial governor in Nassau’s pink house, this Governor can never be evicted, because He lives in the hearts of the King’s citizens.

Myles Munroe’s teaching on the Holy Spirit grew out of a building he could picture from boyhood: the large pink house on Duke Street in Nassau where the British governor of the Bahamas lived. The Bahamas was a crown colony until independence on 10 July 1973, and for all those years the monarch was far away in England. Most Bahamians would never see her. But the governor lived among them, and in his person the crown’s authority was present every day. Munroe remembered the yearly gathering in a park by the beach for the Speech from the Throne. A red ceremonial chair was set out with a crown behind it, and the governor sat in it and read the words of the king or queen. He never told the people anything the monarch had not written, and he never spoke on his own authority. When Munroe read what Jesus promised about the Holy Spirit, he recognized the picture at once. In God’s Big Idea, chapter 4, “The Master Gardener,” and in Rediscovering the Kingdom, he built a teaching on it; he later devoted a whole book, The Most Important Person on Earth, to the same subject, which its publisher describes as the Holy Spirit being the presence and power of Heaven in the world. Here is his argument, step by step, in summary with his own lines. Step one: the Spirit was the Governor from the beginning. In God’s Big Idea he writes that "the Holy Spirit was the Master Gardener of Eden, the Governor of God’s original colony on Earth. Like human colonial governors, He guides and oversees the lives and welfare of His citizens." Adam and Eve tended the garden, but the Master Gardener was the Spirit, who had been present at its making. Step two: a governor executes the king’s will. "As Governor and Master Gardener, the Holy Spirit’s primary role is to execute and enact the will of the King who owns the Garden." Munroe contrasts this with democratic government. An elected governor answers to voters, who can remove him. A kingdom governor answers to the king alone. He illustrates with Pontius Pilate, appointed by the emperor to govern Judea: the people could not vote him out, only petition the emperor to recall him, which Munroe dates to A.D. 36. Step three: the Governor’s aim is transformation. "His major role is to transform the earthly realm into the heavenly realm." The point of a colonial governor was to make the colony reflect the home country. The point of the Holy Spirit is to make the earth, beginning with human lives, reflect Heaven. Step four: no one can know the King’s will without Him. "Man cannot know God’s will except through the Holy Spirit living within him, and the Holy Spirit can live only in a holy vessel." Step five: Adam lost this relationship. "Through abdication of his responsibility as king over earth, Adam lost the most important relationship of all—the Holy Spirit." The Fall, in Munroe’s reading, was not first the loss of a place but the loss of the Governor’s presence, and with it the knowledge of the King’s will. Step six: Jesus restored it. "In Jesus Christ, the Holy Spirit returned to earth to dwell in full force in a human being for the first time since leaving Adam and Eve in the Garden of Eden." What Jesus had, He then promised to His followers. Step seven: this Governor can never be sent home. Here Munroe returns to Duke Street. "The colonial governor of the Bahamas lived in a large pink house built by the British, and when we declared our independence, he returned to England. The Governor of God’s Kingdom on Earth, on the other hand, will never be evicted and sent home." He does not live in a physical building; He dwells in the hearts and lives of the King’s citizens. A people can lower a flag and send a governor away. No one can evict the Holy Spirit from the people of the King. What does he call the reader to do? He asks a single question, and he means it to be answered daily: "Who is tending your garden?" A human life is a garden. Either the Master Gardener tends it, growing the King’s fruit, or the pretender is allowed to fill it with the weeds of fear, hatred and lust. Munroe’s call is to yield the ground to the Governor, to let Him reveal the King’s will, and to live as a colony of Heaven in which His fruit is visible. Now weigh it by the Word. Jesus’ own promise is the foundation: "And I will pray the Father, and he shall give you another Comforter, that he may abide with you for ever;" (John 14:16) And: "Even the Spirit of truth; whom the world cannot receive, because it seeth him not, neither knoweth him: but ye know him; for he dwelleth with you, and shall be in you." (John 14:17) Munroe’s picture of a Governor who speaks only the King’s words is almost exactly Jesus’ description: "Howbeit when he, the Spirit of truth, is come, he will guide you into all truth: for he shall not speak of himself; but whatsoever he shall hear, that shall he speak: and he will shew you things to come." (John 16:13) The governor in the red chair read the monarch’s speech and added nothing; the Spirit of truth speaks what He hears. Jesus also said the Spirit would teach and remind: "But the Comforter, which is the Holy Ghost, whom the Father will send in my name, he shall teach you all things, and bring all things to your remembrance, whatsoever I have said unto you." (John 14:26) And He would empower the King’s ambassadors: "But ye shall receive power, after that the Holy Ghost is come upon you: and ye shall be witnesses unto me both in Jerusalem, and in all Judaea, and in Samaria, and unto the uttermost part of the earth." (Acts 1:8) The Spirit was there at the beginning, as Munroe says; Genesis shows Him moving upon the face of the waters. And the Spirit governs a life, not by force but by leading: "For as many as are led by the Spirit of God, they are the sons of God." (Romans 8:14) His harvest is the very fruit Munroe called Heaven’s culture: "But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith," (Galatians 5:22) Where does the Word guard the teaching? In three places, plainly and with respect for what he was reaching for. First, rank. In every earthly empire a governor is a lesser official than the king. The Holy Spirit is not lesser than the Father or the Son. When Ananias lied to the Holy Ghost, Peter told him, "thou hast not lied unto men, but unto God" (Acts 5:4). Jesus sent His disciples to baptize in the one name "of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost" (Matthew 28:19). Munroe himself confessed one God in three Persons, so this is not a correction of his faith but a guardrail on his picture: the governor image shows the Spirit’s work in the Kingdom, never His rank. Second, history. Munroe says the Spirit returned in Jesus for the first time since leaving Adam and Eve. The Word records the Spirit coming upon many before Jesus: "the Spirit of the LORD came upon David from that day forward" (1 Samuel 16:13), and David prayed, "Cast me not away from thy presence; and take not thy holy spirit from me." (Psalms 51:11) Genesis tells us Yahweh breathed into Adam the breath of life; it does not say the Spirit dwelt in Adam and departed. So that part of his claim goes beyond the text. What is truly new in Jesus is what his own phrase in full force points toward: "For he whom God hath sent speaketh the words of God: for God giveth not the Spirit by measure unto him." (John 3:34) And after Jesus was glorified, the Spirit was given to dwell in every believer, not upon a few leaders only, as Joel foretold. Third, the holy vessel. Munroe is right that the Spirit is holy and makes holy. But the Word never asks us to make ourselves holy first so that He can come. Believers are sealed with the Holy Spirit of promise when they believe, cleansed by the blood of the Lamb, and the Spirit then does the transforming. What does it mean for us now? For a people who have known governors who came to take, Munroe’s teaching is good news in their own language. The Governor Jesus sent comes to give: truth, power, comfort, fruit and freedom. "Now the Lord is that Spirit: and where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is liberty." (2 Corinthians 3:17) He cannot be bribed, outvoted or sent home. He speaks only what the King says, which means that no one who claims to speak for Him can add to the King’s word. And He asks for the ground of our lives. Who is tending your garden? Every morning is a chance to answer.

## 6. Dr. Myles Munroe — Understanding Your Potential: the forest in the seed, and the wealth of the graveyard

*id: `pv-munroe-forest-in-a-seed` · 1680 words*

**The big idea.** In Understanding Your Potential Munroe argues that Yahweh created everything with more inside it than it yet shows: a seed holds a forest, a boy a man, a nation a generation. Potential is what you are yet able to do, nothing in life is instant, and the richest ground on earth is the cemetery, full of songs never sung and books never written. He calls every reader, and above all the poor and formerly colonized peoples he dedicated the book to, to stop burying their gifts and to give the world what Yahweh placed in them.

Understanding Your Potential is the book that made Myles Munroe’s name known to millions, and it is the one he wrote with his own people most clearly in view. He dedicated it "to all the ‘Third World’ peoples throughout the world for whom I live and breathe that you may come to know Jesus Christ," and to his parents and his ten brothers and sisters. He wrote it as a Bahamian who had grown up poor in a colony, among people who had been told for generations, in a hundred quiet ways, that they had little to offer. The book is his answer: Yahweh put more in you than anyone has yet seen, including you. He opens not with a sermon but with a cemetery. He writes that "the richest deposits on our planet lie just a few blocks from your house. They rest in your local cemetery or graveyard. Buried beneath the soil within the walls of those sacred grounds are dreams that never came to pass, songs that were never sung, books that were never written." Then: "Our graveyards are filled with potential that remained potential. What a tragedy!" And the challenge that frames the whole book: "You must decide if you are going to rob the world or bless it with the rich, valuable, potent, untapped resources locked away within you." Here is his argument, step by step, in summary with his own lines. Step one: Yahweh built potential into creation. Among the principles at the end of his first chapter is the plain statement, "God created everything with potential." He grounds it in Genesis, where Yahweh made each plant with its seed inside itself. Nothing Yahweh made was finished at the moment of its making; it carried a future within it. Step two: what potential is. "Potential is therefore not what you have done, but what you are yet able to do." It is not your résumé. It is the part of you not yet spent. Step three: the forest in the seed. This is the image everyone remembers. He imagines holding up a seed and asking what is in his hand. The obvious answer is a seed. "However, if you understand the nature of a seed, your answer would be fact but not truth. The truth is I hold a forest in my hand. Why? Because in every seed there is a tree, and in every tree there is fruit or flowers with seeds in them." Fact is what you see; truth is what Yahweh put there. And the principle runs through all of life. In his introduction he lists it across creation, a flock in every bird, a herd in every cow, a generation in every nation, and he names the tragedy: "Tragedy strikes when a tree dies in a seed, a man in a boy, a woman in a girl, an idea in a mind." Step four: nothing is instant. "People think miracles are instant, but they really are not. They are just a process that has been sped up." Yahweh did not give the earth an instant population. He began with one man, one seed, and let it grow. So the seed in you will not become a forest overnight, and slowness is not failure. Step five: success is a danger. "One of the great enemies of your potential is success." In his chapter principles he puts it even more sharply: "The greatest threat to progress is your last successful accomplishment." And: "Potential never has a retirement plan." Step six: your gift is owed to others. "I believe there are books, songs, art works, businesses, poems, inventions, and investments in you that God intended for my children to enjoy. Don’t give up until you have lived out the full extent of your potential, because you have no right to die with my things." That heading, Don’t Die With My Things, became one of the most quoted lines of his life. His evidence and examples are stories. The introduction opens on a Caribbean island where an old village sculptor passes a plantation owner throwing out an old tree stump. The sculptor asks for a piece of the junk wood, carves from it a beautiful bird, and when the owner later comes by and buys the bird at whatever price the sculptor names, the sculptor reflects, "Junk is in the eyes of the beholder. Some look, but others see." The plantation setting is not accidental. Munroe’s readers in the Caribbean knew whose eyes had called whom worthless. He also points to Abraham Lincoln’s long run of defeats before his election in 1860, to the parable of the talents, to Jesus as the grain of wheat that falls into the ground, and to Paul finishing his course. One supporting claim needs a plain flag: he writes that scientists have estimated that we use as little as ten percent of our abilities. He gives no source for that figure, and we found none; it is a popular saying, and it should be heard as his illustration, not as measured science. His conclusion does not depend on it. What does he call the reader to do? To refuse to be satisfied with what he now is; to keep growing after every success; to stop burying the gift out of fear; and, for whole peoples, to stop waiting for others to supply what Yahweh already planted in them. In his closing chapter, written to the developing world, he says, "Just as there is a forest in every seed, so I am certain there is a new world within your world. Whatever God calls for, He provides for." Now weigh it by the Word. His foundation is in Genesis: "And God said, Let the earth bring forth grass, the herb yielding seed, and the fruit tree yielding fruit after his kind, whose seed is in itself, upon the earth: and it was so." (Genesis 1:11) And: "And the earth brought forth grass, and herb yielding seed after his kind, and the tree yielding fruit, whose seed was in itself, after his kind: and God saw that it was good." (Genesis 1:12) He is right that growth is a process; Jesus described the Kingdom itself growing that way: "For the earth bringeth forth fruit of herself; first the blade, then the ear, after that the full corn in the ear." (Mark 4:28) He is right to warn against despising small beginnings; the prophet asked "For who hath despised the day of small things?" (Zechariah 4:10). He is right that buried gifts are judged. In the parable of the talents, the servant who received one talent explained himself: "And I was afraid, and went and hid thy talent in the earth: lo, there thou hast that is thine." (Matthew 25:25) The master’s rebuke fell on fear dressed up as caution. And the Preacher gives Munroe’s cemetery its plainest commentary: "Whatsoever thy hand findeth to do, do it with thy might; for there is no work, nor device, nor knowledge, nor wisdom, in the grave, whither thou goest." (Ecclesiastes 9:10) The gifts are meant to be spent on others, as Munroe said; Peter writes "As every man hath received the gift, even so minister the same one to another, as good stewards of the manifold grace of God." (1 Peter 4:10) Where does the Word complete what he said? In three places, and each one is a mercy. First, the seed must die. Munroe cites Jesus as the kernel of wheat, and the verse itself sets the terms: "Verily, verily, I say unto you, Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die, it abideth alone: but if it die, it bringeth forth much fruit." (John 12:24) Potential in the Kingdom is not released by self-assertion or by chasing your own dream. It is released by surrender, by losing your life for Jesus’ sake. A reader who hears Understanding Your Potential as a self-help book has missed the seed’s death. Second, the treasure is His. "But we have this treasure in earthen vessels, that the excellency of the power may be of God, and not of us." (2 Corinthians 4:7) Jesus said "without me ye can do nothing" (John 15:5). Munroe agrees; later in the same book he writes, "The secret to a happy and productive life is remaining attached to your Source." Hold the forest image together with that line, always. Third, the measure is faithfulness, not size. The master gave five, two and one talents "to every man according to his several ability" (Matthew 25:15), and his praise to the faithful was the same: "His lord said unto him, Well done, thou good and faithful servant: thou hast been faithful over a few things, I will make thee ruler over many things: enter thou into the joy of thy lord." (Matthew 25:21) No one will be asked why he was not someone else. He will be asked what he did with the seed he was given. And there is grace for the graveyard. Many whose potential lies buried did not bury it themselves. The Word does not forget them. Hebrews says of the faithful who died without receiving the promise that Yahweh provided some better thing, that they without us should not be made perfect. What they could not finish, their children and grandchildren may yet carry. And for the living who feel the years are gone, Yahweh told His people He would restore the years the locust had eaten. What does it mean for us now? For Black families on both sides of the water, this book speaks to a long history of being looked at and called junk. Munroe’s answer is the sculptor’s eyes, which are Yahweh’s eyes: some look, but others see. It means looking at our children, our neighbours and ourselves with those eyes. It means planting what is in our hands now, however small, and trusting the King with the growth. And it means not dying with His things, but leaving them, sung and written and built, for the generation that comes after.

## 7. Dr. Myles Munroe — The Source and the Manufacturer: remaining attached

*id: `pv-munroe-the-source` · 1646 words*

**The big idea.** In Understanding Your Potential Dr. Myles Munroe argued that nothing can live, grow, or become what it was made for once it is cut off from where it came from. Plants live from the soil, branches from the vine, and people from Yahweh who made them. He pictured Yahweh as the Manufacturer who wrote the manual and stands behind the warranty: stay related to Him and keep His specifications, and the potential He built into you is released; leave Him, and you are feeding on a false vine.

Dr. Myles Munroe wrote Understanding Your Potential as a young pastor in Nassau, in a nation that had been independent of Britain only since 1973, after more than two centuries as a crown colony. He dedicated it to the people he would spend his life on, in his words, "to all the 'Third World' peoples throughout the world for whom I live and breathe that you may come to know Jesus Christ." That is the setting for this lesson. He was writing to people who had been told for generations, by a colonial order and by the poverty it left behind, that what they could become was decided somewhere else. His answer was not that they were self-made. It was that they were made, by Someone, and that the way to become what you were made for is to stay joined to the One who made you.

The first half of the book argues that everything Yahweh made carries hidden potential, the forest inside the seed. Chapter 9 asks the question that must come next: if that potential is real, what keeps it alive? He sets one sentence at the head of the chapter as its thesis: "The secret to a happy and productive life is remaining attached to your Source."

He builds the argument in five steps.

The first step is the vine. He describes a grape vine as a farmer would: the thick, woody stem running up the pole holds all the life, and the small green branches holding the grapes have no root of their own. Break one off and plant it and it will never grow, because the life was never in it. From this he draws a rule he says runs all through creation: "Life is not possible when a thing is separated from its source."

The second step is a close reading of Genesis 1. He notices a pattern in how Yahweh creates. When Yahweh wanted plants, He spoke to the soil, and plants came out of the soil. When He wanted animals, He spoke to the ground. Whatever Yahweh speaks, he argues, comes out of what He spoke to. Then the pattern breaks on the sixth day. "Because God wanted man to come out of Himself, He spoke to Himself when He created man." He is pointing to the Let us make man in our image of Genesis 1:26, the only creature Yahweh did not command the earth to bring forth.

The third step is that source sets the ceiling. "Everything is made up of the same stuff as what it came out of," he writes, and so a plant can be no more than the soil and an animal no more than the ground. Then the line on which he builds the chapter: "Since you came out of God, you have the same components as God and your potential is determined by God." He turns it into a warning. A plant that says it is tired of the soil has really decided to die. An animal that refuses to eat what comes from the ground has decided to die. And a person who decides he does not need Yahweh, he argues, has decided never to become all he was made to be.

The fourth step is the false vine. He reads Jesus calling Himself the true vine and asks why the word true is there. Because, he says, there are a lot of other vines to plug into: education, philosophy, science, even religion. He was a university-trained man and never despised learning. His point is about feeding. Whatever you feed on alone sets your limit. Feed only on education and you will have what education can give, an intellectual stimulation, and no matter how many degrees you collect, you will be living below what you were made for.

The fifth step is the Manufacturer, and it is the picture people remembered. Every appliance, he says, comes with a manual. The first page says to read it before plugging the product in. It names the parts and what each can do. It tells you what to expect from the product. And it warns that if anything goes wrong you must not fix it yourself; take it to a technician certified by the manufacturer, or the warranty is void. He applies it to us. Humanity is an excellent, intricately built piece of equipment that does not know what it can do, so the Maker sent a manual. When the equipment broke, we did not take it back to the Manufacturer. We took it to an unauthorized dealer with no genuine parts, and he names that dealer satan. Yet Yahweh, though the warranty had run out, took the product back and is rebuilding it with His own parts. In chapter 11 the warranty becomes the first of his ten keys: "God guarantees the maximum performance of our potential if we remain related to Him and submit to the conditions, specifications, and standards set by Him."

The ten keys are his practical answer to the question of how. He lists them as commands. You must know, which he explains as be related to, your Source. You must understand how the product was designed to function, and he says we were designed to run on faith and love, while fear and hatred short-circuit us. You must know your purpose, because, as he puts it, "Purpose gives birth to responsibility, and responsibility makes demands on potential." You must understand your resources, which were made to live on and with, never for. You must have the right environment, and he names it as the garden, meaning Yahweh’s presence, restored to us by Jesus and kept within us by the Holy Spirit. You must work out your potential, and he notes that the first thing Yahweh gave Adam was not a wife but work. You must cultivate it, guard it, and share it, because nothing in creation exists for itself: the sun does not shine for the sun. And you must know the laws of limitation, which he says are given not to restrict but to protect, so that obedience becomes the protection of potential. His closing call is simple: commit yourself to obeying the Manufacturer, and watch what He built into you come out.

Now weigh it by the Word, because the Word explains the Word.

On his main point he is plainly right, and Jesus said it with more force than he did. "I am the vine, ye are the branches: He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing." (John 15:5) Not less fruit; nothing. Jesus also said, "As the branch cannot bear fruit of itself, except it abide in the vine; no more can ye, except ye abide in me" (John 15:4). When Paul stood among the philosophers of Athens, men who had every vine of learning the ancient world could offer, he told them, "For in him we live, and move, and have our being" (Acts 17:28). The Manufacturer picture is also the Bible’s own, in the potter and the clay: "Shall the clay say to him that fashioneth it, What makest thou?" (Isaiah 45:9) And the Word confirms his point about work: "And the LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to dress it and to keep it." (Genesis 2:15)

There is one phrase where the Word corrects his wording, kindly and plainly. Saying that because we came out of God we have the same components as God reaches past what Scripture says, and so does his line elsewhere in the chapter that we will live forever because it is our nature. The Word tells us exactly how man came to be: "And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul." (Genesis 2:7) We are dust, formed by His hands, made alive by His breath, and made in His image, which means likeness, representation and relationship. We are not portions of His being. Paul says Yahweh is the one "Who only hath immortality" (1 Timothy 6:16), and that "the gift of God is eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord" (Romans 6:23). Life forever is His gift, not our nature.

Notice what that correction does. It does not weaken Dr. Munroe’s teaching; it makes it stronger. If we carried life in ourselves, we might manage on our own for a while. But a creature formed from dust has no life of its own at all. Everything it has is breath on loan. That is exactly why remaining attached is not one spiritual habit among others. It is the difference between a branch and a cutting.

What does it mean for us now? It means naming our false vines honestly. For one generation it was the plantation owner’s approval; for another it was the government job; for ours it may be the credential, the paycheck, the platform, the phone that is the first thing we touch every morning. Some of these are good gifts. None of them is the Source. It means reading the Manual as though the Maker wrote it for the product in your hands, because He did. It means that when something in us breaks, we stop carrying it to unauthorized dealers, whether that is a bottle, a bitterness, or a voice that tells us who we are without asking who made us. And for a people who were told for centuries that their worth was set by others, it means hearing the oldest and best news there is: your worth was set by the One who formed you and breathed into you, and the way to become all He intended is to stay joined to Him, every day, on purpose.

## 8. Dr. Myles Munroe — Releasing and Maximizing Your Potential: work, faith, and dying empty

*id: `pv-munroe-die-empty` · 1560 words*

**The big idea.** In Releasing Your Potential and Maximizing Your Potential, Dr. Myles Munroe argued that the gifts Yahweh puts in a person are revealed only when demands are made on them, that faith and work are the two ways potential is drawn out, and that potential is given to be shared with others and passed to the next generation. His famous charge was to die empty: to spend everything Yahweh deposited in you before you leave, so that nothing He meant for others is buried with you.

Dr. Myles Munroe published Understanding Your Potential in the early 1990s, and the response to it carried him into a series. Releasing Your Potential and Maximizing Your Potential, the latter published by Destiny Image in 1996, take the claim of the first book, that Yahweh buried a forest in every human seed, and press the practical questions. How does the potential actually come out? What draws it? What blocks it? And what happens if a person goes to the grave with it still inside? His publisher summed up the series’ heartbeat in its own description of Maximizing: a book to give readers "a reason to die empty" and to teach them to "die finished, not prematurely." The phrase became his signature, repeated in his books and his preaching for the rest of his life.

The setting matters. He wrote as a pastor in Nassau, in a young nation that had been a British crown colony for more than two centuries. He knew a people whose gifts had been treated as worthless by the order that ruled them, and who had absorbed some of that judgment. At the close of Releasing Your Potential he speaks straight to them in a section headed A Word to the Third World Mind. Its first line, as far as the preview shows, reads, "History reveals the truth that any people who are robbed by oppression" and from there he turns to what such a people must do with what was not taken. Behind everything in these two books is his conviction that a people robbed once must not rob themselves a second time by burying their own gifts.

His argument moves in five steps.

The first step is that potential is revealed by demand. "Potential is determined and revealed by the demands placed on it by its creator," he writes. He goes on to explain that what a product can do is shown by what its maker asks it to do. A person does not find out what Yahweh put in him by introspection. He finds out when Yahweh asks something of him that is bigger than his comfort, and he says yes. Moses did not know he could lead a nation until he was sent. The point cuts against a culture that waits to feel ready. Readiness, in his teaching, is revealed in the obeying.

The second step is faith. "God established faith as the only system through which men and women can touch His power. Potential demands faith, and faith makes demands on potential." Faith, in other words, is not an extra for the especially spiritual; it is how a creature draws on its Creator. And it is not reckless. In one of his most quoted lines he writes, "Faith isn't a jump in the dark. It is a walk in the light." He meant that faith steps out on what Yahweh has already said.

The third step is work. "Potential is what you have. Work is what you do," he writes, and "God gives you work to activate your power." He was impatient with believers who prayed for breakthroughs and would not study, practice, train, or show up on time. In his other books he liked to point out that Yahweh gave Adam work in the garden before anything had gone wrong, so that work is part of the original blessing. The publisher’s description of Releasing Your Potential says the book would deal a death blow to procrastination, and the preview of his final page shows him urging his readers to stop "procrastinating and start planning".

The fourth step is sharing. Potential, he insists, is never for the one who holds it. In Maximizing he argues that we need one another’s gifts to become what we were made to be, and sums it up: "All we have been given is meant to be shared." He thinks in generations, quoting the old saying that "he who plants a tree plans for prosperity": the wise person plants shade he may never sit under.

The fifth step is his famous charge. "Die empty. Die fulfilled by dying unfilled." He paints the cemetery as the wealthiest ground on earth, because buried in it are "dreams and visions that were never fulfilled, books that were never written, paintings that were never painted, songs that were never sung, and ideas that died as ideas." His call to action is plain: "Determine to die empty and leave the earth an inheritance that gives life to others."

It is worth slowing down on why he reached for the graveyard instead of a gentler image. He was speaking to congregations where many people had been taught to expect little, to keep their heads down, and to call that humility. He had watched gifted men cut hair for forty years and never mention the songs they wrote, and gifted women run whole households and church kitchens and never once be asked to lead. He was not attacking ordinary work; he praised it. He was attacking the habit of hiding. The cemetery image was meant to shock his hearers into counting what was still inside them, and into seeing that a hidden gift does not stay safe. It simply goes into the ground. That is also why he tied dying empty to the next generation. A gift poured out becomes an inheritance; a gift held back becomes a loss nobody can even name, because no one ever saw it.

Now weigh it by the Word, letting the Word explain the Word.

On the main line he stands on solid ground. Jesus Himself told the story of the talents. The master entrusted his goods to three servants and went away. Two traded and doubled what they had and heard, "Well done, thou good and faithful servant: thou hast been faithful over a few things, I will make thee ruler over many things" (Matthew 25:21). The third said, "And I was afraid, and went and hid thy talent in the earth" (Matthew 25:25), and the master called him wicked and slothful. Burying a gift is not humility in the Lord’s eyes. It is unfaithfulness. James says faith without works is dead, which is Dr. Munroe’s second and third steps in one sentence. And the Preacher of Ecclesiastes says it with a graveyard of his own: "Whatsoever thy hand findeth to do, do it with thy might; for there is no work, nor device, nor knowledge, nor wisdom, in the grave, whither thou goest." (Ecclesiastes 9:10)

The Word also gives the deepest picture of dying empty, and it is not a motivational speaker. It is the Son. On the night before the cross Jesus prayed, "I have glorified thee on the earth: I have finished the work which thou gavest me to do." (John 17:4) Paul, waiting for his execution in Rome, wrote, "I have fought a good fight, I have finished my course, I have kept the faith:" (2 Timothy 4:7) And of David the Scripture says that after he had served his own generation by the will of God, he fell asleep. Serve, finish, sleep. That is the biblical shape of a life poured out. Peter adds the reason the gifts must move: "As every man hath received the gift, even so minister the same one to another, as good stewards of the manifold grace of God." (1 Peter 4:10) A gift held back from the body is a stewardship left undone.

There is one place where the Word corrects some of his wording, and it should be said plainly and kindly. In the faith chapters of Releasing Your Potential he describes faith as a process of believing, conceiving and speaking until you receive what you desire, and he pictures Yahweh Himself calling light out of the darkness by faith. Scripture frames both differently. Yahweh does not need faith; He is the One faith rests on. "Through faith we understand that the worlds were framed by the word of God" (Hebrews 11:3). And our faith is not a lever for our desires. "And this is the confidence that we have in him, that, if we ask any thing according to his will, he heareth us:" (1 John 5:14) So we keep his call to trust and to work, and we let the Word keep faith pointed where it belongs, at Yahweh’s will and Yahweh’s Word. Dying empty is not getting everything we wanted. It is giving everything He gave.

What does this mean for us now? It means asking, with real seriousness, what is still in the drawer. The song in the notes app. The book outline. The business plan sitting since 2015. The family history only one aunt still remembers. The gift for teaching children that the church has never asked you to use, and that you have never offered. For a people whose gifts were once dismissed or laughed out of the room, it means refusing to finish the robbery ourselves. It means grandparents writing down what they know before it is buried with them, and parents making demands on their children’s gifts instead of protecting them from all difficulty. And it means measuring a life the way the Lord will: not by what we accumulated, but by whether, at the end, we can say with the Son, in our small measure, that we finished the work He gave us to do.

## 9. Dr. Myles Munroe — Purpose and priorities: ask the Manufacturer why you were made

*id: `pv-munroe-purpose` · 1498 words*

**The big idea.** Dr. Myles Munroe taught that purpose is the original reason a thing was made, that only its maker can tell you what it is, and that purpose is the source of priorities. Without purpose a life becomes motion without progress, busy with things that are not evil but are wrong for you. He redefined success as fulfilling the purpose and potential Yahweh gave you, measured by obedience.

Dr. Myles Munroe built a worldwide ministry largely on one word. Purpose ran through nearly everything he wrote, but it was the center of In Pursuit of Purpose, which his publisher described with a sentence that could stand for his whole message: "We must pursue purpose because our fulfillment in life depends upon our becoming what we were born to be and do." That book has no available preview, so this lesson hears his argument from his own later writings, the 2012 compilation Uncover Your Potential and the daily devotional Destiny Image published in 2007, where the same teaching appears in his own words.

The setting is worth remembering. He was writing as a Bahamian pastor and a trainer of leaders across the developing world, to people many of whom had been told for generations that their role was assigned by someone else: by a colonial office, by an employer, by the expectations of a society that did not expect much from them. He was also writing to a modern world that had swung to the opposite error, telling everyone to invent their own meaning. His teaching on purpose answers both. Your purpose is not assigned by those who ruled you, and it is not invented by you. It is given by the One who made you.

His argument moves in five steps.

The first step is a definition. "Purpose is defined as the original reason and intent for the creation of a thing. Therefore, purpose is the source of meaning and significance for all created things." Purpose is prior. A manufacturer does not build a product and then wonder what it might be good for. The intention exists before the first part is made, and the design is shaped to serve it. A thing’s meaning, then, is not something it acquires along the way; it is something it was given at the start.

The second step follows directly, and it is the hinge of the whole teaching. "The purpose of a thing cannot be known by asking anyone other than the designer or the manufacturer." If you find an unfamiliar tool, you do not interrogate the tool, and you do not take a poll of other tools. You ask the one who made it, or you read what he wrote. Applied to a human life, this cuts in two directions at once. It refuses the colonial lie that your purpose is whatever your rulers find useful. And it refuses the modern lie that your purpose is whatever you decide it is. Purpose is received from the Maker.

The third step is that purpose produces priorities. "Purpose is the source of priorities," he writes. Without it, choosing becomes nearly impossible, and life fills up anyway. "The truth is, if you don't know where you are going, any road will take you there." In chapter 7 of Uncover Your Potential he lists what the absence of priority produces: time and energy wasted beyond recovery; busyness with the wrong things; a life spent on the unnecessary; majoring in the minors; and finally a preoccupation with the unimportant that hardens, by default, into a false priority. He adds a distinction that many readers found piercing. The wrong things, he says, may not be bad or evil in themselves. They are simply wrong for you, because they distract you from what you are for.

The fourth step is motion versus progress. His image for a life without purpose is a child’s toy: "Vision is buried in purpose. Without knowledge of purpose, life becomes an endless string of activities with little or no significance. Like a rider on a rocking horse, life without purpose makes much motion but no progress." He pairs it with a second picture: a car is built to be driven, and its "purpose cannot be fulfilled by sitting in the driveway." Purpose demands movement, and movement draws resistance, which he welcomed: "Opposition is proof that you're swimming, not floating." And a vision held without action is no vision at all: "You can have all the vision you want, but until you get a mission, your vision will be no more than a wishful thought."

The fifth step is a new definition of success. "Success can only be defined by fulfilling your potential and purpose, measured by obedience." He explicitly sets aside accumulation, possessions, achievements and other people’s opinions as measures. In his frame, completing the assignment the Maker intended is faithfulness, and faithfulness is success.

His call to the reader, then, is to ask the Manufacturer why you were made, let the answer set your priorities, cut away good things that are wrong for you, get off the rocking horse and out of the driveway, and measure your life by obedience.

Now weigh it by the Word, letting the Word explain the Word.

On his central claim he stands on Scripture’s ground. "There are many devices in a man’s heart; nevertheless the counsel of the LORD, that shall stand." (Proverbs 19:21) Man’s heart is full of devices, plans of his own making; the counsel of Yahweh is what stands. Paul says the same of every believer: "For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works, which God hath before ordained that we should walk in them." (Ephesians 2:10) The good works were ordained before we walked in them; our part is to walk. That is exactly Dr. Munroe’s purpose-before-product. Jeremiah heard it of his own life before birth, that Yahweh knew him and ordained him before he was formed.

The Lord Jesus is the clearest picture of purpose setting priorities. In Capernaum, the whole city gathered at the door to be healed, and the next morning everyone was looking for Him. He had risen a great while before day to pray. When the disciples found Him and told Him that all men sought Him, He answered, "And he said unto them, Let us go into the next towns, that I may preach there also: for therefore came I forth." (Mark 1:38) Good work was waiting in Capernaum. It was not the work He was sent to do that morning, and He knew the difference because He had been with the Father. And in Bethany, when Martha was careful and troubled about many things, all of them good, Jesus told her, "But one thing is needful: and Mary hath chosen that good part" (Luke 10:42). That is Dr. Munroe’s good-but-wrong-for-you in the Lord’s own words.

Two additions from the Word keep the teaching on its foundation. First, many teachers on vision, Dr. Munroe among them in other books, lean on Proverbs 29:18, where there is no vision the people perish. Read whole, the verse joins that vision to keeping Yahweh’s law, and the vision of the prophets was what Yahweh revealed, not what a person dreamed up. That actually fits Dr. Munroe’s own logic better than the popular reading does: the Manufacturer gives the vision, not the product. Second, Scripture names a first purpose that comes before any career, ministry or assignment. Jesus prayed, "And this is life eternal, that they might know thee the only true God, and Jesus Christ, whom thou hast sent." (John 17:3) Every particular purpose grows out of that one. A person who knows exactly what job Yahweh gave him and does not know Yahweh has the order backward.

What does this mean for us now? It means sitting down, perhaps for the first time in years, and asking the Maker directly what He made us for in this season, and then reading His Word with that question open. It means taking an honest inventory of what fills our weeks and being willing to name some good things as wrong for us. For churches, it means asking whether our calendars serve our purpose or have become a rocking horse of their own: programs, events and committees with much motion and little progress. For families whose ancestors had their purposes assigned to them by force, it means teaching our children that their purpose was never anyone’s to assign but Yahweh’s, and that He did not make them to be anyone’s tools. It also means learning to tell purpose from pressure. Many of us carry assignments that were handed to us by need, by family expectation, or by a church that had a hole to fill. Some of those, on reflection and prayer, turn out to be exactly what Yahweh intended; His purposes often come through ordinary needs. Others turn out to be rocking horses we climbed onto because nobody else would. The only way to know the difference is the way Jesus knew it in Capernaum: time alone with the Father, early, before the crowd arrives. Dr. Munroe’s teaching gives us the question; the Lord’s own practice gives us the method. ## 10. Dr. Myles Munroe — The Burden of Freedom: delivered is not yet free

*id: `pv-munroe-burden-of-freedom` · 1683 words*

**The big idea.** In The Burden of Freedom Dr. Myles Munroe spoke to post-colonial peoples, his own Bahamas first, and argued that being released from an oppressor is not the same as being free. Like Israel out of Egypt, a people can be delivered in body and still carry the conditioning of bondage in mind: fear, distrust, a hatred of work, an inability to manage. True freedom, he said, is the liberty to exercise dominion through your gifts in fulfillment of Yahweh’s purpose, and it is a burden only the mature can bear, because it demands responsibility and the renewing of the mind.

Dr. Myles Munroe published The Burden of Freedom in 2000 through Creation House, and it is the most direct thing he ever wrote to his own people. The Bahamas had been a British crown colony from 1718. Slavery there ended with abolition across the British Empire on 1 August 1834. Internal self-government came in 1964, the first Black premier in 1967, and independence on 10 July 1973. Dr. Munroe was nineteen that summer. He describes it in chapter 5: "My own country has enjoyed emancipation from the British crown since 1973." And then, with the honesty that runs through the whole book: "At first we had parades and danced, just like Miriam and the Israelites did on the other side of the Red Sea. But when the music died down, we saw how much we had to learn before actually becoming free."

He wrote for the Caribbean, Africa and South America by name, and his principles reach every people who have come out of slavery, colonization or segregation, including Black communities in the United States. His publisher summed up his burden: the book confronts how "too many people use past oppression to remain mired in hatred and irresponsibility today," and it names telltale effects such as "a hatred for work, laziness, fear, low self-esteem, selfishness, lack of creativity, low initiative, and distrust of those in authority." That is hard language, and he meant it to be. But he was not standing outside the wound pointing at it. He was a son of that history, writing as a pastor who wanted his people whole.

His argument moves in six steps.

The first step is the thesis on the first page: "Freedom is a burden that only the mature can bear." Freedom is not merely the removal of chains. It is the arrival of responsibility. Under a master, others decided where you worked, what you earned and how you lived. Freedom hands those decisions back, and with them the consequences.

The second step is that conditioning outlasts captivity. He tells of a report he says he read in a science journal: a dog was leashed to a stake with its food just out of reach, and each lunge brought pain. After weeks it stopped trying. When the leash was removed, the dog stayed by the stake and nearly starved, until it was carried to the food. He gives no citation, so we hold it as his illustration rather than established science. His conclusion is the point: "when the mind of an animal is conditioned, it will live within the limitations imposed upon it even after it is set free."

The third step is Israel. He reads the Exodus as the Bible’s case study. "They had been delivered from Egypt, but they weren't yet free. So God had to deal with their minds." From this he draws a principle he says applies to individuals, communities and nations: "Conditions determine conduct until interrupted by an external force." Long conditions produce settled conduct, and something from outside, ultimately Yahweh and His Word, has to break in.

The fourth step defines freedom from creation. Reading Genesis 1:26, where Yahweh gives humanity dominion over the fish, the fowl, the cattle and all the earth, he observes, "Man is conspicuously missing from the list." Therefore "any domination of another human spirit is violation of God's natural law." And positively: "True freedom is therefore 'the liberty to dominate the earth through one's unique gifts and talents in fulfillment of God's purpose for one.'" Freedom is not the right to do nothing. It is the restored ability to steward the earth with the gifts Yahweh gave.

The fifth step is his own household. Eleven children and two parents lived in "a little four-room house." Each morning water was pumped by hand into a bucket and poured into a tin tub, and each child stood in it to be washed. When the family built a new house on the east side of Nassau with running water, he writes, "we still had our old wilderness 'tin tub' mentality. We had a brand-new porcelain tub, but we didn't know how to bathe in it!" They kept standing up in the new tub for years. His principle: "Position does not guarantee disposition." A new address, a new title, a new flag, does not by itself produce a new mind.

The sixth step names the marks oppression leaves and the cure. In his chapter 5 summary he lists them bluntly: "Oppression actually conditions people to be unproductive, and laziness becomes a lifestyle." "Oppressed people are afraid of everything, even their own people." "The spirit of oppression and slavery also produces jealousy, distrust, suspicion and hate." In a later chapter he adds that it "destroys the sense of heritage, history, belonging and significance" in families, and says that "In the Caribbean, Africa and South America, this is the source of many social problems today." His cure is responsibility and management. He rebukes a church that can shout but cannot steward: "We can speak in tongues, but we can't speak to the banker." He grounds stewardship in Yahweh’s ownership: "God gave you rulership, not ownership, so He can always take back what you mismanage." And he says, "It is human rights, not civil rights, that set people free," meaning that dignity and the call to dominion are given by Yahweh at creation, not granted by any government.

His call to the reader: accept the burden; stop blaming the past for present irresponsibility; let Yahweh recondition the mind; learn to manage; and walk out of the wilderness.

Now weigh it by the Word, letting the Word explain the Word.

His reading of the Exodus is the Bible’s own. Yahweh saw the affliction of His people and heard their cry by reason of their taskmasters; the bondage was real and Yahweh named it as wrong. Then, having delivered them, He dealt with their minds, exactly as Dr. Munroe says: "And it came to pass, when Pharaoh had let the people go, that God led them not through the way of the land of the Philistines, although that was near; for God said, Lest peradventure the people repent when they see war, and they return to Egypt:" (Exodus 13:17) Within weeks, hungry in the desert, they said, "Would to God we had died by the hand of the LORD in the land of Egypt, when we sat by the flesh pots, and when we did eat bread to the full" (Exodus 16:3). Yahweh led them forty years to humble them and to prove what was in their hearts. Egypt came out of them slowly.

Paul gives the New Testament form of the same truth. "Stand fast therefore in the liberty wherewith Christ hath made us free, and be not entangled again with the yoke of bondage." (Galatians 5:1) And he joins freedom to responsibility just as Dr. Munroe does: "For, brethren, ye have been called unto liberty; only use not liberty for an occasion to the flesh, but by love serve one another." (Galatians 5:13) The renewing he calls for is Paul’s own command: "And be not conformed to this world: but be ye transformed by the renewing of your mind, that ye may prove what is that good, and acceptable, and perfect, will of God." (Romans 12:2) His reading of Genesis 1:26 also stands: dominion was given over creatures, never over people, and Paul told believers, ye are bought with a price; be not ye the servants of men.

Two of his lines need the whole counsel of the Word around them, and the Word corrects them where they reach too far. He writes that "Poverty is ultimately a product of mismanagement." Scripture does say that sloth and poor stewardship bring want; Proverbs walks past the field of the slothful and sees the thorns. But the same Word says, "Much food is in the tillage of the poor: but there is that is destroyed for want of judgment." (Proverbs 13:23) and James cries against the hire of the laborers kept back by fraud. Poverty has more than one root. The Word refuses to let a people blame only the oppressor, and it equally refuses to let anyone pretend the oppression did not happen or did no lasting harm. Both are true, plainly. Likewise, his line that "It is human rights, not civil rights, that set people free" is right about the source of dignity: no government gives it and no government can take it. It must not be heard as dismissing the struggle for just laws. Yahweh commands, "seek judgment, relieve the oppressed" (Isaiah 1:17), and the civil rights movement was, for many of our fathers and mothers, obedience to that command.

What does this mean for us now? It means telling the truth about our history without letting it write our future. It means looking honestly at the tin-tub habits our families still carry: distrust of each other, fear of institutions, scarcity thinking in seasons of provision, the sense that work is something done to us rather than dominion given to us. It means churches teaching management, money, family and enterprise alongside worship, so that we can speak to the banker as well as in tongues. It means telling hard history without bitterness and without contempt for any people, the Egyptians of the Exodus included; Yahweh judged Pharaoh’s cruelty, and He also welcomed the stranger from Egypt among His people, and Dr. Munroe himself aimed his rebuke at the minds of the freed, not at hatred of the former rulers. And it means remembering how much grace is in the Exodus story itself. Israel complained, and Yahweh sent manna. They doubted, and He led them by cloud and fire. He did not wait for them to have perfect minds before He fed them. He renews us the same way, patiently, a day’s bread at a time. The deliverance was Yahweh’s, and the freedom is His too. He brought us out. He is willing to finish the walk with us, renewing our minds until we live as free as He made us.

## 11. Dr. Myles Munroe — The Purpose and Power of Authority, and a Word to the Third World

*id: `pv-munroe-authority` · 1474 words*

**The big idea.** In The Purpose and Power of Authority Dr. Myles Munroe argued that authority is not, at root, control over others but the gifts Yahweh places in a person to fulfill the purpose He made them for, released and developed under the Author who gave them. In the book’s epilogue, A Word to the Third World, he told post-colonial peoples that abuse had taught them to fear or reject all authority, and that this leads to anarchy and new oppression; freedom comes through genuine authority, beginning with Yahweh’s.

Dr. Myles Munroe wrote The Purpose and Power of Authority late in his ministry; its own references to Susan Boyle in 2009 and Pope Benedict XVI’s September 2010 visit to Britain place it around 2010-2011, published by Whitaker House. By then he had spent decades training leaders across the developing world and served as head of an association of Third World leaders. He opens by describing a global mood. In the West, he says, people distrust authority after centuries of revolutions, scandals and unpopular wars. In formerly colonized nations, the distrust is deeper, because, as he puts it, authority came to mean not just restriction but outright domination. One part of the world fears authority, he writes, and another hates it, and few people trust it. His book tries to recover the word from its abuse, and its last pages speak directly to the nations that suffered that abuse most.

His argument moves in five steps.

The first step is a story. In the spring of 2009 a plain, middle-aged, unemployed woman from Scotland named Susan Boyle walked onto the stage of Britain’s Got Talent. The judges were openly skeptical and the audience rolled their eyes. Then she sang, and within a few lines the crowd was on its feet. Dr. Munroe asks what a television contestant has to do with authority, and answers that her story shows the heart of the book: "true authority is personal, and true authority comes from within."

The second step is a redefinition. "Authority does not mean having power or control over others," he writes. It does not come automatically with a title like manager or president. "Personal authority is inherent within every human being," whether the person is in charge or taking orders, and "it cannot be given to someone — only released and developed." Then the definition the whole book rests on: "Personal authority can be defined as the intrinsic gifts a person or thing possesses in order to fulfill the purpose for which that person or thing was placed on this earth." Authority, in his sense, is the God-given capacity and right to function in your domain, the area of your gifting.

The third step is four principles. The Principle of the Author ties the release of your authority to the origin of your gifts: discover the source, and your domain opens. The Principle of Authorization says you have not only the capacity but the permission to carry out your purpose. The Principle of Authenticity: "No person is truly authentic until he is manifesting his inherent authority." You are most real when you are doing what you were made to do. And The Principle of Authority has two sides: everyone is designed to fulfill a purpose, and everything depends on and must yield to something else to function. He adds, against selfish readings, that personal authority is not exclusive. It is designed to work with others and for their benefit; we need each other’s authority to fulfill our own.

The fourth step, in the epilogue, is his diagnosis of the developing world. Colonial powers, he writes, annihilated cultures, raided the resources of their colonies, and traded in human labor and life, reducing human beings to commodities. That abuse produced two attitudes: "(1) they promoted a defeatist, surrendered outlook, and (2) they ignited a fierce resolve to throw off any form of perceived authoritative control." Both, he warns, have often rejected legitimate authority along with false. Restating two principles from the book, he writes that "ignorance of true authority is the father of anarchy" and "without true authority, purpose has no protection." He sees the fruit in some newly independent nations, where native leaders who had lived long in scarcity abuse their own people to grab what they can. His answer is short: "we can never lift ourselves up by tearing others down." We are called to protect not only our own purposes but each other’s.

The fifth step is his resolution: "freedom comes through authority." Freedom and authority, he says, are not enemies. When Yahweh brought a nation of slaves out of Egypt, the first thing He gave them was not permission to do whatever they wanted; He gave them His law through Moses, and His presence to guide them. Recognizing Yahweh’s absolute authority, with the principles of His Word, is essential to true freedom in developing nations, and he quotes Jesus’ words about the truth that sets free. He closes: "Genuine authority is the only path to establishing real freedom."

His call to the reader: discover your gifts and your domain; function in them as a servant of others; and, as nations and churches, build on genuine authority under Yahweh rather than either surrendering to abuse or rejecting all order.

Now weigh it by the Word, letting the Word explain the Word.

His epilogue is on solid ground. The Ten Commandments begin, "I am the LORD thy God, which have brought thee out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage." (Exodus 20:2) Deliverance first, then law. A freed people received order as a gift. The book of Judges shows the alternative he warns of, ending with these words: "In those days there was no king in Israel: every man did that which was right in his own eyes." (Judges 21:25) That is anarchy, and Israel tore itself apart under it. Jesus confronted domination directly: "But Jesus called them unto him, and said, Ye know that the princes of the Gentiles exercise dominion over them, and they that are great exercise authority upon them." (Matthew 20:25) and then, "But it shall not be so among you: but whosoever will be great among you, let him be your minister;" (Matthew 20:26) The colonial order had authority backward; the Kingdom sets it right side up. And the truth Dr. Munroe quoted stands: "And ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free." (John 8:32)

His teaching on gifts also has the Word behind it. A proverb says a man’s gift makes room for him and brings him before great men, as Susan Boyle’s did. Peter tells every believer to minister the gift he has received to others, as a good steward of Yahweh’s grace. The centurion who came to Jesus understood authority better than most religious leaders of his day: "For I am a man under authority, having soldiers under me" (Matthew 8:9). He had authority because he was under it.

That is where the Word refines one of his phrases. Taken alone, true authority comes from within could sound as though authority originates in us. Scripture says it never does. "Thou couldest have no power at all against me, except it were given thee from above" (John 19:11), Jesus told Pilate. And after the resurrection: "And Jesus came and spake unto them, saying, All power is given unto me in heaven and in earth." (Matthew 28:18) Every gift is within us because it was placed there from above, and every right to use it is delegated by the King. Dr. Munroe’s own Principle of the Author points this way, and his epilogue ends by submitting all things to Absolute Authority; we simply say it plainly. The Word also keeps every human ruler accountable. Paul calls rulers ministers of God to us for good, and when men command what Yahweh forbids, the apostles answered that we ought to obey God rather than men. That single balance protects against both of the reactions he diagnosed: the defeated surrender that obeys anyone, and the rebellion that obeys no one.

What does this mean for us now? For individuals, it means finding the domain of our gifting and serving there faithfully, whether or not anyone gives us a title, as many mothers of the church have always done. For churches, it means leaders who lead as servants and members who honor legitimate leadership rather than tearing it down. For our communities and for formerly colonized nations, it means refusing both the old surrender and the reflexive rejection of all order, and building institutions under Yahweh’s Word that protect the purposes of everyone. And for all of us, it means remembering that the freest people on earth are those who live under the authority of the King who made them, because His authority is the only one that never abuses what it holds. And it means telling the hard history of colonial abuse honestly, without softening it and without contempt for any people, because the King we serve used His authority on the cross to pray for those who wronged Him. Grace does not erase the record; it breaks the cycle. A people who have known abusive rule, and then learn to lead as servants, become living evidence that Jesus’ way of authority is not a dream but a better kingdom already at work.

## 12. Dr. Myles Munroe — Overcoming Crisis: the management mandate

*id: `pv-munroe-overcoming-crisis` · 1511 words*

**The big idea.** In Overcoming Crisis, published in 2009 under a first chapter titled Global Crisis, Personal Crisis, Dr. Myles Munroe argued that a crisis is an event you could not control or prepare for, but your response to it can be managed, and that management was humanity’s first assignment: Yahweh did not send rain on the earth until there was a man to work the ground. With Joseph as his model, he taught that the secret to thriving, not just surviving, in a crisis is effective management, and that effective management implies trust in Yahweh.

Dr. Myles Munroe published Overcoming Crisis in 2009 through Destiny Image, and its first chapter, Global Crisis, Personal Crisis, tells us the season it was written for. Across the world people were losing jobs, savings and certainty at once, and Dr. Munroe, a pastor in The Bahamas who had trained leaders across the developing world for decades, wrote a book for people in trouble. He wrote as an islander. In the Bahamas, he reminds his readers, a hurricane forecast is not a figure of speech, and everyone knows that when the warning comes, priorities change in an hour. His chapter titles read like a field manual: The Management Mandate, Seven Ways to Manage a Crisis, Overcoming Seasons of Crisis, The Seed Principle, The Secret to Thriving in a Time of Crisis, Kingdom Deployment, Ten Ways to Rise Above Crisis.

His argument moves in five steps.

The first step is a definition that is honest about our limits. "A crisis is an event over which one has no control. Crises are experiences that you did not anticipate or prepare for," he writes. They take you by surprise and fill you with anxiety. He does not pretend believers are exempt. What he insists on is the next sentence of his teaching: you cannot control the crisis that has come upon you, but a crisis forces the issue, and you can begin to control your response to it. The man told by his doctor that he has weeks to live if he does not change his habits suddenly finds the strength to change. Crisis clarifies.

The second step goes back to the beginning, to Genesis 2. Before Yahweh formed the man, Scripture says, no plant of the field had yet sprung up, because Yahweh had not caused it to rain upon the earth, and there was not a man to till the ground. Dr. Munroe draws the lesson plainly: "God did not allow anything to grow on the earth at first because there was nobody to work the ground." He adds a word study of his own, glossing the Hebrew word for till, abad, as "both 'to serve' and 'to cause someone or something else to serve,' in other words, 'to manage.'" That gloss is his, and it was not checked against a lexicon in this study; but his point does not depend on it, because the verse itself says growth waited for a man to work the ground. Then Yahweh formed Adam and placed him in Eden to dress it and keep it. Dr. Munroe calls this the management mandate. Before there was sin, before there was a curse, there was stewardship. We were made managers.

The third step is his thesis for every crisis: "The secret to thriving (not just surviving) in a time of crisis is effective management." He repeats it on purpose. Management, he admits, sounds dull, and when it works well no one notices it; but when a crisis comes, it is exposed, and people can see whether you are stuck or moving. Then he adds the line that keeps his teaching from collapsing into technique: "The secret is management, and effective management implies trust." A manager does not own what he manages. He works faithfully and trusts the Owner.

The fourth step is Joseph, his model manager. Sold by his own brothers, carried into slavery in Egypt, falsely accused by his master’s wife, forgotten in prison, Joseph had every reason to give up. Instead, Dr. Munroe notes, he managed well in every place he was put, and he was promoted from each one. When Pharaoh dreamed of seven fat years and seven lean years, Joseph did not stop at interpretation. He laid out a plan: find a discreet and wise man, appoint officers, take up a fifth of the harvest in the plenteous years, and store it against the famine. Pharaoh promoted him on the spot. Dr. Munroe’s observation is that Joseph rose because of the crisis, or more precisely because he was ready to manage it, and that Yahweh had been preparing him through every earlier crisis.

The fifth step is the seed principle, with his own testimony. "I'll never forget what Oral Roberts, my father in the faith, said to us back in 1976 in chapel at Oral Roberts University. He said, 'When you can't meet your need, turn it into a seed.'" Unable to pay his tuition, the young Bahamian student took what money he had left to the front of the chapel as an offering, and he testifies that provision came soon after. He goes on to teach that Yahweh often answers prayer with seed rather than finished bread, so that a person who prayed for money may be given a job, and the work becomes the cultivation.

His call to the reader: accept that you cannot control the crisis; take charge of what you can manage; plan like Joseph; trust the Owner; sow faithfully; and expect Yahweh to use the crisis to deploy you.

Now weigh it by the Word, letting the Word explain the Word.

The management mandate is plain Scripture. "for the LORD God had not caused it to rain upon the earth, and there was not a man to till the ground" (Genesis 2:5). And then: "And the LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to dress it and to keep it." (Genesis 2:15) Paul states the principle for every believer: "Moreover it is required in stewards, that a man be found faithful." (1 Corinthians 4:2) Jesus said, "He that is faithful in that which is least is faithful also in much" (Luke 16:10). The wisdom literature commends the ant that gathers in harvest for the season to come, which is Joseph’s plan in miniature.

Joseph’s story confirms the rest, and it adds the note that keeps management humble. When Pharaoh asked him to interpret the dream, Joseph answered, "It is not in me: God shall give Pharaoh an answer of peace" (Genesis 41:16). And Pharaoh’s verdict was not that Joseph was a clever administrator but, "Can we find such a one as this is, a man in whom the Spirit of God is?" (Genesis 41:38) The secret of Joseph’s management was the presence of Yahweh. The Scripture says it again and again in Genesis 39: the LORD was with Joseph, and that which he did, the LORD made it to prosper. Dr. Munroe’s line that effective management implies trust is exactly right; the Word only makes the object of that trust explicit.

On the seed principle, the Word gives the frame his testimony needs. Paul does teach that he who sows bountifully reaps bountifully, and he praises Yahweh, "he that ministereth seed to the sower both minister bread for your food" (2 Corinthians 9:10). Isaiah uses the same picture of seed for the sower and bread for the eater. But Paul also says, "Every man according as he purposeth in his heart, so let him give; not grudgingly, or of necessity: for God loveth a cheerful giver." (2 Corinthians 9:7) Giving is worship and trust offered from the heart, not a transaction that obligates Yahweh to a return on our schedule. Jesus honored the widow who gave her last two mites, and the Word does not say what she received afterward. Dr. Munroe’s story is a true testimony of Yahweh’s faithfulness to him; it is not a formula, and we should not preach it as one, especially to people in desperate need.

There is one more thing the Word puts at the center of Joseph’s crisis, and it is grace. The story is hard history inside the Bible itself: a young man sold into slavery by his own brothers. Scripture does not soften it. Joseph later says his brothers meant it for evil. But when they stood before him, terrified, he wept and forgave them, and said, "But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive." (Genesis 50:20) He then provided for them and their children. The greatest act of management in the story was not the storehouses. It was Joseph refusing to let bitterness manage him.

What does this mean for us now? For families in a layoff, a diagnosis, a storm or a death, it means writing down what we cannot control and handing it to Yahweh, and writing down what we can manage and doing it faithfully, starting today. For churches, it means teaching budgets, savings and planning as part of discipleship, so that our people can be Josephs in their workplaces and neighborhoods when crisis comes. For communities that have known hard history, it means telling it honestly, as Scripture tells Joseph’s, and also refusing to let it rule us, trusting the Yahweh who turns what others meant for evil toward saving many people alive. Crisis is not the end of the story. In Yahweh’s hands, it is often where the manager He has been training is finally deployed.

## 13. Dr. Myles Munroe — The Purpose and Power of Praise and Worship: bragging about Yahweh

*id: `pv-munroe-praise-and-worship` · 1469 words*

**The big idea.** In The Purpose and Power of Praise and Worship Dr. Myles Munroe taught that praise is celebrating Yahweh as our heart’s true home: commending Him, which means entrusting ourselves to His care and recommending Him to others, approving and glorifying Him. He argued that praise is a conscious choice of the will, a willing sacrifice, and an expression of faith, not a warm-up before the sermon, and that praise that flows from knowing Him is bragging about Yahweh instead of ourselves.

Dr. Myles Munroe published The Purpose and Power of Praise and Worship in 2000 through Destiny Image. He wrote it as the founding pastor of Bahamas Faith Ministries International in Nassau, and as a teacher who spoke in churches of many traditions. What troubled him was not one style of worship or another. It was a posture he saw almost everywhere: praise and worship treated as preliminaries. People, he wrote, were so self-focused that they treated expressions of admiration toward Yahweh as something to get through on the way to the teaching and personal ministry that made them feel good. He said plainly that there is no reason to have a meeting at all if praise and worship are not its central focus. The book builds from Yahweh’s original plan for mankind and man’s greatest need, through creating a dwelling place for Him, to the blessing of Judah, and then, in chapter 5, stops to ask the question beneath everything else: What Is Praise?

His argument in that chapter moves in five steps.

The first step is a definition of the heart. The chapter opens with one sentence set apart: "Praise is celebrating God as our heart's true home." Then he lines up the commands of Scripture: David calling the whole assembly to praise; the Psalms calling the servants of Yahweh, all nations and all peoples, and finally everything that has breath, to praise Him. If we are to obey these commands, he says, we must first learn what praise is.

The second step is a definition of the word. Taking it from Webster’s, he writes, "Praise includes commending; expressing approval or a favorable judgment of; and glorifying, especially by crediting with perfections." He spends the most time on commending, which means both to entrust for safekeeping and to recommend as worthy. So: "Praising God by commendation thus means that we entrust ourselves to His care and recommend that others do the same." He shows this in David’s psalms, where trust and praise are almost the same act, and he quotes Psalm 7:1 and Psalm 9:10. He then applies it: a father who has lost his job, heavy and overwhelmed, praises Yahweh by commendation when he entrusts his family to Yahweh’s care and speaks Yahweh’s promises over his circumstances.

The third step names what praise is not. We are, he says, naturally self-centered, always asking how something affects us. Praise turns our eyes from ourselves to Yahweh. His summary is the line people remember: "In essence, praise is bragging about God instead of us." And he adds a check against hollow praise: you cannot brag truthfully about someone you do not know. Praise may begin with what you have heard about Yahweh, but it must grow into what you have experienced of Him, until it flows naturally from friendship.

The fourth step is three marks of true praise, each a section title in the chapter. Praise Is a Conscious Choice: it is an act of the will, not of the emotions; you do not have to feel good to praise Yahweh. He reads Psalm 42, where the psalmist, in tears and feeling forgotten, lectures his own soul to hope in Yahweh and resolves to praise Him still. Praise Is a Willing Sacrifice: the Psalms are full of I will, praise chosen and offered on purpose, sometimes at a cost. Praise Is an Expression of Faith: as faith without works is dead, he says, praise that stays in the heart and is never expressed is dead. His example is Abraham binding Isaac on Mount Moriah, probably not singing, likely heavy-hearted, yet offering the highest praise by trusting Yahweh to keep His covenant.

The fifth step is his call to the reader: stop treating praise as a preliminary; make it a daily, chosen, faith-filled commendation of Yahweh; and let it grow out of a real and deepening knowledge of Him, especially when life is hardest. Here he writes that Yahweh is waiting to act on our behalf but needs us to provide a dwelling place for Him, and that praise is that altar.

Now weigh it by the Word, letting the Word explain the Word.

His central teaching is the Word’s own. "By him therefore let us offer the sacrifice of praise to God continually, that is, the fruit of our lips giving thanks to his name." (Hebrews 13:15) Praise as a willing sacrifice is not his invention; it is apostolic doctrine. David made it his rule: "I will bless the LORD at all times: his praise shall continually be in my mouth." (Psalms 34:1) At all times includes the times we do not feel it. Psalm 42, which he reads so well, says it twice: "Why art thou cast down, O my soul? and why art thou disquieted in me? hope thou in God: for I shall yet praise him for the help of his countenance." (Psalms 42:5) The choice comes before the feeling returns. The prophet Habakkuk said that though the fig tree did not blossom and the fields yielded no meat, yet he would rejoice in Yahweh. And Paul and Silas, beaten and fastened in stocks, prayed and sang praises at midnight, and the prisoners heard them.

His reading of Abraham is confirmed by Abraham’s own words. Going up the mountain with Isaac, he told his servants, "I and the lad will go yonder and worship, and come again to you" (Genesis 22:5). Abraham called it worship. Paul says he "staggered not at the promise of God through unbelief; but was strong in faith, giving glory to God" (Romans 4:20). Faith and praise are joined, just as Dr. Munroe says. His teaching on commending is David’s own: "And they that know thy name will put their trust in thee: for thou, LORD, hast not forsaken them that seek thee." (Psalms 9:10) And his warning against hollow praise echoes Jesus quoting Isaiah about a people who honor Yahweh with their lips while their heart is far from Him. Jesus said the Father seeks worshipers who worship in spirit and in truth.

There is one phrase where the Word corrects his wording, kindly and plainly. Yahweh does not need us to provide Him a dwelling place. Paul told the Athenians that the Maker of heaven and earth dwells not in temples made with hands, "Neither is worshipped with men’s hands, as though he needed any thing, seeing he giveth to all life, and breath, and all things" (Acts 17:25). And yet the Word also says, "But thou art holy, O thou that inhabitest the praises of Israel." (Psalms 22:3) The truth is more wonderful than need. The King who needs nothing chooses to be enthroned among a praising people. Our praise does not supply Him; it welcomes Him. It also guards us from a quiet error that can creep into any church: thinking that the right song, volume or atmosphere will make Yahweh show up. He is not summoned. He is honored. When we gather to brag about Him, we are not opening a door He cannot open Himself; we are joining a celebration that heaven never stopped. That correction protects praise from becoming a technique to make Yahweh act, and returns it to what Dr. Munroe himself said it is: celebration of the One who is our home.

What does this mean for us now? For churches, it means examining whether our praise has become a warm-up, a performance, or a habit, and returning it to the center as an offering to Yahweh, not entertainment for us. For families, it means brag sessions at the dinner table, where children hear their parents and grandparents tell what Yahweh has done, so that their praise will one day grow from knowledge into experience. For those in grief, illness or loss, it means that praise is still available, not as a feeling we must manufacture, but as a choice to entrust ourselves to His care and to say aloud that He is good. Many of us learned that from grandmothers who sang through seasons we will never fully know. For leaders, it means teaching what praise is, the way he did, rather than only telling people when to stand and clap; a congregation that understands commending, choosing and sacrificing will praise differently on the hard Sundays. And it means guarding against the subtle self-centeredness he warned about, where even our worship becomes about our feelings, our preferences, or our platform. Praise is bragging about Yahweh instead of us, and that includes our music, our choirs and our services. And for every believer, it means remembering that praise is never wasted. The One who needs nothing has promised to dwell among those who praise Him, and those who know His name will trust Him, because He has never forsaken those who seek Him.
