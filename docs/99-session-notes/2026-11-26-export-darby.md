## 1. Pastor Stephen Darby — identity, the Church’s account, and the unseen realm

*id: `pv-darby` · 1815 words*

**The big idea.** Stephen Darby pastored Destined Ministries in Louisville, Kentucky, from 2003 until his death in December 2017. By his own testimony he had been a street drug dealer who heard the Lord speak to him in a nightclub and walked out to receive Him in the snow. In the first months of 2016 an internet program, Next Chapter Radio, aired fourteen of his recorded messages week after week, and heard together they press one steady message: your real fight is spiritual, not with the person in front of you; the wounds you carry will run your life until you bring them to the cross; love is stronger than knowledge; and every voice, from the pulpit to the screen to the stranger online, must be known and weighed by the Word.

There is a fitting providence in the name. In the book of Acts, Stephen stood before the council of his own nation, retold their history, and named their sin to their faces: "ye do always resist the Holy Ghost: as your fathers did, so do ye" (Acts 7:51). They stoned him for it, and he died praying for them. Jesus had already said "A prophet is not without honour, save in his own country, and in his own house" (Matthew 13:57), and centuries before, a priest of Bethel told the prophet Amos, "O thou seer, go, flee thee away into the land of Judah" (Amos 7:12). The one who names the sin in his own house is resisted at home. Pastor Stephen Darby carried that name, and by his own account he carried that kind of call. This study keeps his record, lets him speak in his own words, and weighs every word by the Word. Start with the man. He pastored Destined Ministries, which met at 910 East Muhammad Ali Boulevard in Louisville, Kentucky, with Bible study on Wednesday nights and worship on Sunday mornings; Stephen Darby Ministries dates its founding to 2003. His ministry’s own channel marks his birthday as 12 November 1972, and he died in December 2017, at forty-five. The church and the teaching archive went on after him. The author Jamal Byrd wrote in 2022 that he was one of the most powerful preachers he had ever heard, and added, "His sermons are still my favorite." He did not come to the pulpit from the choir loft. In a sit-down teaching aired as The Effeminization of Male Leadership (Next Chapter Radio, 14 February 2016), he tells how it happened: he was a drug dealer, he was drinking in a club when the Lord spoke to him, and he walked out and received Him in the snow. For a month he simply read his Bible and sat in his father’s Bible study before he ever joined a church. At 43:31 he sums it up, "I got radically saved." And at 43:42, "That’s why I’m so radical now." In Nurtured to Death (12 March 2016, at 50:11) he says, "My first drink, real drink, was from my mother’s boyfriend." The same man taught him to gamble. He told it not to shame anyone; he says in the same breath that he loves his mother and that she is saved and delivered. He told it so parents would see what walks into a house with the people they let in. In Beware of the Pied Pipers he admits the hardest thing to give up after he was saved was not a drug but his music, stacks of CDs he could not bring himself to throw away for weeks. He preached as a husband who did ministry beside his wife and as a father raising two sons, nineteen and sixteen in January 2016. He ministered abroad as well; he speaks of time at a large deliverance church in Nigeria and of being in Uganda with his wife. How did we come to hear so much of him in his own voice? In the first months of 2016 an internet program, Next Chapter Radio, gave its weekly hour to his recorded messages. The host told listeners she meant to feature him all year, that he was no mega-church preacher, and that his church was small but heard more outside Louisville than inside it, the old pattern of the prophet without honor at home. Fourteen of those broadcasts survive on the Internet Archive, from Transference on 3 January to Willie Lynch Syndrome on 7 April. Between them come The Debating Spirit of Strife, Pornography, a message on the media, Emotional Witchcraft, The Seed of Hate, The Effeminization of Male Leadership, Beware of the Pied Pipers, a message on the Grammys and their idols, Queen of the Coast, Nurtured to Death, New World Order in America and The New Christian Feminist. Most are sermons preached to his own congregation; you can hear the room answer him. The radio dates are airing dates, so some of the sermons were preached earlier. These were never his whole work. In The New Christian Feminist (31 March 2016, at 6:53) he says of his catalog, "I’m talking about hundreds of messages and most of them are different." How did he preach? In plain street speech, with the Bible open, stopping every few minutes to ask the room, are y’all there? He reads the text, explains it in the language of the block, and then presses it into the kitchen, the bedroom, the job and the pew. He told hearers not to swallow him whole. In The Debating Spirit of Strife (10 January 2016, at 41:01) he says of his own videos, "Watch them at your own risk," and at 41:03 he gives the reason, "Because you don’t know me." He set the written Word above every spoken word, his own included; after telling how false prophecies spoken over him as a young believer left him in confusion, he says at 39:11, "This word is better than my word." His ministry’s statement of faith stands where he stood: "We believe that all Scripture centers around the Lord Jesus Christ in His person, work, and in His first and second coming." It confesses the one true and living God "revealed to us as the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit," and it says, "We believe that salvation is the gift of God’s grace." It ends its gospel article plainly: "We believe that Jesus is the Way, The Truth, and the Life—the one and only way to eternal life." Heard together, the fourteen broadcasts press a few threads again and again. The first is that the real fight is spiritual. His first broadcast opens on "For we wrestle not against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers, against the rulers of the darkness of this world, against spiritual wickedness in high places." (Ephesians 6:12), and he argues that the moment a believer sees only the person in front of him, he has already lost. The second is the wounded heart. In Transference, The Seed of Hate, Emotional Witchcraft and Nurtured to Death he traces how an old wound, never brought to the cross, gets taken out on the next husband, the next wife, the next pastor, the next child. The third is love over knowledge: he warned a generation that read everything online and loved less and less that knowledge puffs up and love is the stronger power. The fourth is to know the voices. In Strife he applies Paul’s word to know those who labor among you to strangers on the internet; in Beware of the Pied Pipers, the Grammys broadcast, the media broadcast and Pornography he applies it to music, screens and celebrities, insisting that nothing we take in is neutral. The fifth is order in the house: in The Effeminization of Male Leadership, Nurtured to Death and The New Christian Feminist he calls men to lead, protect and stay, and calls the church to recover the order of the home. The sixth is the long wound of a people. He names slavery and Jim Crow and says plainly that Black people have legitimately been victims, and then he refuses to let that become a permanent identity. The seventh is watchfulness. In Queen of the Coast and New World Order in America he warns of the occult and of the powers of the age, sure that the last days are on us. Under all of it runs the call he never dropped: repent, come to Jesus, bear your own cross, and come to church. Now weigh it, because that is what he asked for and what the Word requires: "Prove all things; hold fast that which is good." (1 Thessalonians 5:21). Much of what he taught the Word confirms plainly. Our fight is not with flesh and blood. Unforgiveness is a prison. Love never fails. Knowledge without love puffs up. A man should lead his house by laying his life down for it. Bitterness defiles many. On those points he is simply right, and he said them with a force that has helped many people. In a few places the Word and the record correct a specific claim, and grace means saying so plainly. Where he spoke as if psychology were only a cover for demons, the Word itself is more careful: Matthew records that Jesus healed the sick, the lunatick and the possessed, naming them as different needs, and Darby himself told his hearers to keep taking their medicine. Where he spoke in sweeping terms about Black women, the Word gives every woman and every man the same honor in Christ. In Willie Lynch Syndrome he treats the Willie Lynch letter as a real slave master’s speech; historians have shown it to be a twentieth-century composition, in print by 1970, though the divisions it describes are real and the Word speaks to them. In the media broadcast he says that the people now called Jews are false Jews who own the media and the banks, and he speaks of the six million as if the number were a tool for winning sympathy; in Willie Lynch Syndrome he lays the entertainment business and the slave ships at the same door. That accusation against a whole people is old and false. The murder of about six million Jews in the Holocaust is established history, and the Word gives no believer license for contempt toward any people; Paul writes to Gentile believers about Israel, "Boast not against the branches" (Romans 11:18). On the question of Black Hebrew identity he spoke two ways; in Strife he said he had studied it and had not seen the link, and that without Jesus no lineage saves anyone, and on that last point the Word is with him, for it says of Jesus that "there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved" (Acts 4:12). Where he names spirits like the Queen of the Coast, those names and ranks come from West African deliverance tradition, not from Scripture; what Scripture does teach is a real unseen enemy and a risen Christ who holds all power. So how should we receive him? Not by swallowing everything and not by dismissing the man. Discernment is not a hedge; it is the work of keeping what is true and letting the Word correct what is not, the same Test we run on every voice and on ourselves. He was a former hustler who loved Jesus, loved his wife, raised his sons, and spent his voice telling a hurting people that their pain was real and that they did not have to stay in it. Hear him in his own words in the lessons that follow, and weigh every line by the Word, as he asked.

## 2. Pastor Darby — Transference: stop fighting the wrong fight

*id: `pv-darby-transference` · 1809 words*

**The big idea.** In the first broadcast of 2016, Pastor Darby taught that a believer’s real fight is never with the person in front of him but with the spiritual powers behind the conflict, and that one of the enemy’s chief strategies is transference: an old wound that was never healed, from a parent, a former partner or an abuser, gets taken out on a new and neutral relationship, a spouse, a friend, a church. His answer is to stop fighting the wrong fight, to look back honestly at the pattern, to bring the wound to the cross, and to carry your own cross instead of making someone else pay for your pain, helping one another without becoming each other’s crutch.

Pastor Stephen Darby’s sermon Transference aired on Sunday night, 3 January 2016, on Next Chapter Radio, an internet program whose host had decided to give the whole year to his preaching. It was the first broadcast of the series, and the host introduced it by defining the word in her own way: somebody treated me wrong, I never dealt with it, and now you come along and I take it out on you. Then the recording begins, and you are in his church in Louisville, where the people answer him and he keeps asking, are y’all there? He laid a foundation first, and he called it the scripture that tells us to stop fighting the wrong fight: "For we wrestle not against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers, against the rulers of the darkness of this world, against spiritual wickedness in high places." (Ephesians 6:12). He drew out the point at once: "As a Christian, once I see the person, I’ve lost the fight." A moment later he said it again: "Once I start focusing on the person, I’ve lost the fight." A natural enemy you can knock down, he said, but a spiritual enemy calls for the weapons of that realm. So he went to Paul: "(For the weapons of our warfare are not carnal, but mighty through God to the pulling down of strong holds;)" (2 Corinthians 10:4). He taught that our weapons are words spoken in the name of Jesus, prayer and binding and loosing, and he took apart the playground rhyme about sticks and stones, saying words cut deeper and stay in the heart long after bruises heal. Every person who comes to you, he said, comes in some spirit, good or bad, and "Your job is to discern what spirit" you are dealing with. The enemy’s strategy is to pull you out of the spirit and into the natural, which is why he fights the very things that build you up: prayer, fasting, reading the Word, being in church. Unforgiveness, he said, is fighting the wrong fight. You think you are holding the person who hurt you; you are the one bound. "Always ask yourself, what’s behind this?" he said, and he urged the church not to repay evil for evil, because doing good for evil breaks the power of the spirit behind the attack. Then he took a detour that we will weigh below. He argued that modern psychology renames what Scripture calls spirits, so that what was once called a spirit of lust is now called an addiction, and he claimed that psychology was formed by atheists to explain away possession and that its drugs sedate the flesh but never cure the spirit. He pointed to the boy in the Gospels who threw himself into fire and water until Jesus cast out the spirit. He said that family illnesses like diabetes and high blood pressure are generational curses. But he also said, plainly, "Keep taking your pills. I ain’t telling you to stop. I ain’t even against it." Then he reached what he called his turn. The main goal of the enemy’s warfare, he said, is to move your attention from spiritual things to people, especially to your spouse, your friends, your pastor and your church. He does it through what Darby called a pre-issue: a traumatic experience already inside you, never resolved. Mama abandoned me; daddy abandoned me. Before people are saved, he said, they drink over it, smoke over it, sleep around over it. "You go into sin trying to escape something," he said, and, "Sin is an escape." Escape brings a brief release but no healing. And many believers, he said, came to the altar, said a prayer, and never went back to deal with the wickedness and wounds in their hearts so the Lord could fill those places. So the wound comes out on the next person. A woman whose last man left her cannot trust the man who stays. A man whose mother never nurtured him takes it out on his wife. "What are they doing? They’re transferring to you a pre-wound." He noticed that people often hold the person who really hurt them in the highest esteem, a sacred cow nobody may criticize, while the spouse pays for everything. He read a definition aloud: transference is an emotional attempt to get relief from old pain by making the people in a neutral relationship make up for it. In his words, I am making you pay. He brought it into the church. People join, praise the preaching, and within a month say the pastor hurt them, when they have not known him long enough for him to hurt them; the ones closest to us are the only ones who can really wound us, he said, which is why Jesus felt the betrayal of His disciples. And he went to the pool of Bethesda, where a man had lain thirty-eight years. Jesus asked him, "Wilt thou be made whole?" (John 5:6) and the man answered with complaint that no one would put him in the water. "Not everybody wants to be well," Darby said, and, "Not everybody wants to be well because sometimes complaining and being difficult, it becomes a lifestyle." A wound, he said, is like a vacuum that keeps sucking the life out of everyone near it; when it is healed it is sealed, and the person finally has something to give. Feeding the vacuum only makes it stronger. He said transference also shows up as unrealistic demands, wanting a spouse to make up for everything you never got, and in parents who dress little girls to look grown, which he said always reveals something wrong in the parent’s mind. From there he spoke at length about marriage, about the quiet strength of a wise woman and the need for a man to lead, and he said that Adam was cursed for listening to his wife. Near the end he named two Bible examples of transference. John the Baptist, in prison, sent to ask Jesus whether He was the one, and Darby read that as blame laid on the leader for his own situation. And at Ziklag, when David’s men lost their wives and children, they spoke of stoning David, and David went to pray. His remedy came from Luke: "And whosoever doth not bear his cross, and come after me, cannot be my disciple." (Luke 14:27). He applied it plainly: "Nobody is responsible for your pain." Even if a person wronged you, a Christian cannot make them pay; you must forgive if you want to be forgiven. If you do not carry your own cross, he said, you will spend your life looking for someone to carry it for you, and leave marriages and churches when they refuse. So he gave two tools. First, "Ask yourself why am I getting mad?" If the person in front of you did not cause it, you are transferring. Second, look back over your relationships and see the same scene repeated: "That’s your pattern." And he closed with balance. He helps his wife in everything, he said, but helping is different from being a crutch; enabling keeps people as they are. "But I cannot be your crutch." Now weigh it by the Word. On his foundation he is simply right. Ephesians 6:12 and 2 Corinthians 10:4 teach exactly what he preached: our wrestling is not against people, and our weapons are not carnal. The Word tells us how to fight that fight, "Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good." (Romans 12:21) and "be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another" (Ephesians 4:32). His reading of Bethesda is faithful; Jesus did ask the man whether he would be made whole. His closing balance is the Word’s own balance. Paul writes "Bear ye one another’s burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ." (Galatians 6:2) and three verses later, "For every man shall bear his own burden." (Galatians 6:5). We carry one another, and each of us carries what only he can carry to Jesus. Darby’s line about the crutch is a good summary of those two verses. In a few places the Word corrects a specific claim, and grace means saying so plainly. The Gospels do record Jesus casting a spirit out of a tormented boy, and they also keep needs apart; Matthew writes that the people brought Him "those which were possessed with devils, and those which were lunatick, and those that had the palsy; and he healed them" (Matthew 4:24). Not every sickness is a spirit, and not every affliction is a curse; of the man born blind Jesus said "Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents" (John 9:3). ## 3. Pastor Darby — The debating spirit of strife

*id: `pv-darby-debating-spirit-of-strife` · 1607 words*

**The big idea.** Pastor Darby taught that the adversary is a debater who comes with a question, while Yahweh is a declarer who speaks with authority, and that a debating spirit of strife had swept the body of Christ: believers who hunt teachers online, label anyone who differs a false teacher, attack the leaders who once fed them, and isolate themselves from the church. He called believers to honor what Yahweh did through a fallen teacher without following his error, to know those who speak into their lives before receiving them, to refuse slander that has no first-hand witness, and to explain their faith without striving, because strife is a work the Word names and Yahweh hates.

Pastor Stephen Darby’s The Debating Spirit of Strife aired on Next Chapter Radio on 10 January 2016, a week after Transference. The host introduced it with a story of her own: a stranger, not even on her friends list, had attacked Darby online after the first broadcast without listening to the message. She told listeners strife was a tactic to pull believers away from prayer and into their feelings. Then the recording begins mid-thought, in Darby’s church, and he goes straight to the young believer. His first point was about growth. When you are new in the faith, he said, Yahweh can use almost any minister to feed you, but then a heretic hunter comes along and says, did you know this about him, and the young believer is cut off, stops trusting anyone, and stops growing. Most ministers do not start out false, he said; they fall, the same way any believer can, under heavier attack. When a leader you followed gets off course, you get off with him, the way you follow a man only as he follows Christ, but you do not go back and stab him through the heart. He said it plainly: "I didn’t say I’m going to follow you but I’m going to honor you for what God used you to do in my life." He compared it to the command to honor father and mother, which we need precisely when we are old enough to see our parents’ faults. Of a preacher in error he said, "He’s just preaching something wrong," and that does not mean he is unsaved; Yahweh can reel him back in. Next he spoke to the isolated. Many of these attackers, he said, do not go to church at all. They sit at home offended with everybody, like Elijah in the cave saying everyone else has bowed the knee, and they hide behind fake names online like snipers. But believers cannot grow alone. When Moses asked what name to give Pharaoh, Yahweh answered "I AM THAT I AM" (Exodus 3:14), and by the end of the plagues even Pharaoh’s magicians confessed "This is the finger of God" (Exodus 8:19). One day in prayer he renounced every false word spoken over him and his mind cleared. "This word is better than my word," he said, meaning the written Word over every spoken one, his own included. And the servant of the Lord must not strive; but be gentle unto all men, apt to teach, patient" (2 Timothy 2:23-24). Paul tells Titus to avoid foolish questions and genealogies and contentions, because they are unprofitable and vain. Knowledge without love does puff up, as Paul wrote to Corinth. The Word does require two or three witnesses before a matter is established, and it does list among the seven things Yahweh hates "he that soweth discord among brethren" (Proverbs 6:19). On the bloodline question, the Word is with him: Peter preached that "there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved" (Acts 4:12), and Paul wrote "ye are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28). Where he spoke of the Egyptian myths, the Word’s own answer is sufficient; it never borrowed its Christ from anyone. Where he told witchcraft testimonies from Nigeria, they are his report of what he was told, and they rest on that; what the Word guarantees is that darkness is real and that "greater is he that is in you, than he that is in the world" (1 John 4:4). One thing in this sermon we do not carry forward: in telling one of his stories he exposed an anonymous critic publicly and accused him of pledging himself to a false god. The Word he preached corrects that moment too; love does not answer strife with strife. What does it mean now? More Christians argue online than ever. His sermon gives a plain path. Stay in a church where people know you. Know before you receive. Honor those the Lord used, even when you cannot follow them. Ask whether a story has a witness. Explain your faith gently and stop when it turns to striving. Paul’s word is the summary: "Let nothing be done through strife or vainglory" (Philippians 2:3).

Children, ask your parents or a grandparent what they remember. Friends, talk it over and name one teacher the Lord used in your life and thank Him for that person together.

## 4. Pastor Darby — The seed of hate: when hurt turns to hate

*id: `pv-darby-seed-of-hate` · 1668 words*

**The big idea.** Pastor Darby taught that the adversary’s chief weapon in the last days is to get believers to trade love for knowledge, and that hate rarely arrives as hate: it is planted as a hurt that never healed, which festers, turns to hate, makes a person cynical and unteachable, and grows through the stages of Psalm 1, from listening to ungodly counsel, to standing among sinners, to sitting in the seat of the scornful where bitterness defiles others. Because the heart is deceitful, he said, only the Word can show us what is in it; the cure is conviction, confession, transparency, and the love of 1 Corinthians 13, which never fails.

Pastor Stephen Darby’s The Seed of Hate aired on Next Chapter Radio on 7 February 2016. The recording begins in the middle of his church service, and partway through he decides on the title out loud: seat of hate, no, seed of hate, he says, that is a better word. He said he was working the message out as he preached it, from the calls and emails his ministry received every day. His starting point was the remnant. The enemy, he said, will wear out some saints in the last days, but never all of them, because Yahweh always keeps a remnant. The danger is what he called spiritual anemia, believers whose love has grown cold without their knowing it. Then he gave his thesis: "satan’s number one weapon in the last days is to make you change love for knowledge." Knowledge is not the strongest power, he said. "The most dominant power is love." And he defined it so no one could mistake it for softness: "The God kind of love is not a love that agrees with sin." Love brings direction, he said: "Love brings clear direction." Love is the one power the enemy has no answer for, because love is what defeated him at the cross, when Jesus prayed for His executioners instead of cursing them. "But love is the ultimate weapon." He described the people who called and wrote to his ministry. Many were online all day gathering knowledge, but were not in a church, and said nobody was real any more. "You have a cynicism based upon your hurt and then your hurt is turning into hate," he said, and, "Healthy people are around people." He said the movements of the day, on every side of every question, were motivated less by what they loved than by what they hated, and that a person carrying an unhealed hurt is easy to recruit into someone else’s organized hate. He called this the disillusionment Jesus foretold: "And because iniquity shall abound, the love of many shall wax cold." (Matthew 24:12). He pictured it as a cup of hot coffee set out in the snow. It does not go cold all at once; it grows cold, slowly, to the temperature of its surroundings. So if you are growing cold, look at what you have surrounded yourself with. He made a hard point about criticism. People now call every correction hate, he said, but when someone tells you your wig is crooked, that is not a hater; it is the truth about you. "Change is not possible where there’s no receiving of corrective criticism." A social-media mind, where everything gets a like and there is no dislike, makes people take any disagreement as rejection. Then he traced where the seeds come from: what we saw in school, racism, the police, the news, the movies; a mother who left, a father who never stayed, a husband or wife who cheated. We say we forgave, he said, but secretly a seed of hate is growing. You know a seed is there when you see a cycle, the same thing over and over. And because the original hurt was never healed, it festers like an open sore, so that every new person who comes close presses on it, and we take them for the source: "they didn’t bring the pain, they just touched the pain." You can spend fifty years focusing on the people who touched the wound and never get the source healed. Why can’t we see it? He went to Proverbs: "The words of a talebearer are as wounds, and they go down into the innermost parts of the belly." (Proverbs 18:8). The wounds are inside, in the heart. And the heart, he said, cannot judge itself, because "The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked: who can know it?" (Jeremiah 17:9). So we need something that can. He read "For the word of God is quick, and powerful, and sharper than any twoedged sword, piercing even to the dividing asunder of soul and spirit, and of the joints and marrow, and is a discerner of the thoughts and intents of the heart." (Hebrews 4:12). The Word brings conviction, he said, and conviction is the Word revealing what is truly in my heart; when I repent of what it shows me, my deliverance begins. Without conviction a person keeps saying, I just haven’t met the right man, the right woman, the right church. He spoke of Paul calling himself the chief of sinners at the height of his ministry, and of himself: "I know so much He showed me about me. I ain’t got time to talk about nobody else." Salvation saves the spirit, he said, but the engrafted Word keeps saving the soul, the mind, will and emotions, where things are still hidden that the Lord means to bring out. He then laid the seed of hate over Psalm 1: "Blessed is the man that walketh not in the counsel of the ungodly, nor standeth in the way of sinners, nor sitteth in the seat of the scornful." (Psalms 1:1). First, a hurt person rejects love, because he wants his day in court, and goes looking for counsel among the ungodly, an unsaved sister, a talk-show guru, new age teachers. Next he stands in the way of sinners, back in the club or the old crowd, finding more comfort in worldly music than in the music of the church. Last he sits in the seat of the scornful, which Darby called bitterness. He tied it to the root of bitterness that defiles many: "It’s one thing to have bad fruit. It’s another thing to turn good fruit bad." The bitter person wants your marriage to be as bad as his. He showed how the seed wrecks relationships. Some people are single, he said, not because no one chose them but because the seed of hate drove the chooser away, trying to mold the other person into someone who could never hurt them. It wrecks faith too, because faith works by love: "So if you notice people that are full of hate have no faith." He read 1 Corinthians 13 and said that without love even accurate prophecy is noise. Many couples argue about the television or the bills, he said, when the real issue is "Longing to be loved but too fearful to trust." Love is a risk, because it requires us to open up. Near the end he turned to the cross. Jesus’ goal for us is that we grow into His likeness, and the climax of His life was total exposure; He hung naked before the world. "Crucifixion is the ultimate exposure." Adam reached for fig leaves; Yahweh keeps bringing our hidden things to light, he said, not to shame us but because there is no glory in fixing someone nobody knew was broken, and because open confession is how healing comes. He closed with love that never fails. Even if loving costs you, he said, you never lose, because you get the will of Yahweh. Then he balanced it: "And love ain’t no doormat." "Love will tell the truth." He warned his church about the hatred online, "You should not absorb more of worldliness and hatred than you absorb love," and urged real fellowship over five thousand online friends. And he left them with a choice: "If you’re not growing in love, then you’re growing in hate." He ended by leading the church in a prayer for deliverance from the seed of hate and the root of bitterness. Weigh it by the Word. Almost every step stands on the text. Jesus did warn that iniquity would cause the love of many to wax cold. Paul did write that knowledge puffs up while love edifies, and that "faith which worketh by love" (Galatians 5:6) is what avails in Christ. Hebrews does warn "lest any root of bitterness springing up trouble you, and thereby many be defiled" (Hebrews 12:15), which is exactly his teaching on the seat of the scornful. John writes "There is no fear in love; but perfect love casteth out fear" (1 John 4:18), the answer to the fear of trust he named, and James writes "Confess your faults one to another, and pray one for another, that ye may be healed" (James 5:16), which is his call to transparency. His word that love is no doormat and tells the truth is Paul’s own, "speaking the truth in love" (Ephesians 4:15). ## 5. Pastor Darby — Emotional witchcraft: victim psychology

*id: `pv-darby-emotional-witchcraft` · 1784 words*

**The big idea.** Pastor Darby taught that what the world calls victim psychology the Word would call emotional witchcraft: using your emotions to control someone else’s, through manipulation, intimidation and domination. Drawing on Martha in Luke 10, he showed how a person who takes on what nobody asked of her, and then is not applauded, turns the hurt into blame and appeals for pity to control others. He said such a person never sees himself as the one doing harm, consumes people instead of enjoying them, and holds others hostage; and he insisted that even those who have truly been wronged, as Black Americans have been, must break the habit by going to the cross and saying, it is me.

Pastor Stephen Darby’s Emotional Witchcraft / Victim Psychology aired on Next Chapter Radio on 1 February 2016. The host warned listeners it went in a different direction from his earlier broadcasts and said it described something almost everyone has met: people who make themselves the victim to make you feel bad and act the way they want. Then the recording opens on Darby, and he goes straight to the title, admitting nobody else would preach on it. He began with definitions. "But the modern term for emotional witchcraft is victim psychology." Victim psychology, he said, is the mindset of the modern self-centered person; spiritually it is witchcraft. "That means someone is using their emotions to control yours." He explained: "witchcraft is control," control through manipulation, intimidation and domination, to trick, scare and force. Emotional witchcraft does that with feelings. The modern person, he said, has been trained in it by reality television, where people manipulate each other and are never wrong. It is hard to confront, because victims always think they are the victim. "I taught you all before that victims always end up victimizing." A child who was hurt grows into an adult who hurts; a child raised without affection grows into a parent who will not hug his own children and justifies it. At root, he said, it is narcissism, a person who justifies the pain he causes while never seeing his own ways. His Bible text was Luke 10. Jesus came to the house of Mary and Martha. Mary sat at His feet, doing what she was meant to do; Martha busied herself with things, he said, that no one had told her to do. "When you do what you’re not told to do, you create frustration." Martha did not get the recognition she expected for her work, and she became offended at the sister who was sitting at Jesus’ feet. So she appealed to Him: "But Martha was cumbered about much serving, and came to him, and said, Lord, dost thou not care that my sister hath left me to serve alone? bid her therefore that she help me." (Luke 10:40). Darby called that an emotional appeal meant to control. Jesus, he said, told her she was doing too much, worried about too much, and had never learned to stay in her lane; and instead of admitting it, she blamed Mary as if Mary were lazy. He brought it home with a smile: many of our mothers and grandmothers cook the big ham nobody asked for, then are offended that nobody appreciates it. If it were really for Jesus, he asked, why would she be upset about the work? He described how it looks in families: the relative who blows up at you, disappears, and comes back weeks later asking about the children as if nothing happened, never repenting, holding everyone hostage until he decides to be all right. He said the culture now applauds victimhood, and he told of a sentencing he had watched online in which a mother who had left her daughters with a man who abused them stood in court speaking as if she were the victim, and the judge told her she was not. He said people stuck in victimhood are always jealous of those who chose right, as if another couple’s faithful marriage owed them something. He read aloud a psychological description of victim mentality, a person who avoids responsibility by blaming others, and said psychology can name the problem but not cure it: "Psychology can diagnose it. They can’t deal with it." He said it often begins in childhood, especially with abandonment, when a child learns that being vulnerable means getting hurt and so decides always to be in control, and therefore always to be right. Such a person keeps a double standard and believes the world owes him. "We don’t enjoy people. We consume people," he said. I cannot enjoy your ways; I must make you into me before I feel safe. His test was simple: "Tell them no." Watch, he said, how fast the knives come out. ## 6. Pastor Darby — Pornography: the open door, the broken wall, and the renewed mind

*id: `pv-darby-pornography` · 1627 words*

**The big idea.** In this sermon Pastor Darby calls pornography the most destructive thing a culture can accept. He argues that it teaches a man (and a woman) to see an object instead of a person, that the adversary works through a weakness a person already has, and that every look removes a brick from the wall that guards the soul until the person is addicted. Its wages are loss: marriages, children, honor. His remedy is the Word: confess it as adultery of the heart, guard the house, monitor the screens, reach for help, and renew the mind until you see your spouse as a person again.

The setting matters. Pastor Stephen Darby pastored Destined Ministries in Louisville, Kentucky, and taught a large and growing audience online. Early in 2016 a Christian internet program, Next Chapter Radio, began playing his sermons one per week, and on 17 January 2016 it played this one: nearly ninety minutes on pornography. The host warned listeners that it was a sensitive subject and told them to have their Bibles open. Darby himself had been saved, by his own account in another sermon, for about twenty years; he had grown up in the streets, the clubs and the music, and he preached as a man who knew the territory. He opened without any hedging. At 4:27 he says, "This is the most destructive thing that any culture has ever accepted." His frame was a culture in decline. Before a people falls, he argued, it turns its back on the true God, Yahweh, begins to worship the creature rather than the Creator, embraces sexual practices Yahweh called wrong, and then reaps the consequences. That is Paul’s own sequence in Romans 1, and he believed his own country was walking it. Here is his argument step by step. Step one: a generation has learned sex wrong. When he was young, he said, a boy had to hunt to find pornography, and might keep one magazine for a year or two. Now it comes straight to a phone, and children can get past the blocks on a computer. At 7:00 he says, "we got a generation of young men who grew up on it." A child has not yet built the ability to tell fantasy from reality, so what he sees on a screen he takes as real. At 8:20 he draws the conclusion: "most men that you meet learn sex wrong," and many spend the rest of their lives chasing the first rush rather than learning intimacy, love and how to protect a woman. Step two: pornography turns persons into objects. At 8:53 he says that "what pornography does to your mind is make everything an object of your desire or the object of your lust." Later, at 57:07, he puts it more sharply: it "trains you not to see the person but see the object of your release." He warned that this is how a man stops seeing a daughter, a niece or a sister as family, and he said the same corruption works in women. That is why he linked it to the abuse of children in the home, and why he urged parents to know who is in the house and what is on its screens. Step three: temptation comes through a weakness. He took the congregation to James 1 and read that a person is tempted "when he is drawn away of his own lust, and enticed" (James 1:14). Every person has his own lust, his own tendency, he said; his weakness is not yours. The adversary studies that weakness the way the Philistines studied Samson, and often, he argued, works through a family for generations, wounding one generation so that the next goes searching to fill the wound. Step four: sin is a wall coming down. His central picture was a wall around the mind, the will and the emotions. While the wall stands, the enemy cannot get in. Each time a person gives in, a brick comes out. At 37:11 he says, "I’ve created an opening for the enemy to come in." That, he said, is addiction: a person can mean it when he says never again and still lack the power to carry it out, because the wall is down. He rested this on a proverb: "He that hath no rule over his own spirit is like a city that is broken down, and without walls." (Proverbs 25:28). Step five: the wages are paid now. James finishes the chain: "Then when lust hath conceived, it bringeth forth sin: and sin, when it is finished, bringeth forth death." (James 1:15). At 40:04 Darby says, "death always means loss," and he named the losses: marriages, children, money, jobs, reputation, honor. People talk about not being judged, he said, while the judgment is already showing in their lives. Step six: Jesus moved the line to the heart. He read findings that married men who use pornography feel less satisfied with their wives and less emotionally attached to them, that wives notice and are hurt by the difference, that users become desensitized and seek more extreme material, and that the main defenses are a close family life, a good marriage, good relationships between parents and children and deliberate monitoring of the internet. He spoke from the pastor’s chair too: he said many Christian wives had come to him with the same question, sensing that something was wrong in their marriage. What did he call his hearers to do? First, guard the house. At 80:45 he says, "God gonna hold you accountable for what you let in your house," and at 82:55 he tells parents to "stop allowing the culture to dictate your house." Know what is on a child’s phone; a young child does not need a device with the whole internet on it. Hold the standard high even if you failed at that age yourself. At 86:46 he says, "you have to be wise enough to realize you can’t control it" by your own strength. Then he told his own story. As a young man he was very strong and relied on his strength, held everything inside, and leaned on no one, not even his wife. At 88:20 he says, "it’s real funny how when you don’t have your strength you’ll reach out." When his strength failed, his marriage grew. Fourth, renew the mind. At 84:34 he admits for all of us, "we learned sex wrong," and at 85:54 he says the goal is to "renew your mind so you start seeing her and not an object." Talk to your wife about her day. Wives, look at your husband and see him. Now weigh it by the Word. On the heart, he is exactly right: Jesus placed adultery in the look. On the chain of lust, sin and death, he is reading James as James wrote it. On the wall, the proverb says what he said. *id: `pv-darby-pied-pipers` · 1466 words*

**The big idea.** Pastor Darby takes the old tale of the piper who led a town’s children away with his music and applies it to the church. Music and celebrity, he argues, are never neutral; famous people who enter the church without real change can pipe a compromised message to the young, as Simon the sorcerer was baptized yet kept a heart that was not right. Because judgment begins at the house of God, the church must weigh what comes inside, guard its sheep from wolves however famous, change what it listens to, and keep a quick, repentant heart.

The setting matters. Pastor Stephen Darby pastored Destined Ministries in Louisville, Kentucky, and by the mid-2010s his sermons reached far beyond his congregation. On 22 February 2016 the Christian internet program Next Chapter Radio played this one, Beware of the Pied Pipers, and its host introduced it as a warning about anyone who uses song, dance and entertainment to lead people away from Yahweh. The years before it had seen celebrities move freely between the stage and the church: entertainers giving testimonies, worldly artists featured on gospel records, and Christian television hosting best-selling relationship advice. Darby, who had come out of the streets and the clubs himself, preached as a man who knew how strong that pull is. He began with a children’s tale. In the legend of the Pied Piper of Hamelin, a piper dressed in many colors offered to rid the town of its rats with his pipe; the rats followed his music to the river; the town refused to pay; and the piper came back and played again, and the children followed him away. Darby told it as if from London, but the legend belongs to Hamelin in Lower Saxony, Germany, where an old town record says that in 1284, on 26 June, 130 children were lured away by a piper. The phrase pied piper came to mean a person who attracts a following through charisma or false promises, and that is the sense Darby used. Here is his argument, step by step. Step one: music and influence are never neutral. At 5:13 he says "we really think music is neutral and that’s the worst thing to think." All music, he said, carries a spirit; the piper did not drag anyone, he played, and they followed. Step two: the church is full but unchanged. At 6:13 he says "We have a lot of church but not a lot of holiness," and he added that we have a lot of church but not a lot of change. The adversary, he argued, has used a Trojan horse: a gift welcomed into the city with soldiers hidden inside. Step three: judgment begins at the house of God. He was careful on this point. The church does not judge the world; the world judges itself. But Peter wrote, "For the time is come that judgment must begin at the house of God: and if it first begin at us, what shall the end be of them that obey not the gospel of God?" (1 Peter 4:17) and Paul asked, "do not ye judge them that are within?" (1 Corinthians 5:12) When someone carries worldly influence into the church, the church has the duty to weigh it. He accepted that duty for himself too. At 33:31 he says, "If I say something wrong, man, get up and leave." And at 27:02 he praised a listener who corrected a verse he had misquoted: "you are never going to be deceived. You know why? Because you actually look up what I said." Step four: Simon the sorcerer. He read Acts 8. Simon had amazed Samaria with sorcery. When Philip preached Christ, "Then Simon himself believed also: and when he was baptized, he continued with Philip, and wondered, beholding the miracles and signs which were done." (Acts 8:13) But when Simon saw the Spirit given through the apostles’ hands, he offered money for the same power, and Peter answered, "Thou hast neither part nor lot in this matter: for thy heart is not right in the sight of God." (Acts 8:21) At 32:19 Darby says "he was in the house but not of the house." Simon followed the man of God to learn the source of the power, so that he could go back to manipulating the people. That, Darby said, is the pied piper’s heart. Step five: the modern pied pipers. He named public messages that had entered the church on the strength of fame. He criticized a comedian’s best-selling relationship book, Act Like a Lady, Think Like a Man (2009), for counseling Christian women to wait ninety days rather than waiting for marriage; the counsel was big enough that the 2012 film made from the book bills one character as "The 90 Day Rule Girl." He criticized a stage-and-film franchise beloved in Black churches for, in his reading, making the church people hypocrites and fools while a profane grandmother delivered the wisdom. And he pointed to a singer whom the church embraced in a season of scandal, whose gospel-style songs were sung in sanctuaries. The record on that singer tells on itself: the industry nicknamed him "the Pied Piper of R&B"; his 2004 double album Happy People/U Saved Me paired dance tracks with inspirational ones, and reporters noted that many fans found the shifts between the inspirational and the X-rated jarring; he was acquitted in 2008, and then in 2021 and 2022 he was convicted of crimes involving child sexual abuse and sex trafficking and sentenced to a combined 31 years. At 36:22 Darby says "a wolf is a wolf," famous or not, and he charged pastors who love celebrity more than their sheep with failing their charge. Step six: what you behold has power. At 17:00 he says "what you behold has power." Companies pay millions for a minute of advertising because sight and sound sell; a drug commercial with soft music sells what its spoken side effects would never sell. He drew on Jacob’s striped rods in Genesis 30, the flocks bringing forth "cattle ringstraked, speckled, and spotted" (Genesis 30:39) after looking at them. What did he call his hearers to do? Change what you listen to. At 51:58 he testified that "the strongest trouble I had when I got saved was giving that up": two thousand dollars of CDs that kept pulling him back to the clubs. Test every voice, including his. Love the people you warn: at 53:59 he says "all the people I mentioned I love them," and at 59:26, "I’m praying for all of them people to get saved." And repent quickly. At 62:59 he says "the trick is it’s to always have a quick repentant heart." Now weigh it by the Word. Jesus gave the same warning and the test: "Beware of false prophets, which come to you in sheep’s clothing, but inwardly they are ravening wolves." (Matthew 7:15) "Ye shall know them by their fruits" (Matthew 7:16). Paul warned the elders at Ephesus that grievous wolves would enter in, not sparing the flock, and charged them to feed the church (Acts 20:28-29). On what we behold, Paul agrees with Darby and tells us what to fill the mind with: "Finally, brethren, whatsoever things are true, whatsoever things are honest, whatsoever things are just, whatsoever things are pure, whatsoever things are lovely, whatsoever things are of good report; if there be any virtue, and if there be any praise, think on these things." (Philippians 4:8) The Word adds balances to three of his points. On Jacob’s rods, Jacob himself later testified that the increase came from Yahweh, not the sticks: "Thus God hath taken away the cattle of your father, and given them to me." (Genesis 31:9) What we behold shapes us, but the power to change us belongs to Him. On the origin of the adversary’s music, Ezekiel 28:13 mentions tabrets and pipes in the making of the covering cherub, and the Word goes no further. On Simon, the story does not end in condemnation: Peter called him to repent: "Repent therefore of this thy wickedness, and pray God" (Acts 8:22). Three details of his evidence are corrected by the record: the legend is German, not English; Franz Mesmer, whom he called a rabbi who invented hypnotic beats, was a German physician educated at Jesuit universities whose theory was animal magnetism; and the music industry is owned by corporations, not five people. His remarks about the private lives of named living people are left out, because what cannot be established must not be passed on. What does it mean for us now? The pipers are louder than ever, in our ears all day. So we change what we feed on, we welcome correction, we protect the young, and we pray for the famous instead of following them. As his ministry’s own statement of faith admits, "The flesh is never eradicated in this life." That is why the quick, repentant heart he preached is not a weakness but the daily way of a disciple.

Children, ask your parents or a grandparent what they remember. Friends, talk it over and swap one song you listen to for one that honors the King.

## 11. Pastor Darby — The Effeminization of Male Leadership: the Ahab in the house of God

*id: `pv-darby-effeminization` · 1870 words*

**The big idea.** In this sit-down teaching Pastor Darby argues that the church has slowly trained its men to be soft where they should be strong: rewarded for passivity, taught to hand every decision to the pastor or to a word from the Lord, and used to fighting by going behind a brother’s back instead of talking to his face. Pastor Stephen Darby recorded The Effeminization of Male Leadership on a Saturday as what he called a sit-down teaching, meant to be posted online, and the internet program Next Chapter Radio broadcast it on 14 February 2016, Valentine’s Day. He said he chose to sit down rather than preach from his pulpit so he could be more thorough, and he said he might make a series of it, because he believed this was at the heart of many of the problems in the church. The setting matters. Darby pastored Destined Ministries in Louisville, Kentucky, a congregation he built after a dramatic conversion that he describes in this very message. He had been a drug dealer. Before he ever went to church he read his Bible and won people to Jesus, and when he finally joined a congregation he was shocked by what he found among the men: men who looked strong on the outside and then would not speak to a brother straight, would fall out over a disagreement, and would work behind his back for months. He began by grounding his right to speak. The world, he said, is judged by its own sin; the church is to judge itself, because the Bible says judgment begins at the house of God. He pointed to the prophets and apostles who brought correction, and he said the ministry of setting the church in order is largely missing because preachers fear crossing the powerful. The fourth step is his text from Isaiah 3, which he read as a picture of a people under judgment: the mighty man and the judge taken away, the daughters of Zion walking with stretched-forth necks, and the line "As for my people, children are their oppressors, and women rule over them. O my people, they which lead thee cause thee to err, and destroy the way of thy paths." (Isaiah 3:12) He read it to say that when men are falling in the streets or have backed away, women end up carrying the leadership. He added at 36:35, "I’m not saying a woman can’t be in leadership," and at 25:33, "Man, this is not against women."

The fifth and longest step is Ahab. In 1 Kings 21, Naboth refused to sell the vineyard of his fathers to the king. Darby admired Naboth, who knew his rights and answered like a man. Ahab, by contrast, went home heavy and displeased, lay on his bed, turned away his face and would not eat. Jezebel asked whether he governed Israel, then wrote letters in his name, sealed them with his seal, and had Naboth falsely accused and stoned. Darby says the Ahab in the story is the main problem, because he vacated his authority and so opened the door. He applies it sharply: the man who will not work but wants to preach, the minister who says the Lord is calling him while his wife pays every bill. His line at 52:38 is, "They always trade their responsibility for spirituality."

His call to action is concrete. Pastors must stop putting men in leadership before those men are proven at home. He gives an order he finds in Eden: the Word of Yahweh, then work, then a place to live, then a wife. At 63:05 he says it is because "work is spiritual," and he explains that a job teaches a man to get up, to finish what he starts, and to submit to a supervisor. He tells how the Lord told him to get a job when he wanted to go straight into preaching, and how he ran his own construction company for more than ten years, still running it while he pastored, and learned endurance from it. At 65:07 he warns, "We are putting people in ministry that have not proven themselves as men first." He tells parents to send soft boys out to work around strong men, and he urges every man to handle conflict as a man: at 70:19, "Men should be able to say, listen brother, this is how I feel, this is what I think." And he closes with his hope, at 69:03: "And I believe we’ll give our women something they can be proud of instead of something they have to tolerate."

Jesus Himself told a wronged brother where to start: "Moreover if thy brother shall trespass against thee, go and tell him his fault between thee and him alone: if he shall hear thee, thou hast gained thy brother." (Matthew 18:15) The Word ties leadership in the church to faithfulness at home: "(For if a man know not how to rule his own house, how shall he take care of the church of God?)" (1 Timothy 3:5) It calls a man who will not provide for his household worse than an unbeliever (1 Timothy 5:8), and it says plainly that if any would not work, neither should he eat (2 Thessalonians 3:10). Adam was given the garden to dress and keep before Eve was brought to him (Genesis 2:15). And Darby’s favorite proverb from this message is true for every man and woman: "He that hath no rule over his own spirit is like a city that is broken down, and without walls." (Proverbs 25:28) On the central story, the Word puts the blame where Darby put it: "But there was none like unto Ahab, which did sell himself to work wickedness in the sight of the LORD, whom Jezebel his wife stirred up." (1 Kings 21:25)

In a few places he spoke more broadly than the Word, and the Word corrects those claims narrowly. First, he often called gossip, grudges and backbiting a female way of handling conflict. The Bible names those sins for everyone: a whisperer separates chief friends, and the counsel to be swift to hear and slow to wrath is given to every believer (James 1:19). Second, Isaiah 3 does not make a woman’s leadership itself the curse. The judgment begins with Yahweh taking away the mighty man, the judge, the prophet and the prudent (Isaiah 3:1-2), and the verse Darby read ends by blaming those who lead the people astray. The same Bible honors Deborah, who judged Israel and called herself a mother in Israel (Judges 4:4; 5:7). Third, in passing he said Jehoshaphat was killed going to war with Ahab; the record says Jehoshaphat cried out and was spared, and it was Ahab who died (1 Kings 22:32, 37). Fourth, the protection of women from abuse in the workplace is not a plot against men; the Word tells men to treat younger women as sisters, with all purity (1 Timothy 5:2), and to honor their wives (1 Peter 3:7). And tears are not weakness: Jesus wept at Lazarus’ tomb, and David wept until he had no power left and then encouraged himself in the LORD. What Darby was really after is the small spirit that cannot take correction, and on that he is right.

There is grace in this story too. When Elijah confronted Ahab, Ahab tore his clothes, fasted and went softly, and Yahweh said, "Seest thou how Ahab humbleth himself before me?" (1 Kings 21:29) No man is beyond turning. The ministry’s own statement of faith says that individuals must choose to keep the flesh in subjection to Jesus Christ through the power of the Holy Spirit.

For us now, the message is a mirror for men and a mercy for families. Men, take up what you have set down: your word, your work, your home, your family. Settle things face to face. Pastors, raise men instead of dependents. And everyone, honor one another, because the aim was never to lower women but to raise men worthy of their trust.

TALK ABOUT IT TOGETHER. Parents, ask your children what it means to be strong the way Yahweh wants, and why Jesus says to talk to a person instead of about them, and listen to the end before you teach. Children, ask your parents or a grandparent what they remember. Friends, talk it over and think of one person you need to talk to face to face this week, and pray for that talk together.

## 12. Pastor Darby — The New Christian Feminist: sisters over everything, or God over everything

*id: `pv-darby-sisters-over-everything` · 1724 words*

**The big idea.** Preaching to his own congregation, Pastor Darby warns that many women are getting saved but keeping the world’s motto, sisters over everything: a bond of shared pain with girlfriends and with a women’s ministry that grows stronger than the bond of the gospel and the covenant of marriage. He does not oppose women preaching, but he says a women’s ministry that never teaches what Titus 2 commands, to love husbands, love children and be discreet, ends up pulling wives out of their homes. His cure is order and stability: Yahweh first, then your spouse, then your children; cleave to your mate; stay planted; and let godly friendship replace the old sisterhood.

Pastor Stephen Darby preached The New Christian Feminist: Sisters Over Everything to his own congregation at Destined Ministries in Louisville, Kentucky, and the internet program Next Chapter Radio broadcast it on 31 March 2016. Unlike his sit-down teachings, this one is a live sermon; on the recording his people answer him, and he presses them, can we talk or not talk? The title borrows a slogan from the culture of the time, sisters over everything, a play on a popular saying that put friends above relationships. Darby took it as a summary of a worldview that, he believed, had walked into the church through the front door.

He told his congregation how the message came to him, and the setting explains much of what follows. He said he does not usually build a sermon from an idea; he sees something and it speaks to him. He and his wife had spent an evening listening to different ministries on YouTube and on a Christian television network, and no matter who was preaching, he kept hearing the same few themes, your season, your breakthrough, your calling, preached to the same audience. He asked the Lord why the Word, which is so vast, was being narrowed this way, and he reached a conclusion that he stated plainly. In The New Christian Feminist, Sisters Over Everything (Next Chapter Radio, 31 March 2016, at 7:40) he says, "Women are getting saved, but they’re continuing the attitude of the world." A moment later, at 8:03, he explains what he means: for many believers the "sister bond is stronger than the gospel bond." A woman marries but her girlfriends still come first; she is saved but keeps the same worldly circle.

His argument unfolds in steps. The first concerns titles. He described looking into a minister who called herself an apostle prophetess and finding three small congregations meeting in hotel suites. He said people pile on titles like toppings on a pizza, chief apostle, overseer, bishop, as if the title confers authority, and he reminded them of the five ministry gifts Paul names and of the disciples who had no schooling. At 10:59 he says, "being with Jesus trumps seminary and school." The Word agrees: "Now when they saw the boldness of Peter and John, and perceived that they were unlearned and ignorant men, they marvelled; and they took knowledge of them, that they had been with Jesus." (Acts 4:13)

The second step concerns pain. A ministry that gathers people around their shared hurt will grow, he said, because everyone can relate, but relating is not the same as healing. At 18:36 he says that "in order to be healed you have to have a word that goes against your emotion." He compared it to a father who tells a hurting daughter, it is all right to grieve for a while, but now get up, because predators come to those who stay down.

The third step concerns balance. He believed a husband and wife are a counterweight to each other, one leaning toward feeling and the other toward rigid logic, and that a ministry led without that balance tips one way or the other. He tied it to false prophecy and to the spirit that Paul cast out of the slave girl in Philippi, a spirit that makes people think they are prophetic.

The fourth step answers the question he says people asked him first: does he believe in women preachers? His answer was Joel’s prophecy, which Peter preached at Pentecost: "And it shall come to pass afterward, that I will pour out my spirit upon all flesh; and your sons and your daughters shall prophesy, your old men shall dream dreams, your young men shall see visions:" (Joel 2:28) At 35:50 he says, "I don’t have no problem with nobody doing nothing in order." His concern was not whether a woman can speak for Yahweh but whether she is covered and in order, because, he said, out of order is not safe.

The fifth step is the heart of the sermon, Titus 2. He read that the aged women are to be holy in behavior, not false accusers, teachers of good things, "That they may teach the young women to be sober, to love their husbands, to love their children," (Titus 2:4) "To be discreet, chaste, keepers at home, good, obedient to their own husbands, that the word of God be not blasphemed." (Titus 2:5) Then he asked, at 37:38, "Why do you have to teach what should be obvious?" His answer was that in our society it is no longer obvious to love your husband or your children, and that he could hardly remember a women’s conference on either. He praised one woman preacher who taught women to raise their babies first, because ministry would still be there, and he called that balanced. He also spoke about discretion and modesty in the house of Yahweh, and about the old church mothers who quietly took a young woman aside and helped her dress and carry herself well. He mentioned with appreciation that his own wife makes many of her own clothes.

The sixth step is Esther 1. He read how Queen Vashti refused to come at the king’s command and how the king’s wise men feared that her refusal would teach every wife in the kingdom to despise her husband. He applied it to wives who leave their homes for endless ministry events, and he told a story he called heartbreaking: a man who worked two and three jobs, prayed with his family and pleaded for years for his wife to stay home and stop telling their business in the pastor’s office, who finally left and married again, while she was still alone ten years later.

The seventh step is his call to action, and it is the most practical part of the sermon. Cleave to your spouse, as Genesis commands: "Therefore shall a man leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave unto his wife: and they shall be one flesh." (Genesis 2:24) He and his wife, he said, lost friends early in their marriage because they decided to cleave; at 78:47 he says, "Because we decided we going to cleave like the Bible say." Keep your mother and your friends out of your marriage’s business; when you disagree, stay in the house and talk it out. Then he gave the order of a godly life at 87:44: "That’s the correct order God first then your spouse," and after that the children. He urged his people to stay planted where Yahweh puts them rather than running whenever a word stings: at 82:53, "stability is the key." He applied it to men too, at 83:22: "I’m not just talking to the women. You’re out of order too as a man." Men must submit to godly leadership if they want their wives to trust them, and wives must not mock a husband who is finally getting active in church. He told men to drop friends who pull them toward carnality and to find godly brothers who talk about prayer and date nights, and he put the whole sermon in one sentence at 85:26: "I need to understand God over everything." He closed by saying that if anyone left his ministry and never came back, he hoped they would say, at 91:11, that "they taught us about being married and being stable."

Now weigh it by the Word. Much of this stands on the text. Titus 2 is Scripture’s own curriculum for women teaching women, and its first lessons are love for husband and children. Genesis 2:24 sets the marriage covenant above every other human bond, which is why a married couple must leave as well as cleave. The counsel about friends is the Word’s too: "He that walketh with wise men shall be wise: but a companion of fools shall be destroyed." (Proverbs 13:20) And his warning that comfort without truth does not heal agrees with the Word, which binds up the brokenhearted with truth, not flattery.

The Word also fills in what Darby’s sermon passes quickly over. Titus 2 is itself a women’s ministry, so the question is never whether women should gather but what they teach. Women served Yahweh with honor in Scripture: Deborah judged Israel; Anna the prophetess served in the temple night and day; Philip had four daughters who prophesied; Phebe served the church at Cenchrea; and Priscilla with her husband Aquila took Apollos aside and expounded to him the way of God more perfectly (Acts 18:26). And the Word teaches the interdependence that Darby called balance in a fuller way than his generalizations about emotional women and logical men: "Nevertheless neither is the man without the woman, neither the woman without the man, in the Lord." (1 Corinthians 11:11)

On Vashti, the Word gives context that his application leaves out. The king summoned her on the seventh day of a feast, "when the heart of the king was merry with wine" (Esther 1:10), to show the people and the princes her beauty. Scripture does not hold up Ahasuerus or his counselors as models of godly headship. And the same book honors Esther, who went to the king uncalled, saying "and if I perish, I perish" (Esther 4:16), and saved her people. The Word’s pattern for a husband is the self-giving love of Christ (Ephesians 5:25), and for the whole household, mutual submission in reverence for Yahweh (Ephesians 5:21). Read that way, Darby’s real point survives: a home where everyone, husband first, puts Yahweh over everything is a home that stands.

What does it mean for us now? For women: let your sisterhood be the Titus 2 kind, the kind that sends you home stronger, loving your husband and your children. For men: be the kind of husband a wife can trust to lead, submitted to Yahweh and to godly counsel. For both: cleave, stay planted, talk it out at home, and put Yahweh over everything.

TALK ABOUT IT TOGETHER. Parents, ask your children what it means to put Yahweh over everything, even over our closest friends, and listen to the end before you teach. Children, ask your parents or a grandparent what they remember. Friends, talk it over and name one friend who helps you love Yahweh more, and thank that friend together this week.

## 13. Pastor Darby — Nurtured to Death: the boy who was comforted but never corrected

*id: `pv-darby-nurtured-to-death` · 1781 words*

**The big idea.** Pastor Darby argues that a generation of boys has been nurtured to death: comforted, defended and excused, but never corrected by a father, so they grow up strong in body and weak in spirit, unable to take a man’s correction or carry pressure. He traces it through Eden, through slavery’s assault on the Black family, through welfare rules that pushed fathers out, and through his own childhood among his mother’s boyfriends and the streets. His call is to bring fathers and godly men back into boys’ lives, to discipline and not only comfort, to stop teaching children that their fathers are no good, and to forgive.

Pastor Stephen Darby preached Nurtured to Death to his congregation at Destined Ministries in Louisville, Kentucky, and Next Chapter Radio broadcast it on 12 March 2016. The radio host introduced it as a message for mothers and sons. It came in a season when the country was arguing about policing and about Black lives, and Darby opened with an incident from that argument before turning to his real subject, which was what happens to a boy who is comforted but never corrected.

The incident was a video seen across the nation. On 26 October 2015 at Spring Valley High School in Columbia, South Carolina, a sheriff’s deputy working as a school resource officer threw a student who would not leave her math class out of her desk and across the room; the sheriff fired him two days later, saying he had not been trained to throw a student, and the record notes that the teacher and an administrator in the room had supported the deputy. Darby said he was offended twice: by what was done to the girl, and by those who said she deserved it because she had a smart mouth. Even if her mouth was smart, he said, she did not deserve that treatment. He compared it to enslaved girls whipped for being sassy and asked how far we have really come. And he asked why the adults in that room stood by, saying that a generation earlier a Black man would have come to her defense. He called that silence a kind of neutering, and it set up his thesis. In Nurtured to Death (Next Chapter Radio, 12 March 2016, at 7:24) he says, "Because there is a deficiency in us if we were not raised by masculinity." Boys who do not get a father’s spirit around them, he said, are like children who missed their vitamins.

His argument then moves in steps. The first is a distinction between two kinds of love. Nurture, he says, is what Yahweh put in women, and it is mostly comfort. At 12:48: "A nurturer does not necessarily grow you up because a nurturer is a comforter." At 13:18: "Now fathers do not nurture. They discipline." If a child gets only nurture, he says, he will be lopsided. He will look strong on the outside, he may even be hard on a woman, but when another man corrects him he goes soft, goes home and complains, or reaches for a fight because he has no other tools. Young men kill each other over small slights, he argues, not because they are hard but because they are soft inside and have never learned to carry anger, pressure or disappointment.

The second step is Eden. He reads Genesis 3 and stops on Yahweh’s words to Adam: "And unto Adam he said, Because thou hast hearkened unto the voice of thy wife, and hast eaten of the tree, of which I commanded thee, saying, Thou shalt not eat of it: cursed is the ground for thy sake; in sorrow shalt thou eat of it all the days of thy life;" (Genesis 3:17) Darby says the point is not merely that Adam ate, but that he hearkened to another voice over Yahweh’s. At 36:51 he says, "Real leaders don’t listen," and then at once qualifies it at 36:56: "I don’t say we don’t take counsel." A leader, he explains, goes to Yahweh first, and counsel confirms what Yahweh has said. He also gives the order he sees in Genesis 2: Yahweh gave Adam His Word, then work, then a place, then a wife, and he tells young women that the real test of a good man is that he has his own before he asks to move in.

The third step is history. He reminds his congregation that enslaved men and women were not allowed to marry and that children were sold off quickly, so that a man could not protect his woman or his children and had nothing to fight for. The record tells on itself here: enslaved people had no legal marriage in the United States, and through the domestic slave trade about one million were forcibly moved from the Upper South to the Deep South, sales that broke up many families. He argues that welfare policy later repeated the pattern by making a father’s presence cost a family its support, and the record shows at least one such rule: Alabama denied aid when a substitute father visited the home, until the Supreme Court struck the rule down unanimously in King v. Smith in 1968. He also named a document called NSSM 200 as a 1960s plan to suppress the Black family. The record shows something different: National Security Study Memorandum 200 was requested in 1974 and concerns population growth in thirteen developing countries abroad, so that particular claim goes beyond what the document says. He then said, at 58:14, "Not putting it all on the women," and insisted that the side of the story nobody tells is that the one who runs the home bears responsibility for what is learned there.

The fourth step is his own testimony, and it is the heart of the sermon. His first real drink came from his mother’s boyfriend, the same man who taught him to shoot dice. He remembers selling drugs as a young man on a street where his best friend’s mother sold herself, and he watched that friend grow into one of the most abusive men toward women he ever knew, because of the shame and anger he carried. He remembers crack houses where mothers did what they did in front of their children. At 49:39 he names the habit that hides all this: "We revised the past to make it look good once we get older." And he refuses to dishonor his mother; he says plainly that he loves her and that she is saved and delivered. ## 14. Pastor Darby — Queen of the Coast: spiritual warfare and absurdity in small doses

*id: `pv-darby-queen-of-the-coast` · 1729 words*

**The big idea.** In a spiritual-warfare sermon Pastor Darby teaches that the church is in a real war with the kingdom of darkness, and that one of its oldest weapons is seduction: drawing believers into sexual sin and getting them used to sin a little at a time, which he calls absurdity in small doses, until what was once shocking feels normal. Using the West African tradition of a water spirit called the Queen of the Coast and a deliverance account he read aloud, he warns that prayerless Christians are easy targets and praying, fasting Christians are the ones darkness fears. His call: pray, fast, keep the Word in your heart, stop entertaining what you want to be free of, guard your eyes and your marriage, and bless those who provoke you.

Pastor Stephen Darby preached Queen of the Coast to his congregation at Destined Ministries in Louisville, Kentucky, and Next Chapter Radio broadcast it on 6 March 2016. At almost two hours it is one of the longest messages in the series, and it is an unusual one. Darby told his people that it grew out of a year in which the church had done little but fast and pray, and that he believed doors were opening because of those stored-up prayers. He called it a spiritual-warfare message for those with some background in the subject, and he said the enemy’s warfare was thick and was aimed chiefly at the church.

The setting helps us understand him. Darby had preached in Nigeria and Uganda and had sat under deliverance ministries there. He believed many Black Americans live with spiritual realities they never name, because they grew up calling them by softer words. In Queen of the Coast (Next Chapter Radio, 6 March 2016, at 12:32) he says, "many of us have grown up in superstition," and he listed southern charms, voodoo and hoodoo among them. He began with a call to loyalty. He pointed to Daniel’s three friends, who told the king they would not serve his gods, and said true followers of Christ must be ready to take the same stand: "But if not, be it known unto thee, O king, that we will not serve thy gods, nor worship the golden image which thou hast set up." His argument then moves in steps. The first describes the enemy he has in mind. In West and Central Africa, he says, people speak of water spirits, called mermaid spirits or Mami Wata, and in Haiti, Lasirèn, and some keep shrines to them. He offered his own account of their origin, that they are hybrid creatures from before the flood whose spirits survived in the water. Here the record and the Word must both speak plainly. The human mind, he explains, will reject something absurd when it comes all at once, but will accept it in small amounts, like the frog in the pot that does not jump out while the water warms slowly. ## 15. Pastor Darby — Willie Lynch Syndrome: a chain on the mind, and the truth that breaks it

*id: `pv-darby-willie-lynch-syndrome` · 1953 words*

**The big idea.** Reading aloud the so-called Willie Lynch letter, Pastor Darby argues that Black Americans still live out a pattern of division planted in slavery: young against old, light against dark, women against men, every small difference made bigger, until a people distrusts itself, hates its own features, and reverses the roles of husband and wife. He calls it a chain on the mind, and his cure is instruction, identity, elders who impart, fathers who show up, marriages built on cooperation, and righteous living. The record shows the letter itself is a twentieth-century composition; the division he names, and the documented history behind it, are real, and the Word speaks to both.

Pastor Stephen Darby preached Willie Lynch Syndrome to his congregation at Destined Ministries in Louisville, Kentucky, and Next Chapter Radio broadcast it on 7 April 2016. At two hours it is the longest message in the series, and it carries the weight of a subject many Black Americans have lived with: the sense that something done in slavery is still being lived out in families, churches and neighborhoods. Darby had touched on it before, he said, but wanted to take it deeper. The radio host introduced it as a cultural problem with a biblical solution.

The document at the center of the sermon is the so-called Willie Lynch letter, which claims to be a speech delivered in 1712 on the bank of the James River in the Colony of Virginia by a slave owner from the West Indies, teaching Virginia planters how to control enslaved people by setting them against each other. Darby reminded his hearers that Louis Farrakhan had read from it at the Million Man March, and he read long sections aloud. He also knew it was called a myth and a hoax, and he answered that charge directly. In Willie Lynch Syndrome (Next Chapter Radio, 7 April 2016, at 7:37) he says, "In other words, we’re living the letter today. So if you say the letter didn’t exist, why are we living it?" His argument was that the fruit proves the root: whether or not the letter existed, the mindset does.

Before following his argument, the record must be stated plainly, because we teach the truth. The letter has been in print since at least 1970, often under the title The Making of a Slave, and spread widely in the 1990s. A reference librarian at the University of Missouri–St. Louis posted it online in 1993, having received it from the publisher of a local business directory, and she later became convinced it was a forgery. It contains words and phrases, such as "refueling" and "fool proof," that were not in use until the twentieth century, and the historian Roy Rosenzweig noted that its categories of skin color, age and gender are distinctly twentieth-century ways of dividing people. Historians, among them Rosenzweig and William Jelani Cobb, concluded it is a modern hoax. The term lynch law comes, by the better record, from Charles Lynch, a Virginia justice of the peace in the Revolutionary era. So the letter is not a record of 1712. But much of what Darby built on it does not need the letter at all.

His argument moves in steps. The first is a confession. At 8:10 he says, "I had a problem because I bought into the idea that my people was bad." He blames a church culture that, in his experience, never highlighted Black achievement, and schools that taught children nothing about themselves. At 9:36: "Anything that I’m involved in that doesn’t give me identity or purpose, then I’m not going to want to do it." He describes boys labeled early and pushed toward failure.

The second step is justice. If someone robs you of a hundred dollars and never makes restitution, he says, the passing of a hundred or three hundred years does not mean you were not robbed. He points out that the amendment that abolished slavery made an exception, and here the record agrees with him word for word: the Thirteenth Amendment forbids slavery and involuntary servitude "except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted." He argues that a conviction becomes a lifelong chain that takes away a young man’s vote and his future.

The third step is division, and it is the heart of the sermon. Reading the letter’s list of differences, he says the same strategy was used in his own lifetime through COINTELPRO, the FBI’s counterintelligence program, to divide Black leaders. Here the record tells on itself in the government’s own words. A March 1968 FBI memo set as its goals to prevent the coalition of militant Black groups and to prevent the rise of a leader who could unify them, and the Bureau worked to widen the rift between Malcolm X and Elijah Muhammad by fostering internal disputes. No forged letter is needed to prove that division was used as a weapon. At 32:23 Darby says, "Because this is how you have been conquered because of division." He names the divides one by one: light skin and dark skin, natural hair and relaxed, women and men, the educated and the uneducated, the ghetto and the suburbs. He warns against denouncing your own people in front of cameras that want a fight.

The fourth step is the generations. He says elders too often thought raising a child meant only food, clothes and a roof, and never purposely taught children how to navigate life. At 37:06: "Somebody’s got to purposely set out to give you knowledge." At 37:40, of his own children: "See, I refuse to just feed you and clothe you and not have no stake in your future."

The fifth step is self-image. He grieves over skin bleaching and hair shame and the money made from them, and he tells his people, at 27:50, "So when you look in the mirror, you begin to appreciate what the Most High has given you," and at 45:04, "What God gave you is beautiful."

The sixth step is the home. Reading the letter’s section on breaking the family, he argues that slavery and what followed reversed the roles of men and women, teaching women to depend only on themselves and men to stay back. His counsel to couples is warm and practical. A husband must lead, and when the bills are due he must come with a plan and feel his wife’s worry with her. At 97:35: "Submission is cooperation," a wife’s cooperation with a husband who actually leads. He urges fathers to go to their children’s schools, at 110:03: "Because when a father shows up at school it’s different."

The seventh step is his call to action. He closes with Proverbs: "He that refuseth instruction despiseth his own soul: but he that heareth reproof getteth understanding." (Proverbs 15:32) The pattern, he says, takes hold where people are living in sin, and righteous living and receiving instruction break it. At 115:47: "Willie Lynch is a chain on the mind." At 116:20: "But you cannot despise instruction." And he insists on his calling, at 73:44: "I’m not no motivation speaker. I’m a preacher."

Now weigh it by the Word. First, Darby’s answer to the hoax question, that we are living it so it must be true, does not stand. The Word does not let us carry a falsehood even for a good cause: "Wherefore putting away lying, speak every man truth with his neighbour: for we are members one of another." (Ephesians 4:25) The pattern being real does not make the letter real; it means the letter borrowed a real wound. And the real record is stronger than the forgery, because it is the oppressors’ own words: laws that refused enslaved people marriage, sales that drove about a million people south and broke families, an amendment with an exception, and FBI memos that set out to divide. The Word had already condemned the trade at its root: "And he that stealeth a man, and selleth him, or if he be found in his hand, he shall surely be put to death." (Exodus 21:16)

Second, on division Darby stands squarely with Jesus: "And Jesus knew their thoughts, and said unto them, Every kingdom divided against itself is brought to desolation; and every city or house divided against itself shall not stand:" (Matthew 12:25) And with the psalmist: "Behold, how good and how pleasant it is for brethren to dwell together in unity!" (Psalms 133:1) Dr. Chancellor Williams, whose lessons in this course study the fall of African states, reached the same conclusion from history: unity is strength and division is vulnerability, and he named disunity the principal internal factor. Paul gives the believer’s part: "Endeavouring to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace." (Ephesians 4:3)

Third, his teaching on identity. In this sermon Darby also taught that the descendants of the enslaved are the scattered tribe of Judah. The Word speaks to identity first in Christ: "And hath made of one blood all nations of men" (Acts 17:26), and "And if ye be Christ’s, then are ye Abraham’s seed, and heirs according to the promise." (Galatians 3:29) The true Jew, Paul says, is one inwardly (Romans 2:29), and heaven’s crowd is drawn from every nation, kindred, people and tongue (Revelation 7:9). Whatever any family’s lineage, the dignity Darby wanted his people to feel is already theirs as image-bearers and as heirs in Christ. The ministry’s own statement of faith says that man was created directly by and in the image of God. Fourth, he read Luke 22, where Jesus tells the disciples to take swords, as a call to armed self-defense. The Word’s own context is narrower: when they showed two swords Jesus said, "It is enough" (Luke 22:38), and when Peter used one, Jesus told him to put it away (Matthew 26:52).

Fifth, one claim must be named and set aside plainly, because truth and grace require it. In this sermon Darby also said that Jews owned the slave ships and run the entertainment industry. That claim is false, and it is an old myth. In 1995 the American Historical Association condemned any statement alleging that Jews played a disproportionate role in the Atlantic slave trade, and historians such as Wim Klooster and Seymour Drescher found that Jewish merchants were a small fraction of it and in no period led it. The Word forbids carrying such a charge: "Thou shalt not bear false witness against thy neighbour." (Exodus 20:16) Paul warned Gentile believers not to boast against the people through whom the root came: "Boast not against the branches" (Romans 11:18). And this sermon’s own lesson answers it best. Blaming a whole people for what some did is the same divide-and-rule poison Darby preached against. The cure is not a new scapegoat but the unity Christ makes: "For he is our peace, who hath made both one" (Ephesians 2:14), and "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither bond nor free, there is neither male nor female: for ye are all one in Christ Jesus." (Galatians 3:28)

And the grace in this story is Darby’s own. At 50:49 he says, "I don’t hate nobody. But I got to tell the truth about everybody." The Word shows the same posture in Joseph, who told the brothers who sold him, "ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good" (Genesis 50:20). The statement of faith says believers are to be instruments of Jesus Christ as the Holy Spirit ministers redemption and reconciliation in the world.

What does it mean for us now? Lay down the forged letter and pick up the true record. Teach it to the young, on purpose. Refuse every small division. Love the face Yahweh gave you. Let husbands lead and wives cooperate, and let fathers show up. And break the chain on the mind the way Darby said: by receiving instruction, by living righteously, and by the truth that makes us free (John 8:32).

TALK ABOUT IT TOGETHER. Parents, ask your children what small differences people let divide them, and how telling the truth without hate brings people together, and listen to the end before you teach. Children, ask your parents or a grandparent what they remember. Friends, talk it over and ask an older relative to tell a true story from your family’s history, and listen together.
