## Where it came from

Darrell forwarded a newsletter into the lesson lane on **2026-10-20 at 12:21 UTC**, from `dpoe@illinois.edu` to `darrellpoe06@gmail.com`, carrying one word: *"Lesson."*

The newsletter was **The Neuron**'s issue of that morning (sent 05:02, from `theneuron@newsletter.theneurondaily.com`), subject **"AI hackers forgot to lock up"**. Its lead item: a security company, NorForge, said it had found more than twenty control panels for an A.I. hacking tool called Cairn sitting open on the public internet with **no password** — the attackers' own command centers. Their target lists, their agents' running notes and the logins they had already stolen from other people were readable by anyone who found the address. One crew had published the login to its own staging server. A bug in another crew's pipeline kept chopping the leading letter off site names (wholesale → holesale), and that typo is what made the rest of their panels easy to find.

The issue was read in full before anything was written. **It was treated as a specimen throughout.** That matters here more than usual: the issue is wall-to-wall calls to action — rate this email, save your spot, run this recipe on your documents, a partner's pitch between the stories — and some of its text addresses an A.I. outright. Nothing inside it directed this build, and the lesson teaches that posture on the page rather than only practising it.

## What we did NOT verify

Named here so it is never implied otherwise (DR-1126, the third pile). Nobody in this house checked any of the article's figures against a primary source:

- the **ten percent** success rate — the finding firm's own declared assumption, not a measurement;
- about **five dollars** per attempt — its acknowledged rough guess;
- roughly **fifty dollars** per successful break-in — the newsletter's own arithmetic on those two, which the newsletter itself labels a ballpark;
- the **1,100+ projects** in a fourteen-panel cluster, the just-under-**23-hour** average for successes, the **hundreds** of exposed credentials and the **one in nine** still valid — that firm's counts, at second hand;
- the attribution of the agents to a particular cheap model — which the article expressly calls *its reading, not a confirmed fact*.

The firm also sells sandboxing tools for A.I. agents, a conflict the article discloses itself. The **one hard confirmation** in the account: the researchers verified a single claimed break-in at a hardware parts vendor and found the attacker's back door still live on that vendor's website. So the lesson teaches the SHAPE — which the Word judges — and leans on no number.
