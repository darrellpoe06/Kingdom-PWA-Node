## The Frame (carried forward from Batch 1)

The frame is unchanged from Batch 1 and is summarized here; see Batch 1 for the full statement.

- **The measuring rod is the King Himself** — Yahweh's Way, character, and the Godhead as revealed in the 66. The 66 are the rod *because they reveal Him*.
- **Rank does not authenticate the message** (Galatians 1:8 — "even if we, or an angel from heaven"). Not an apostle's rank, not a council's rank — Eastern or Western — not an ancient compiler's rank. The message goes to the standard. Applied evenly: a council's *omission* is not Yahweh's verdict, and a tradition's *inclusion* is not automatic canonization.
- **Presumptive weight:** a text that is ancient, that the inspired writers knew and used, and that bears Yahweh's revealed character, carries genuine presumptive weight — until a specific, demonstrated reason, measured against His character, moves it. Reception history is *evidence to weigh*, never a verdict on its own.
- **Yahweh's Way includes accommodation** — He meets people in their conditions (Matthew 19:8) — *and* accommodation in the 66 names itself as accommodation. A book reflecting human limitation is not thereby disqualified; a claim presenting itself as the non-accommodated ideal is tested *as* the ideal.
- **"Open" means** a genuine, weighty question with real substance on both sides — never a reflex. Weak objections are named as weak and demoted. A conceivable explanation with no evidential support *keeps a question open* but cannot *close* it — applied evenly in both directions.

**A note specific to Batch 2.** The deuterocanonical books are different in kind from 1 Enoch and Jubilees in one important respect: they are received as fully canonical Scripture by **the overwhelming majority of the world's Christians** — Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox (including the Ethiopian Tewahedo tradition), and in lectionary use across much of the Anglican Communion. They appear in the Septuagint — the Greek Scriptures the New Testament authors most often quoted. So for this batch, the presumptive weight is generally *higher* at the starting line than for the Ethiopian-unique books, and "the broad church received these" is a substantial and wide reception, not a narrow one. The Protestant 66-book canon's exclusion of them is, in the long view of church history, the minority position — which does not make the exclusion wrong, but does mean the frame's discipline (reception is evidence, not verdict; rank does not authenticate) must be applied here with particular evenness.

## The Structure of Each Entry

Same as Batch 1: (1) what the book is; (2) the presumptive weight; (3) the character test against the King as the 66 reveal Him; (4) claimed disqualifiers — which hold and which are demoted; (5) genuinely open questions with real substance; (6) what the evidence settles. Shorter books are treated more briefly, but each gets its own process.

# ENTRY 1: TOBIT

## What the Book Is

A "biblical novella" — a narrative of two faithful Israelite families in the Assyrian exile. Tobit, a righteous man of the northern tribe of Naphtali, is blinded; Sarah, a young woman, is afflicted by a demon (Asmodeus) that has killed seven successive bridegrooms. In answer to both families' prayers, Yahweh sends the angel Raphael, who guides Tobit's son Tobias to heal his father and free Sarah. **Setting:** 8th–7th century BCE Assyria and Media. **Likely composition:** roughly 3rd–2nd century BCE. **Language:** Aramaic and Hebrew fragments of Tobit were found at Qumran — confirming a Semitic original and pre-Christian Jewish origin.

## The Presumptive Weight

- Ancient and pre-Christian — confirmed by the Qumran Aramaic and Hebrew fragments.
- Received as canonical by the broad church — Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox — and quoted as authoritative by early Christian writers (Cyprian, Augustine).
- A faithful continuous reception across most of Christendom for the better part of two millennia.

## The Character Test

**Where it bears His character:** The book's heart — faithfulness in exile, the power of prayer, honoring parents, burying the dead at personal risk, almsgiving, the sovereignty of Yahweh to hear and send help — is consonant with the 66. Raphael's ministry parallels the angelic help seen throughout the 66.

**What the gathering must weigh:** Tobit contains specific historical and geographical details that critics argue do not align with known history (the book's handling of Assyrian chronology, distances). And the healing of Sarah involves the burning of a fish's heart and liver to drive off the demon — which critics describe as resembling sympathetic magic, against Yahweh's prohibitions of sorcery. Both belong in the character test, not as automatic disqualifiers, but as content to weigh: does the historical detail rise to the level of error that strains a claim of inspiration, or is it the freedom of a narrative genre? Does the fish-organ episode contradict Yahweh's character, or is it a narrative device within a story whose moral center is prayer and divine help?

## Claimed Disqualifiers — Which Hold, Which Demoted

- **Demoted:** "Not in the Hebrew canon." Reception history; not a demonstrated contradiction of Yahweh's character.
- **A real question, carried to open questions:** the historical/geographical difficulties, and the fish-organ healing episode.

## Genuinely Open Questions

- **The historical and geographical details.** *Why open:* the 66 reveal a God of truth; if a book presented as conveying His word contains demonstrable historical error, that bears on the character test. *Counter-substance:* Tobit is plainly a *narrative* of a didactic kind — the question is whether its genre makes precise historical accuracy beside the point, the way a parable's details are not "errors." *What would close it:* the gathering's determination of whether Tobit's genre is history-bearing or wisdom-narrative.
- **The fish-organ healing.** *Why open:* Yahweh forbids sorcery; an episode resembling it must be weighed against His character. *Counter-substance:* the text frames the means as given by Yahweh's own angel Raphael — which, if so, removes it from the category of sorcery entirely (sorcery being power sought *apart from* Yahweh). *What would close it:* the gathering's reading of whether the episode is sorcery or angelic provision.

## What the Evidence Settles

Settled: Tobit is an ancient, pre-Christian Jewish work (Qumran-confirmed); it is canonical in most of Christendom; its moral and devotional center is consonant with the 66; it contains historical/geographical details that are disputed.

# ENTRY 2: JUDITH

## What the Book Is

A narrative of deliverance: when an Assyrian army under the general Holofernes besieges Israel, a devout widow, Judith, uses her courage, faith, and cunning to enter the enemy camp and behead Holofernes, breaking the siege. **Likely composition:** 2nd century BCE. **Language:** Greek is the surviving text; a Hebrew original is debated.

## The Presumptive Weight

- Ancient, pre-Christian Jewish origin.
- Canonical in the broad church (Catholic, Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox); cited by early Christian writers.
- A long, continuous reception across most of Christendom.

## The Character Test

**Where it bears His character:** Judith's reliance on Yahweh, her call to the people not to presume upon or "test" God yet to trust Him, the deliverance of the covenant people against overwhelming odds — these are deeply consonant with the 66's pattern (compare the deliverance narratives, the songs of Deborah and Miriam).

**What the gathering must weigh:** Judith contains pronounced historical and geographical difficulties — it opens by calling Nebuchadnezzar the king of *Assyria* reigning in *Nineveh*, which does not align with known history. And Judith's method involves deception (she lies to Holofernes) and an assassination. The gathering must weigh: are the historical "errors" so deliberate and front-loaded that the book is signaling it is a *parable-like* narrative, not a history? And does Judith's deception of an enemy in wartime sit within the 66's own portrayals (e.g., Rahab, the Hebrew midwives — deception of a murderous power, framed approvingly) or against them?

## Claimed Disqualifiers — Which Hold, Which Demoted

- **Demoted:** "Not in the Hebrew canon." Reception history.
- **A real question, carried to open questions:** the historical framing, and the ethics of Judith's deception and killing.

## Genuinely Open Questions

- **The historical framing.** *Why open:* same as Tobit — a God of truth, and a book with demonstrable historical dissonance. *Counter-substance:* the dissonance is so immediate and conspicuous (Nebuchadnezzar as king of Assyria) that many scholars read it as a deliberate genre signal — "this is a didactic tale, not a chronicle." *What would close it:* the gathering's determination of genre.
- **The deception and the killing.** *Why open:* the character test must ask whether Judith's means honor Yahweh. *Counter-substance:* the 66 themselves contain approved deception of murderous powers (Rahab in Joshua and James; the midwives in Exodus 1) and divinely-used deliverers who kill oppressors (Ehud, Jael — and Jael's killing of Sisera is celebrated in Judges 5). Judith may sit squarely within that established pattern rather than against it. *What would close it:* the gathering's reading against those 66 parallels.

## What the Evidence Settles

Settled: Judith is an ancient, pre-Christian Jewish work; canonical in most of Christendom; its faith-and-deliverance center is consonant with the 66; it contains conspicuous historical dissonance that scholars widely read as a deliberate genre marker.

# ENTRY 3: WISDOM OF SOLOMON

## What the Book Is

A wisdom book, written in the persona of Solomon, exhorting the pursuit of divine Wisdom, contrasting the righteous and the wicked, affirming the immortality of the righteous, and reviewing Yahweh's saving acts in Israel's history. **Likely composition:** 1st century BCE, probably in Alexandria. **Language:** Greek — and most scholars hold it was *composed* in Greek, not translated.

## The Presumptive Weight

- Pre-Christian Jewish origin.
- Canonical in the broad church; quoted very early — Clement of Alexandria treated it as Scripture.
- **A notable New Testament connection:** Hebrews 1:3 describes the Son as the *apaugasma* ("radiance") of God's glory — and *apaugasma* appears in Wisdom 7:26, describing Wisdom as the "radiance" of eternal light. This is a verbatim lexical point of contact. It does not, by itself, prove canonicity (see the demoted objections), but under the frame — where inspired-writer usage carries real weight — it is a genuine point of contact worth the gathering's attention. Some scholars also see contact between Wisdom's argument and Paul's in Romans 1.

## The Character Test

**Where it bears His character:** Wisdom's portrait of God — His justice, mercy, sovereignty over history, love of what He has made ("you love all things that exist," Wisdom 11:24), and the call to righteousness — is strikingly consonant with the 66. Its affirmation that the righteous are in God's hand is consonant with the hope the 66 carry.

**What the gathering must weigh:** The Solomonic persona — Wisdom is written *as* Solomon but was composed roughly 900 years after Solomon. This is the same pseudonymous-framing hinge identified in Batch 1, and the gathering should apply the principle it settles there consistently here. The question is whether the Solomonic voice is a recognized wisdom-literature convention or a literal authorship claim.

## Claimed Disqualifiers — Which Hold, Which Demoted

- **Demoted:** "Not in the Hebrew canon." Reception history.
- **Demoted:** "Composed in Greek, therefore not Scripture." Composition language is not a character-test criterion — the New Testament itself was composed in Greek. Noted, not decisive.
- **A real question, carried to open questions:** the Solomonic pseudonymous framing.

## Genuinely Open Questions

- **The Solomonic framing.** *Why open:* the pseudonymous-genre hinge from Batch 1 applies directly. *What would close it:* the gathering's decision on whether recognized pseudonymous convention bears on inspiration — decided once, applied consistently across all affected books.

## What the Evidence Settles

Settled: Wisdom is a pre-Christian Jewish work; canonical in most of Christendom; its portrait of God is strongly consonant with the 66; it has a verbatim lexical point of contact with Hebrews 1:3; it is written in a Solomonic persona roughly 900 years after Solomon.

# ENTRY 4: SIRACH (ECCLESIASTICUS)

## What the Book Is

A large wisdom book — practical instruction on the fear of the Lord, ethics, family, speech, friendship, work, and worship — closing with a "praise of the fathers," a review of Israel's great figures. **Author:** uniquely among these books, Sirach *names its author* — Yeshua ben Sira (Jesus son of Sirach) — and carries a preface by the author's grandson, who translated it from Hebrew into Greek. **Composition:** roughly 2nd century BCE. **Language:** Hebrew original — and substantial Hebrew portions have been recovered (the Cairo Geniza, Masada, Qumran).

## The Presumptive Weight

- Ancient, pre-Christian, Hebrew — and **not pseudonymous**: it names its real author. This sets Sirach apart from most of the books in Batches 1–2.
- Canonical in the broad church; one of the most frequently cited of these books by the early Fathers; echoed in the Didache.
- Continuous, wide reception.

## The Character Test

**Where it bears His character:** Sirach's content is overwhelmingly consonant with the 66 — it reads as a close companion to Proverbs. The fear of the Lord, wisdom, righteous living, honoring parents, care in speech — this is the 66's own wisdom tradition extended. Augustine grouped it among the "prophetic books because of the authority deservedly accredited to them."

**What the gathering must weigh:** Sirach's own preface. The grandson-translator, in his preface, asks the reader's indulgence for any imperfections in the translation. Some have argued this preface *implies the book does not claim to be inspired Scripture* — that an inspired book would not apologize for its rendering. The gathering must weigh whether a translator's humility about his *translation work* says anything at all about the *inspiration of the original* — these may be entirely separate questions.

## Claimed Disqualifiers — Which Hold, Which Demoted

- **Demoted:** "Not in the Hebrew canon." Reception history — and notably, Sirach was used and respected within Judaism even after it was not included in the rabbinic canon.
- **A real question, carried to open questions:** the preface and what it implies.

## Genuinely Open Questions

- **The grandson's preface.** *Why open:* if a book's own framing disclaims the status of Scripture, that is a content-level signal worth weighing. *Counter-substance:* the preface's humility is explicitly about the *translation*, not the original composition — a translator apologizing for translation difficulty says nothing necessarily about whether the Hebrew original was inspired. *What would close it:* the gathering's reading of whether the preface bears on the original's status at all.

## What the Evidence Settles

Settled: Sirach is an ancient, pre-Christian Hebrew work that names its real author (not pseudonymous); canonical in most of Christendom; its content is strongly consonant with the 66's own wisdom tradition; its grandson-translator's preface expresses humility about the translation.

# ENTRY 5: BARUCH

## What the Book Is

A short book presented as written by Baruch, the scribe of the prophet Jeremiah, during the Babylonian exile — containing a confession of sin, a wisdom poem, and words of consolation and hope for Jerusalem. **Likely composition:** disputed; commonly dated 2nd century BCE, possibly composite. **Language:** Greek surviving text; a Hebrew original for at least part is argued.

## The Presumptive Weight

- Pre-Christian Jewish origin.
- In several early canonical lists Baruch was treated as *part of Jeremiah* — Cyril of Jerusalem and Athanasius both list "Jeremiah, with Baruch, Lamentations, and the Letter" as one unit. So Baruch's early reception is closely tied to a protocanonical book.
- Canonical in the broad church.

## The Character Test

**Where it bears His character:** Baruch's content — corporate confession of sin, acknowledgment of the justice of Yahweh's judgment, the call to return, the hope of restoration — is thoroughly consonant with the 66, especially with Jeremiah, Lamentations, and the penitential prayers of Daniel 9 and Nezra/Nehemiah.

**What the gathering must weigh:** the attribution to Baruch the scribe, against a likely composition date well after him — the pseudonymous-framing hinge again. And the gathering should weigh whether Baruch's early treatment *as part of Jeremiah* strengthens its position (tied to a protocanonical book) or simply reflects manuscript convenience.

## Claimed Disqualifiers — Which Hold, Which Demoted

- **Demoted:** "Not in the Hebrew canon." Reception history.
- **A real question, carried to open questions:** the Baruch attribution.

## Genuinely Open Questions

- **The attribution to Baruch.** *Why open:* the pseudonymous-framing hinge. *What would close it:* the gathering's settled principle on pseudonymous framing, applied consistently.

## What the Evidence Settles

Settled: Baruch is a pre-Christian Jewish work; closely tied in early reception to the book of Jeremiah; canonical in most of Christendom; its penitential and hope-bearing content is strongly consonant with the 66; its attribution to Jeremiah's scribe is later than the scribe.

# ENTRY 6: THE LETTER OF JEREMIAH

## What the Book Is

A short polemic against idolatry, framed as a letter from the prophet Jeremiah to the exiles in Babylon, warning them at length not to fear or worship lifeless idols. **Likely composition:** disputed; possibly 4th–2nd century BCE. **Language:** debated — a Greek fragment was found at Qumran, and scholars dispute whether it was composed in Greek or translated from Hebrew.

## The Presumptive Weight

- Pre-Christian Jewish origin; a copy was present at Qumran.
- Transmitted in the broad church, often as an appendix to Baruch or to Jeremiah.

## The Character Test

**Where it bears His character:** the content is a sustained attack on idolatry — entirely consonant with the 66's relentless witness against idols (Isaiah 44, Jeremiah 10, Psalm 115). There is little in its content that strains against Yahweh's revealed character; its theme *is* one of the 66's central themes.

**What the gathering must weigh:** the Jeremiah attribution against a likely later date — the pseudonymous-framing hinge. And it is brief and repetitive; some weigh it as a homiletical expansion of Jeremiah 10 rather than an independent prophetic word.

## Claimed Disqualifiers — Which Hold, Which Demoted

- **Demoted:** "Not in the Hebrew canon." Reception history.
- **Demoted:** "Repetitive / merely expands Jeremiah 10." A judgment of literary quality or originality, not a demonstrated contradiction of Yahweh's character. Noted, not decisive.
- **A real question, carried to open questions:** the Jeremiah attribution.

## Genuinely Open Questions

- **The attribution to Jeremiah.** *Why open:* the pseudonymous-framing hinge. *What would close it:* the gathering's settled principle, applied consistently.

## What the Evidence Settles

Settled: the Letter of Jeremiah is a pre-Christian Jewish work (present at Qumran); its anti-idolatry content is strongly consonant with the 66; its attribution to Jeremiah is likely later than the prophet.

# ENTRY 7: 1 MACCABEES

## What the Book Is

A sober historical narrative of the Maccabean revolt — the Jewish struggle, beginning around 167 BCE, against the Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes and his suppression of the faith; the rededication of the Temple; and the leadership of Judas Maccabeus and his brothers. **Composition:** roughly late 2nd century BCE. **Language:** Hebrew original (now lost), surviving in Greek — the Greek itself shows clear signs of translation from Hebrew.

## The Presumptive Weight

- Ancient, pre-Christian, Hebrew in origin.
- Canonical in the broad church (Catholic, Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox).
- Widely regarded — even by many who do not hold it canonical — as a **generally reliable historical source** for the period, sober and restrained in tone.
- It provides the historical background of Hanukkah, the Feast of Dedication, which the 66 reference: John 10:22 places Yahshua at the Temple "at the Feast of Dedication."

## The Character Test

**Where it bears His character:** 1 Maccabees portrays zeal for Yahweh's covenant, courage under persecution, faithfulness unto death rather than apostasy, and the deliverance of the worshipping community. Its restraint — it does not embellish with the miraculous — is itself notable. The faithfulness it depicts is the faithfulness the 66 honor.

**What the gathering must weigh:** 1 Maccabees is, by genre, *history* — and presents itself as such, without prophetic claim. The gathering must weigh what canonical status means for a sober historical book: the 66 contain historical books (Kings, Chronicles), so "it is history" is not itself a strike — but the gathering should weigh whether 1 Maccabees carries the marks of Yahweh's authoritative word the way the 66's histories do, or whether it is faithful history without that quality. Notably, 1 Maccabees makes no claim of inspiration for itself.

## Claimed Disqualifiers — Which Hold, Which Demoted

- **Demoted:** "Not in the Hebrew canon." Reception history.
- **Demoted:** "It is just history / makes no inspiration claim." The 66 include historical books; genre alone is not a disqualifier. But the absence of a self-claim is *noted* and belongs in the gathering's weighing.
- No specific content contradiction of Yahweh's character is demonstrated against 1 Maccabees.

## Genuinely Open Questions

- **What canonicity means for a sober history with no self-claim of inspiration.** *Why open:* this is a genuine *criteria* question — the gathering must decide whether canonicity requires a prophetic quality beyond reliable, faithful history. *What would close it:* the gathering's settled criteria for what canonical status requires.

## What the Evidence Settles

Settled: 1 Maccabees is an ancient, pre-Christian, Hebrew-origin historical work; canonical in most of Christendom; widely regarded as historically reliable; consonant with the 66 in the faithfulness it depicts; it makes no self-claim of inspiration and provides the background of the Feast of Dedication referenced in John 10:22.

# ENTRY 8: 2 MACCABEES

## What the Book Is

Not a sequel to 1 Maccabees but a parallel account, covering a shorter span of the same era, drawn (the book says) as a condensation of a five-volume work by one Jason of Cyrene. More overtly theological and emotional than 1 Maccabees, it emphasizes martyrdom, the resurrection of the body, and divine intervention. **Composition:** roughly late 2nd to 1st century BCE. **Language:** Greek — composed in Greek.

## The Presumptive Weight

- Pre-Christian Jewish origin.
- Canonical in the broad church.
- **The strongest New Testament point of contact in this batch.** Hebrews 11:35 — *"Some were tortured, refusing to accept release, that they might rise again to a better life"* — has no plausible source other than 2 Maccabees 6–7, the account of the martyrs (Eleazar; the seven brothers and their mother) who refused release to obtain "a better resurrection." The verbal and conceptual match is close: the unique idea of refusing deliverance for the sake of a *better resurrection*, the specific tortures, the language of mocking and scourging. A significant body of scholarship argues this reference should carry the same weight as a direct quotation. Under the frame — where inspired-writer usage is the heaviest kind of evidence — this is the most weighty single fact in Batch 2.

## The Character Test

**Where it bears His character:** 2 Maccabees affirms truths the 66 affirm — the resurrection of the body (compare Daniel 12:2), Yahweh's judgment and mercy, the honor of those who die faithful rather than apostatize, creation out of nothing (2 Maccabees 7:28). Hebrews itself reaches for the Maccabean martyrs as exemplars of *faith* — placing them in the same "cloud of witnesses" as the heroes of the 66.

**What the gathering must weigh — the genuine content question:** 2 Maccabees 12:43–45 records Judas Maccabeus making atonement and prayer *for the dead*, and the text comments approvingly that it was "a holy and pious thought" to pray for the dead in expectation of the resurrection. This is the single most theologically consequential passage in the deuterocanon, because doctrine (prayer for the dead) has been built on it, and because the 66 do not teach prayer for the dead. The gathering must weigh this directly and carefully against the King's character and the Godhead as the 66 reveal them. This is not "the mainstream disagreed" — it is a specific content question, and it is weighty.

**Also to weigh:** the book's own preface, in which the epitomist describes his labor of condensing Jason of Cyrene's work and asks indulgence for any shortcomings — raising, as Sirach's preface does, the question of whether the book frames itself as Scripture.

## Claimed Disqualifiers — Which Hold, Which Demoted

- **Demoted:** "Not in the Hebrew canon." Reception history.
- **Demoted:** "Composed in Greek." Not a character-test criterion; the New Testament was composed in Greek.
- **A real question, carried to open questions:** the prayer-for-the-dead passage, and the self-framing of the preface.

## Genuinely Open Questions

- **2 Maccabees 12:43–45 — prayer for the dead.** *Why open — and this is the weightiest content question in Batch 2:* the 66 do not teach prayer for the dead; a book teaching it, presented as carrying authority, sits in real tension with the character test. *Counter-substance to weigh:* (a) the passage's underlying conviction — that the dead will rise — *is* consonant with the 66; the question is narrower, whether the *practice* drawn from that conviction strains against the 66's revealed pattern of how the living relate to the dead. (b) The gathering must distinguish "the 66 do not teach this" (true) from "the 66 forbid this" (a stronger claim that must be tested, not assumed) — silence and prohibition are different, and the frame requires that distinction be made honestly. *What would close it:* the gathering's discernment, against the King's character and the Godhead, of whether the practice in 12:43–45 is contrary to His revealed Way or an unprohibited extension of a hope the 66 share.
- **The preface and self-framing.** *Why open:* same question as Sirach — does the epitomist's humility about his condensation bear on the work's status? *What would close it:* the gathering's reading.

## What the Evidence Settles

Settled: 2 Maccabees is a pre-Christian Jewish work; canonical in most of Christendom; it has the strongest New Testament point of contact in this batch (Hebrews 11:35 and the martyrs of 2 Maccabees 6–7); it affirms bodily resurrection and creation from nothing, consonant with the 66; it contains, in 12:43–45, an approving account of prayer for the dead, which the 66 do not teach.

# ENTRY 9: THE ADDITIONS TO ESTHER

## What the Book Is

Six passages found in the Greek (Septuagint) text of Esther that are absent from the Hebrew. They are notable because the Hebrew Esther famously never mentions God explicitly; the Greek additions *supply* explicit references — prayers of Mordecai and Esther, dreams, an explicit attribution of events to God's providence. **Likely composition:** 2nd–1st century BCE. **Language:** Greek; the additions appear to have been composed to accompany the Greek translation.

## The Presumptive Weight

- Pre-Christian Jewish origin, attached to a protocanonical book.
- Transmitted in the broad church as part of Greek Esther.

## The Character Test

**Where it bears His character:** the additions make explicit what the Hebrew Esther leaves implicit — that Yahweh's providence is behind the deliverance. Their content (prayer, dependence on God, providence) is consonant with the 66.

**What the gathering must weigh:** the additions are demonstrably *later supplements* to an existing book, composed for the Greek version — not part of the original Hebrew Esther. This is a different situation from a freestanding book. The gathering must weigh whether later supplementation of a protocanonical book can carry canonical authority, or whether the supplements stand in a different category than the book they augment.

## Claimed Disqualifiers — Which Hold, Which Demoted

- **Demoted:** "Not in the Hebrew canon." Reception history — though here it is more pointed, because the additions were never in the Hebrew *of this specific book*.
- **A real question, carried to open questions:** the status of later supplements to a protocanonical book.

## Genuinely Open Questions

- **Can later supplements to a canonical book share its authority?** *Why open:* a genuine criteria question — the additions are by nature secondary to Esther. *What would close it:* the gathering's settled principle on supplementary material (which also bears on the Additions to Daniel below).

## What the Evidence Settles

Settled: the Additions to Esther are pre-Christian Jewish supplements composed for the Greek text of Esther; absent from the Hebrew; their content (providence, prayer) is consonant with the 66; they are by nature later supplements to a protocanonical book.

# ENTRY 10: THE ADDITIONS TO DANIEL

## What the Books Are

Three texts found in the Greek versions of Daniel, absent from the Hebrew-Aramaic Daniel:
- **The Prayer of Azariah and the Song of the Three** — set within the fiery furnace (between Daniel 3:23 and 3:24): Azariah's penitential prayer, and the three companions' hymn of praise after their deliverance.
- **Susanna** — a virtuous woman falsely accused of adultery by two lecherous elders; the young Daniel exposes the elders by cross-examining them separately. Often called one of the earliest detective stories.
- **Bel and the Dragon** — two narratives in which Daniel exposes the lifelessness of idols and the fraud of their priests.

**Likely composition:** 2nd–1st century BCE. **Language:** debated; a Hebrew or Aramaic original is argued for the additions on the strength of their Semitic style, though some features suggest Greek.

## The Presumptive Weight

- Pre-Christian Jewish origin, attached to a protocanonical book.
- Early Christian reception: Irenaeus cites Susanna; Susanna was popular in early Christian art (catacombs, sarcophagi); the Prayer of Azariah and the Song entered Christian liturgical use.
- Transmitted in the broad church as part of Greek Daniel.

## The Character Test

**Where it bears His character:** The Prayer of Azariah is a model penitential prayer, deeply consonant with the 66 (it closely resembles the prayer of Daniel 9). The Song of the Three is pure doxology — creation praising its Maker — consonant with Psalms. Susanna's center is Yahweh's vindication of the innocent and exposure of corrupt judges — consonant with the 66's justice. Bel and the Dragon's polemic against idols is consonant with the 66's anti-idolatry witness. The *moral and theological content* of all three sits comfortably with the King's character.

**What the gathering must weigh:** as with Esther's additions, these are demonstrably *later supplements* to an existing book. And one source observes that Susanna and Bel and the Dragon read as designed "to entertain" — narratively delightful, detective-story-like — more than to carry the weight the Aramaic Daniel carries. The gathering must weigh: the supplement question, and whether narrative genre ("entertaining" wisdom-tales) bears on canonical status.

## Claimed Disqualifiers — Which Hold, Which Demoted

- **Demoted:** "Not in the Hebrew-Aramaic Daniel." Reception history — though, as with Esther, pointed: these were never in the Semitic book.
- **Demoted:** "They read as entertainment." Literary genre is not a demonstrated contradiction of Yahweh's character — the 66 contain narrative artistry. Noted, not decisive.
- **A real question, carried to open questions:** the status of later supplements to a protocanonical book.

## Genuinely Open Questions

- **Can later supplements to a canonical book share its authority?** *Why open:* the same criteria question as the Additions to Esther. The content of these three passes the character test cleanly; the live question is their *category* — supplements, not freestanding revelation. *What would close it:* the gathering's settled principle on supplementary material.

## What the Evidence Settles

Settled: the Additions to Daniel are pre-Christian Jewish supplements to Greek Daniel, absent from the Semitic text; their moral and theological content is consonant with the 66; they had early and warm Christian reception; they are by nature later supplements to a protocanonical book.

# ENTRY 11: THE PRAYER OF MANASSEH

## What the Book Is

A short penitential prayer — fifteen verses — placed in the mouth of Manasseh, the wicked king of Judah, expressing his repentance during his Babylonian captivity. Its occasion is 2 Chronicles 33:11–13, 18–19, which *reports* that the captive Manasseh humbled himself, prayed, and was restored — and even refers to "his prayer" being recorded in other annals. The Prayer of Manasseh supplies the words that 2 Chronicles refers to but does not give. **Likely composition:** uncertain; commonly 2nd century BCE–1st century CE. **Language:** debated; surviving in Greek.

## The Presumptive Weight

- Pre-Christian or early-era Jewish origin.
- It fills a gap explicitly opened by a protocanonical book — 2 Chronicles refers to Manasseh's prayer; this supplies it.
- In the Ethiopian Tewahedo tradition the Prayer of Manasseh is incorporated into 2 Chronicles. It has had wide devotional and liturgical use across Christendom.

## The Character Test

**Where it bears His character — and this one is striking:** the Prayer of Manasseh is one of the most concentrated expressions of repentance and Yahweh's mercy in all of this literature. Its theology is thoroughly consonant with the 66: God's readiness to forgive even the worst sinner who genuinely repents, the contrition of a broken and humble heart (compare Psalm 51), the magnitude of divine mercy. It contains no doctrine in tension with the 66. Its center *is* the King's character — His mercy.

**What the gathering must weigh:** the attribution-to-Manasseh question (the pseudonymous-framing hinge — though here the book supplies a prayer that a protocanonical book says Manasseh actually prayed, which is a somewhat different situation than a freestanding pseudonymous work). And the supplement question — it is, by nature, filling a gap in 2 Chronicles.

## Claimed Disqualifiers — Which Hold, Which Demoted

- **Demoted:** "Not in the Hebrew canon." Reception history.
- **A real question, carried to open questions:** the attribution, and the supplement status — though both are softened by the fact that 2 Chronicles itself points to the existence of such a prayer.

## Genuinely Open Questions

- **The attribution and supplement status.** *Why open:* the pseudonymous-framing hinge and the supplement question both apply. *Counter-substance:* both are unusually soft here — 2 Chronicles 33 explicitly refers to Manasseh's prayer and its being recorded, so a text supplying that prayer is filling a gap the 66 themselves open, not inventing a frame from nothing. *What would close it:* the gathering's settled principles on pseudonymous framing and supplementary material — applied here noting how much the 2 Chronicles reference softens both questions.

## What the Evidence Settles

Settled: the Prayer of Manasseh is a Jewish penitential prayer supplying the prayer 2 Chronicles 33 refers to; its theology of repentance and divine mercy is thoroughly consonant with the 66, with no doctrine in tension; it is by nature a supplement, and 2 Chronicles itself points to such a prayer's existence.

## Cross-Cutting Observations for the Gathering

1. **The deuterocanon's presumptive weight starts higher than the Ethiopian-unique books'.** These books were received as canonical Scripture by the overwhelming majority of Christians across most of church history, appear in the Septuagint the apostles most often quoted, and several have genuine New Testament points of contact. The Protestant exclusion is, historically, the minority position. The frame's discipline — reception is evidence not verdict, rank does not authenticate — must therefore be applied here with particular evenness, *in both directions*: the broad reception is weighty evidence, but it is still not, by itself, a verdict.

2. **The two genuine content questions in Batch 2 are 2 Maccabees 12 (prayer for the dead) and, more mildly, the Tobit fish-organ episode.** Almost every other "concern" in this batch resolves to one of two *criteria* questions, not content contradictions. The prayer-for-the-dead passage is the weightiest single item — it is a real content tension with the 66, and the gathering must weigh it carefully, including the honest distinction between "the 66 do not teach this" and "the 66 forbid this."

3. **Two criteria questions govern most of this batch, and the gathering should settle them explicitly:**
   - **The pseudonymous-framing hinge** (carried from Batch 1) — governs Wisdom, Baruch, the Letter of Jeremiah, and partly the Prayer of Manasseh.
   - **The supplement question** — *can later supplements to a protocanonical book share its canonical authority?* — governs the Additions to Esther, the Additions to Daniel, and partly the Prayer of Manasseh. This question did not arise in Batch 1 and is new here.

4. **Sirach stands somewhat apart** — it names its real author, is not pseudonymous, has a recovered Hebrew original, and reads as a direct companion to Proverbs. Of all the books in Batch 2, Sirach carries the fewest open questions.

5. **The historical-difficulty objections (Tobit, Judith) may resolve at the level of genre.** The conspicuousness of Judith's historical dissonance in particular leads many scholars to read it as a deliberate genre signal. The gathering's determination of genre — history-bearing versus didactic narrative — would resolve most of these at once.

6. **The character test does real work in this batch — and mostly clears ground.** Run honestly, the character test finds the *content* of most of these books strongly consonant with the 66: Sirach with Proverbs, Baruch and the Prayer of Azariah with the penitential tradition, Wisdom's portrait of God, the Letter of Jeremiah's anti-idolatry, the Prayer of Manasseh's mercy. The genuine content tension is concentrated in one passage (2 Maccabees 12). This is the opposite shape from a reception-history approach, which would treat all seven-plus books as uniformly "disputed." The character test distinguishes: most of this content bears the King's fingerprints; the gathering's hardest *content* work is one passage, and its main *criteria* work is two principles.

7. **Criteria before verdicts.** As in Batch 1: the gathering settles its criteria explicitly — the rod is Yahweh's Way, character, and the Godhead as the 66 reveal them; rank never authenticates; the pseudonymous-framing principle; the supplement principle; and the question of whether canonicity requires a quality beyond faithful history (1 Maccabees) — and then applies them book by book.
