# DR-0821 — Owning the light instead of renting it: count the cost, carry the risk honestly, and follow His Wisdom first (L221)

**Date:** 2026-10-08
**Status:** accepted
**Area:** Living Lessons (L221), biblical economics and stewardship of a real household decision
**Principle:** Spoken Teachings Are Build Input (Layer 0), DR-0611 (spoken and sent lessons), DR-0076 (verify every verse; measure, do not claim; proven-to-catch), DR-0100 (state established fact plainly, flag the open narrowly), DR-0098 (teach the Word, do not debate it), DR-0530 (the Resources), DR-0733 (every lesson sends you to someone), DR-0331 (render his words for meaning, never his voice)

## Context

Darrell forwarded a utility-industry newsletter into the app by email on
2026-10-08 with one word on top, Lesson, and his own teaching under it
(rendered for meaning, DR-0331):

> Solar panel costs, and switching to owning them rather than renting from a
> utility, then paying that off in twenty years or less with two percent
> interest, will save money against an ever-increasing electrical energy cost,
> because the grid cannot keep up with the demand and the projected future
> demand. Also using electric vehicles, where the energy to move a car four
> hundred miles comes from one charge. The difference between the two is night
> and day. However, there is risk in all things, and following His Wisdom is
> most important.

Read where he put the last clause. The arithmetic came first in the sentence
and His Wisdom came last, which in plain speech is where the governing clause
sits. So the lesson works the arithmetic honestly and then lets that clause
rule it, in his order.

Two things made this lesson different from the ones built earlier the same day.
First, the Word has a great deal to say about this subject and most of it is
never read aloud in a financing conversation: Jesus' own command to count the
cost (Luke 14:28-30); what borrowing does to a man, in the word Scripture
itself chose (Proverbs 22:7); what Scripture says about interest, including
the parts about a brother (Exodus 22:25; Deuteronomy 23:19-20) and Nehemiah
ending the practice rather than reforming it (Nehemiah 5:10-11); the diligent
against the hasty (Proverbs 21:5; Proverbs 28:20); the risk teaching, which is
neither pretending risk away nor refusing to sow (Ecclesiastes 11:2,4,5,6;
James 4:13-15); and the Resources in their order, Wisdom building, Understanding
establishing, Knowledge filling (Proverbs 4:7; Proverbs 24:3-4) under
Psalms 127:1.

Second, his teaching carries real-world money numbers, and DR-0100 forbids both
failures available here: floating a figure as a bare claim, and hedging
documented fact into nobody-knows. So every number in the lesson was researched
and is attributed in the prose, and the genuinely contested part is named
narrowly rather than smeared over the whole topic.

## What was measured

**His claims, checked.**

- **Installed cost.** Residential solar in 2026 is reported at about $2.60 per
  watt on the EnergySage marketplace, where shoppers collect competing bids,
  and about $3.36 per watt as the national residential average from Wood
  Mackenzie and the Solar Energy Industries Association.
- **Payback.** Commonly seven to fifteen years, governed almost entirely by the
  household's own rate: on the order of five years at 29 cents a kilowatt-hour
  and fourteen to sixteen at nine cents. The thirty-percent federal residential
  credit expired at the end of 2025, so a system installed in 2026 does not
  receive it, and that single change moves payback further than hardware prices
  do. Several current guides still quote the old credit, so the lesson sends the
  reader to a tax professional rather than to a sales sheet.
- **His two percent, and the thing most likely to cost this family money
  quietly.** The arithmetic at two percent is sound. What is sold at two percent
  is the question. Lenders publish tiers pairing a sub-three-percent APR with a
  dealer fee of roughly $1.50 to $3.00 per watt added to the loan principal,
  while a near-six-percent APR carries little or none; typical dealer fees are
  estimated at fifteen to thirty percent of system price. On a $26,000 cash
  price, a twenty-five-percent fee makes the financed balance $32,500 and
  interest runs on the larger number for the whole term. The low rate is bought,
  and it is bought with the buyer's principal. The lesson works that arithmetic
  on the page and gives the two questions that dismantle it.
- **His point about the grid.** Direction correct, stated plainly. The Energy
  Information Administration's 2026 forecast has the average residential price
  rising from 17.30 cents a kilowatt-hour in 2025 to 18.29 cents in 2026 and
  attributes the rise to grid and capacity costs even as wholesale prices are
  expected to fall; utilities requested $18.6 billion in rate increases in the
  first six months of 2026, already more than half of 2025's record year; data
  centers used roughly 4.4 to 4.7 percent of national electricity in 2023,
  having tripled over a decade, with projections of 345 to 490 terawatt-hours by
  2028 to 2030. **The open part, named narrowly (DR-0100 tier 2):** the
  reliability council revised its summer 2026 peak projection downward, citing
  how slowly large new loads actually come online, while still expecting peak
  demand some eleven gigawatts above 2025; and the share of a household bill
  attributable specifically to data centers is contested, with one analysis
  finding about a five-percent effect on total electricity prices and roughly
  3.6 percent for residential customers in the counties studied.
- **Night and day, and where it disappears.** Home charging runs roughly four to
  six cents a mile against twelve to sixteen cents for gasoline at 27 to 30
  miles a gallon, so his gap is real. Two conditions erase it, and the lesson
  puts both in the same breath: public fast charging at 35 to 48 cents a
  kilowatt-hour can equal or exceed gasoline per mile, so home charging is the
  entire advantage.
- **Four hundred miles on one charge.** Real, but at the top of the market
  rather than the middle of it. The Lucid Air Grand Touring is EPA-rated 512
  miles on 19-inch wheels and 446 on 21-inch, the Chevrolet Silverado EV Max
  Range 493, the Cadillac Escalade IQ up to 465 by the manufacturer, while most
  2026 models sit between 290 and 361 miles, and below freezing a 300-mile
  rating can deliver 180 to 225. Stated so nobody plans a trip on the top of the
  market.

**Proven-to-catch (DR-0076 §3).** Five real defects, each caught by a gate
rather than by reading the lesson over:

1. The strict corpus check rejected **eighteen** quoted spans. Four verses in
   this set carry a curly apostrophe in the corpus and I had typed a straight
   one: Psalms 24:1 (`LORD’s`), Luke 12:15, Proverbs 16:9 and Proverbs 19:21
   (`man’s`). The comparison normalizes whitespace only and never apostrophes,
   which is exactly why it caught them.
2. One quiz explanation quoted Proverbs 22:7 as "The borrower is servant to the
   lender" with a capital T. The verse reads "and the borrower is servant to the
   lender", so the capital made it not-verbatim. Corrected to the clause as
   written.
3. `title-in-narrative` reported the child and youth bands as not naming their
   own lesson: both opened on the short title and the full name carries a
   second half. Both now open on the whole title.
4. `hasAllThreeEverywhere` was false for the lesson and three bands, all on the
   same word: the parents-to-children prompt read "Parents, count the cost ...
   with your children", and "count" is not one of the verbs the detector knows.
   Rewritten as "Parents, sit down with your children and count the cost ...",
   which is also the better sentence.
5. The band-weight gate failed child at 0.312 and youth at 0.517. Both were
   raised with teaching the bands genuinely lacked, not filler: for children, a
   worked rent-against-own arithmetic ($200 a month is $2,400 a year and
   $24,000 over ten, and you own nothing at the end), the five questions in a
   child's words, and the electric-car cents-per-mile comparison; for youth, the
   same arithmetic in its plainest form, the twenty-year-commitment-on-a-house-
   you-may-move-out-of question, Ecclesiastes 11:6, Proverbs 11:14 quoted rather
   than merely listed, and the steward's standard from 1 Corinthians 4:2.

## Impact

L221 joins Living Lessons as the 218th module, dated 2026-10-08 beside L219 and
L220 from the same intake run. It gives the family and the church a decision
they are actually facing, worked in the Word's own order:

- whose light it is, before any price appears;
- rent and purchase are different shapes, and a monthly-against-monthly
  comparison is built to hide which one you are in;
- count the cost, by His own command, and the specific failure He named;
- what borrowing does, in the word Scripture chose;
- what Scripture says about interest, read aloud rather than skipped;
- the measured numbers, attributed, including the credit that expired;
- the dealer fee under a two-percent rate, with the arithmetic on the page;
- the grid, stated plainly with the contested part named narrowly;
- night and day, and the two conditions that erase it;
- risk is in all things, and the Word's answer is to spread it and sow anyway;
- His Wisdom first, and the order that builds: Wisdom, Understanding, Knowledge;
- the heart check, the steward's standard, and five questions short enough to run
  at a kitchen table before anyone signs.

## Decision

Ship L221 with its full four-band build, a ten-question quiz, twelve benefits,
twelve facilitator talking points, its date row, and a verse-pin test that also
gates the real-world numbers. Every figure stays attributed in the prose; the
contested share of a household bill stays named narrowly rather than used to
dismiss the whole trend; and his own framing, including "night and day" and
"there is risk in all things", is carried rather than paraphrased away.

`re-review: 2026-04-08` — six months out, re-check the five figures most likely
to move (cost per watt, the federal credit's status, the EIA residential price
forecast, typical dealer-fee tiers, and the longest EPA ranges) and update the
lesson's numbers, because a lesson that teaches stale numbers as current is the
DR-0076 failure in a different coat.
